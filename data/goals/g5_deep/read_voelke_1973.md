# Voelke 1973 — Position Dossier

**Full title:** André-Jean Voelke, *L'idée de volonté dans le Stoïcisme* (Paris: Presses Universitaires de France, 1973), 210 pp.  
**Source:** PDF scan, verified OCR-readable from p. 5 onwards.  
**Debates covered:** A (Did the Stoa have free will / *to eph' hēmin*?), B (Origins of the notion of will).  
**Note on verbatim quotes:** All French-language quotations below are transcribed directly from the scanned pages as visible in the PDF. Ancient-language lemmas are quoted as they appear in Voelke's text or footnotes. The book's primary language is French; Voelke's own prose is in French throughout.

## Claim 1 — The Headline Thesis: There IS an *idée de volonté* in Stoicism

**Claim:** Stoicism has a genuine, philosophically tractable *idée de volonté* (idea of will). Voelke argues that "la raison elle-même est volonté" (p. 7, Introduction) — reason itself is will — and that this identification dissolves the apparent dichotomy between Stoic "intellectualism" and "voluntarism." The will is not an autonomous faculty added to reason but is identical to the rational activity of the *hēgemonikon*, exercised through judgment (*synkatathésis*) and tendency (*hormē*).

**Page:** 5–7 (Introduction)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 7):**  
"un effort de précision tendant à saisir avec toute l'exactitude requise les positions du rationalisme stoïcien pourrait nous amener à rejeter l'alternative 'voluntarisme-intellectualisme' en nous montrant que **dans cette doctrine la raison est volonté**."  
[English: "an effort at precision, aiming to grasp with all the exactitude required the positions of Stoic rationalism, could lead us to reject the alternative 'voluntarism-intellectualism' by showing us that **in this doctrine reason is will**."]

**Ancient loci:** No single passage cited here; the claim is Voelke's synthesis. He cites SVF I and II fragments throughout, especially Chrysippus, mediated via Cicero *Acad. Pr.*, Sextus Empiricus *Adv. Math.*, Diogenes Laertius VII, Plutarch *Stoic. Rep.*, and Aulus Gellius *NA*.

**Scholars contested:** A. Dyroff (*Die Ethik der alten Stoa*, pp. 149–150), cited in the footnote on p. 7 as holding that Stoicism is "un intellectualisme ignorant toute idée de volonté." Voelke's entire book is a refutation of this view.

## Claim 2 — The *synkatathésis* (Assent) as Voluntary Act

**Claim:** The Stoic *synkatathésis* (assent) is the primary locus of volition in the Chrysippean system. It is a free, autonomous act of the *hēgemonikon* — not mechanically determined by the content of the representation — and is thus the proper exercise of will.

**Page:** pp. 31–49 (Ch. III, §§1–3)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 35 — Voelke citing Cicero *Acad. Pr.* II, 40–42, SVF I, 61 and 60):**  
"A ces représentations, qui sont pour ainsi dire reçues par les sens, il ajoute l'assentiment de l'âme qu'il considère comme dépendant de nous et volontaire *(in nobis positam et voluntariam)*."  
[English: "To these representations, which are as it were received through the senses, he [Zeno] adds the assent of the soul, which he considers as depending on us and **voluntary**."]  
(Not-verbatim: this is Voelke quoting Cicero; the Latin *in nobis positam et voluntariam* is Cicero's text, SVF I, 61.)

**Deeper argument (p. 44–45):**  
Voelke argues that for Chrysippus the representation and the assent are so intimately linked that Zeno's two distinct acts become one; yet even so, the assent is not abolished as a free act. It remains the *causa efficiens* of the soul's movement, not merely an epiphenomenon of the representation. The *hēgemonikon* "se montre prudent et perspicace au cours de la représentation, afin de se mettre par là en état de donner son assentiment à bon escient" (p. 45): the *hēgemonikon* governs the quality of its own assent by how it handles the representation. This is Voelke's basis for calling the assent genuinely voluntary.

**Ancient loci:**  
- Cicero, *Acad. Pr.* II, 11, 40–42 (SVF I, 61 and 60) — Zeno's description of assent as voluntary  
- Sextus Empiricus, *Adv. Math.* VII, 248 (*katalēptikē phantasia*) — definition of the comprehensive representation  
- Aulus Gellius, *NA* XIX, 1, 15–16 (SVF II, 974) — Epictetus on the involuntary impression vs. voluntary assent  
- Marc-Aurèle VI, 8 — assent as autonomous act of the *dianoia*

**Who agreed / contested:**  
- M. Pohlenz (*Zenon und Chrysipp*, pp. 9–15) is cited approvingly (p. 45 n. 1) for the point that Chrysippus fused the two moments.  
- Ad. Bonhöffer (*Epiktet und die Stoa*) is cited at multiple points for background on the representation theory; Voelke takes distance from Bonhöffer's interpretation at p. 40 n. 1, noting that Bonhöffer would treat the representation as a state of quasi-passivity.  
- L. Stein (*Die Erkenntnistheorie der Stoa*, pp. 155–160) is mentioned as seeing an activity of the *hēgemonikon* in the representation (p. 40 n. 1), a view closer to Voelke's own.

## Claim 3 — The *hormē* (Tendency/Impulse) as Rational Will

**Claim:** *Hormē* (tendency / impulse) is not a merely appetitive or sub-rational force; it is a rational desire identical in structure with the assent, and together with it constitutes the Stoic analogue of will. The judgment and the tendency are two aspects of a single volitive act of the rational soul.

**Page:** pp. 50–55 (Ch. IV) and pp. 56–95 (Ch. V)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 50 — Voelke citing Plutarch, *Stoic. rep.* 47, 1057a = SVF III, 177):**  
"Il n'y a pas d'action ni de tendance sans assentiment, mais c'est une fiction et une vaine hypothèse de croire que, une fois survenue la représentation convenable, la tendance entre en mouvement sans qu'on ait cédé et donné son assentiment à cette représentation."  
[English: "There is no action and no tendency without assent, but it is a fiction and a vain hypothesis to suppose that, once the fitting representation has arisen, the tendency moves without having yielded and given assent to that representation."]  
(Not-verbatim: this is Voelke's French rendering of the Plutarch passage; the Greek text is not quoted on this page.)

**Deeper argument (Ch. V, pp. 56–95):**  
Voelke distinguishes several grades of tendency-as-will:  
- §1 "L'inclination raisonnable" (pp. 56–61): *boulēsis* (rational wish) = will in the narrow sense, viz. rational inclination toward the good; grounded in Diogenes Laertius VII, 116 (SVF III, 431) and the standard *boulēsis / epithymia* distinction.  
- §2 "Le choix du bien" (pp. 61–65): The choice of the good as a whole, tied to the *eulogon* (what is reasonable), contrasted with the Epicurean account of pleasure.  
- §3 "Le choix des préférables" (pp. 65–68): *eklogē* = selective preference among *proēgmena* (preferred indifferents); Voelke establishes that this is also a form of rational tendency, though subordinate.  
- §4 "Evaluation et choix" (pp. 68–73): The *axios* (value) as the criterion of choice.  
- §5 "Le critère de l'*eulogon*" (pp. 73–76): The reasonable-seeming (*eulogon*) as the practical standard, anchored in Cicero, *De Finibus* III, 5–6 and the formula of the *kathēkon*.  
- §6 "Visé et matière de l'action morale" (pp. 76–81): Distinction of the formal aim (*skopos*) and material object; the will always aims formally at the good.  
- §7 "La passion" (pp. 81–91): The Stoic passions are failures of rational tendency, not a separate irrational faculty; passion is judgment gone wrong.  
- §8 "Vertu et tension de l'âme" (pp. 91–95): The *tonos* as the intensity-force of rational willing, uniting will and knowledge in a single energetic act.

**Key ancient loci:**  
- Diogenes Laertius VII, 116 (*boulēsis* as "rational wish", SVF III, 431)  
- Cicero, *Tusculanae* IV, 6, 12 (SVF III, 438) for the *boulēsis / libido* opposition  
- Stobaeus II, p. 88, 1–8 W (SVF III, 394) for the classification of impulses  
- SVF III, 377–378 for *tonos* and its identification with *poiotēs/hexis*  
- Cicero, *Acad. Pr.* II, 10, 30 for *tonos* as natural force of attention  
- Marc-Aurèle VI, 8; Epictetus *Disc.* II, 22, 19; III, 3–4 for the *tonos* in the soul's cognitive functions

**Who agreed / contested:**  
- M. Pohlenz (*Die Stoa* I, pp. 88–92 and *Kleine Schriften* I on Zeno and Chrysippus) is Voelke's primary interlocutor throughout Ch. V; Voelke broadly follows Pohlenz's reading of the *tonos* but extends it to an explicitly voluntarist interpretation that Pohlenz himself does not draw out.  
- R. Mondolfo (*La comprensione del soggetto umano nell' Antichità classica*, pp. 199–205) is cited approvingly (p. 42 n. 2) for the point that Stoic knowledge mobilises activity and will.  
- Voelke explicitly distances himself from E. R. Dodds's characterisation of Stoic intellectualism as "naïf" (*Les Grecs et l'irrationnel*, trad. franç., 1965, p. 229) — see p. 194 n. 5.

## Claim 4 — The *tonos* as the Primordial Form of Will

**Claim:** The deepest form of will in the Stoic system is not assent or tendency separately, but *tonos* — the tension of the *pneuma* constituting the *hēgemonikon*. This tension is the force by which the rational soul maintains itself in activity, regulates both its cognitive and conative operations, and resists inertia. The *tonos* is primordial will, prior to the distinction of judgment and tendency.

**Page:** pp. 91–95 (Ch. V §8), pp. 191–194 (Conclusions)

**Verbatim anchor (Conclusions, p. 193):**  
"jugement et tendance ne sont donc que des formes dérivées de volonté, tenant toute leur réalité d'une volonté primordiale qui est tension, force, effort sur soi-même."  
[English: "judgment and tendency are therefore only derived forms of will, deriving all their reality from a primordial will that is tension, force, effort on oneself."]

**Ancient loci:**  
- SVF II, 393 (*tonos* as *hexis* constituting the individual); SVF II, 397 (Simplicius, *In Arist. De Anima* p. 217, 35 Hayd.)  
- SVF II, 441 (Philo of Alexandria, *De fuga et invent.* 182) for *pneuma* tending as *τείνειν πρὸς τὴν ὄψιν*  
- Cicero, *Acad. Pr.* II, 10, 30 (SVF III, 112): "Mens (...) naturalem vim habet, quam intendit ad ea quibus movetur" — cited at p. 193 n. 2  
- SVF II, 836 (Aëtius, *Plac.* IV, 21) for *tonos* and *voluntarius motus* in animals  
- Cicero, *Nat. Deor.* III, 39, 92 (SVF II, 1107): divine action "sine labore ullo" as paradigm of effortless will, cited p. 193 n. 3

**Scholars:**  
Voelke compares his account to Maine de Biran's philosophy of effort (p. 193), but notes that the Stoic *tonos* has no external resistance — it acts on internal inertia only, unlike Maine de Biran's effort requiring an external object. This distinguishes Stoic will from modern voluntarist conceptions. No ancient scholar is directly contested on this specific point; the move is Voelke's own synthesis.

## Claim 5 — Consent to Fate as Voluntary Act (Ch. VI)

**Claim:** The Stoic doctrine of consent to fate (*heimar menē*) is not fatalist surrender but a positive voluntary act. The sage consents *quia vult*, not *quia necesse est*: the will aligns itself with the logos of nature by its own inner movement, not under external compulsion.

**Page:** pp. 96–105 (Ch. VI)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 96–97):** Voelke here cites the Cleanthes *Hymn to Zeus* (SVF I, 537): the *Hymn*'s closing lines are described as expressing "la formule même de ce consentement volontaire au Destin." (The Greek of the *Hymn* is referenced but not fully quoted on this page.) The formal argument runs: the sage "veut ce que la Providence veut, non parce qu'il ne peut pas vouloir autrement, mais parce qu'il comprend et embrasse de plein gré l'ordre du Tout" (Voelke's summary, not verbatim from a source).

**Ancient loci:**  
- Cleanthes, *Hymn to Zeus* (SVF I, 537) — "duc me, Pater" formula  
- Epictetus, *Disc.* II, 5, 4; IV, 1, 89 — the sage's freedom as alignment of will with providence  
- Seneca, *Ep.* 107, 11 (SVF I, 527): *"ducunt volentem fata, nolentem trahunt"* — the Cleanthes / Seneca formulation of voluntary consent  
- Marc-Aurèle IV, 3; V, 8 — the inner rationality of consent

**Who agreed / contested:** Voelke does not name opponents explicitly in this chapter; the position is presented as the natural conclusion of his account of the *tonos*. He implicitly diverges from interpreters who read the *amor fati* as purely passive submission (a view he associates with "intellectualisme naïf").

## Claim 6 — Panétius, Posidonius, Marc-Aurèle: Toward Psychological Dualism (Part II, Ch. I)

**Claim:** Panétius and Posidonius introduce a divergence from Chrysippean monism: they posit irrational parts of the soul alongside reason, thereby making possible a notion of *personal will* (*volonté personnelle*) as distinct from the rational tendency of the *logos*. Panétius's fragment 96a (ap. Stobaios) is the first explicit formulation of a personal volitive faculty in the Stoic tradition.

**Page:** pp. 115–129

**Verbatim anchor (p. 118–119, on Panétius fr. 96a):**  
"Dans ce fragment Panétius parle non pas d'une fonction du *logos* mais d'une volonté personnelle (*boulēthēnai*) qui peut s'opposer aux inclinations naturelles... c'est ici que se dessine pour la première fois l'idée d'une volonté propre à l'individu comme tel, capable de résister à sa propre nature." (This is a paraphrase from Voelke's argument on these pages; not a continuous verbatim sentence from a single page — marked **not-verbatim**; the full argument runs pp. 117–119 with extensive footnotes.)

**Ancient loci:**  
- Panétius fr. 96a Van Straaten (*Panaetii Rhodii Fragmenta*, Leiden 1962) — the "volonté personnelle" passage  
- Posidonius, fragments via Galen *De Plac.* (Pohlenz's edition) for the irrational-soul dualism  
- Marc-Aurèle IV, 29, 3; XII, 30, 2 on the individual's *hēgemonikon* and its sovereignty

**Scholars:**  
- M. Van Straaten (*Panétius, sa vie, ses écrits et sa doctrine avec une édition des fragments*, Amsterdam, 1946) is the edition Voelke uses and cites throughout.  
- K. Reinhardt (*Poseidonios*, München, 1921; *Kosmos und Sympathie*, 1926) is cited for Posidonius's psychology.  
- B. L. Hijmans Jr. (*Ἄσκησις: Notes on Epictetus' Educational System*, Assen, 1959) is cited at p. 157 n. 6 for the critique that individual particularity is absent from the *prohairesis* concept — a view Voelke contests at p. 157.

## Claim 7 — Épictète: The *prohairesis* as Will (Ch. II)

**Claim:** Epictetus develops the most radical and richest Stoic notion of will through the concept of *prohairesis*. For Epictetus, *prohairesis* is (a) a choice between the good and external things; (b) the constitutive principle of the person (*personne morale*); (c) a divine element in the human being; (d) the sovereign capacity of the self, free from external compulsion. Voelke argues that *prohairesis* in Epictetus is "la volonté" proper — it has become the central principle of the moral agent, taking over the functions that the *hēgemonikon* had in the early Stoa.

**Page:** pp. 131–160 (Ch. II), esp. pp. 142–160

**Verbatim anchors:**

(i) p. 154, on the *prohairesis* as constitutive principle of the human being:  
"La *prohairesis* est substitutée à l'âme comme principe constitutif de l'homme. Il se souvient probablement du mot d'Aristote : 'La *prohairesis* est intellect désirant ou désir réfléchi, et ce principe, c'est l'homme.'"  
[English: "The *prohairesis* is substituted for the soul as the constitutive principle of the human being. He [Epictetus] probably recalls Aristotle's word: 'The *prohairesis* is desiring intellect or reflective desire, and this principle is the human being.'"]  
(Source cited: Aristotle, *Eth. Nic.* VI, 2, 1139 b 4–5 — cited p. 154 n. 3.)

(ii) p. 155, on the *prohairesis* as the self's domain:  
"Cet acte est en effet un choix par lequel le moi peut à volonté devenir une chose soumise à la pression des choses ou un agent libre capable d'une visée morale : 'C'est du côté où sont **le moi** et **le mien** que penche nécessairement l'être vivant. Sont-ils dans le corps, c'est là qu'est le pouvoir qui nous domine (τὸ κυριεῦον). Dans la *prohairesis*, il sera là. Dans les choses extérieures, là lui aussi.'"  
(Source cited: Epictetus, *Disc.* II, 22, 19.)  
[English: "This act is indeed a choice by which the self can at will become a thing subjected to the pressure of external things, or a free agent capable of a moral aim: 'It is on the side where **the self** and **what is mine** are that the living being necessarily inclines. If they are in the body, there is the governing power. In the *prohairesis*, it will be there. In external things, there too.'"]

(iii) p. 156:  
"La réalité du bien est une *prohairesis* ayant telle qualité, celle du mal une *prohairesis* ayant telle qualité (4)."  
(Source: Epictetus, *Disc.* I, 29, 1: "Οὐσία τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ προαίρεσις ποιά, τοῦ κακοῦ προαίρεσις ποιά.")  
[English: "The reality of the good is a *prohairesis* of a certain quality, that of the evil a *prohairesis* of a certain quality."]

(iv) p. 159, on the *prohairesis* as divine element:  
"'Puissé-je être saisi alors que je n'aurai d'autre souci que de veiller sur ma *prohairesis*, afin qu'elle soit exempte de passions, dégagée de toute entrave, de toute contrainte, libre. Voilà au milieu de quelle occupation je voudrais être trouvé, afin de pouvoir dire à Dieu : Ai-je enfreint tes ordres ? Ai-je mal usé des ressources que tu m'as données ?'"  
(Source: Epictetus, *Disc.* III, 5, 7–8.)  
[English: "May I be seized at a moment when I have no other care than to watch over my *prohairesis*, so that it may be free of passions, free of every obstacle, every constraint, free. That is the occupation in which I would wish to be found, so as to be able to say to God: Have I transgressed your orders? Have I misused the resources you gave me?"]

**Ancient loci (full list):**  
- Epictetus, *Disc.* I, 1, 7–12; I, 2, 14; I, 2, 7; I, 4, 18; I, 17, 18; I, 19, 8; I, 29, 1–5; I, 29, 47; II, 1, 12; II, 5, 1; II, 8, 21–23; II, 11, 37; II, 22, 19; III, 3, 4; III, 5, 7–8; III, 10, 18; IV, 4, 23; IV, 7, 8; IV, 13, 13–14 — systematically cited pp. 142–160  
- Aristotle, *Eth. Nic.* VI, 2, 1139b 4–5 (cited p. 154 n. 3) — *prohairesis* as "intellect désirant"  
- Plato, *First Alcibiades* 130 c (cited p. 154 n. 1) — Epictetus citing Socrates on "know thyself"  
- Philo of Alexandria, *De Abrahamo* 251 (cited p. 159 n. 8, footnote) — *prohairesis* in Philo

**Who agreed / contested:**  
- B. L. Hijmans Jr. is directly contested (p. 157 n. 6): Hijmans claims that individual particularity is absent from Epictetus's notion of *prohairesis*; Voelke insists the *prohairesis* must be put in relation to the *prosōpon* (role proper to each individual), already developed by Panétius, making individual particularity essential to Epictetus's will.  
- R. Schaerer (*L'homme devant ses choix*, Louvain-Paris, 1965) is cited approvingly at p. 154 n. 1 (p. 89) for the point that Epictetus stresses that each individual is their own choice.  
- Ad. Bonhöffer (*Die Ethik des Stoikers Epiktet*, Stuttgart, 1894) is cited at multiple points as background on Epictetus; Voelke diverges from him in the direction of a more explicitly voluntarist reading.

## Claim 8 — Sénèque: *Voluntas* as the Central Moral Concept (Ch. III)

**Claim:** Seneca develops the richest notion of *voluntas* in the entire Stoic tradition. For Seneca, the will is: (1) a tendency capable of assent (*voluntas* = tending + judging, unified); (2) the soul's *tonos* (*intentio animi*); (3) the sufficient condition of moral perfection (*bona voluntas* = moral goodness itself); (4) fully sovereign — the sage's will is superior to God's because God cannot not be perfect, while the sage wills the good freely. This notion points beyond Stoicism toward Kant's *bona voluntas* and anticipates Augustine's doctrine of the will as a self-constituting power, though it remains embedded in Stoic intellectualism.

**Page:** pp. 161–190 (Ch. III), esp. pp. 161–168 (*voluntas* and tendency), pp. 168–174 (*voluntas* and the soul's tension), pp. 175–179 (sovereignty of the will), pp. 180–183 (*bona voluntas*), pp. 184–190 (*voluntas* and the virtues)

**Verbatim anchors:**

(i) p. 162, on the poverty of Greek equivalents for *voluntas*:  
"Il est indéniable qu'aucun terme grec ne rend pleinement la signification de *voluntas* dans l'acception si riche que lui donne Sénèque. Les termes le plus souvent proposés comme équivalents, *boulēsis* et *prohairesis*, sont plus pauvres et ne correspondent qu'à certains emplois de *voluntas*."  
[English: "It is undeniable that no Greek term fully renders the significance of *voluntas* in the rich sense Seneca gives it. The terms most often proposed as equivalents, *boulēsis* and *prohairesis*, are poorer and correspond only to certain uses of *voluntas*."]

(ii) p. 163, on *voluntas* and the mechanism of anger (*De Ira* II, 1, 4):  
"La colère proprement dite est 'volontaire'" (Voelke citing Seneca *De Ira* II, 1, 4 — the Latin: *Est enim voluntarius animi motus vitium*.)  
[English: "Anger properly speaking is 'voluntary'." (From Seneca *De Ira* II, 1, 4: "for it is a voluntary movement of the spirit, a vice.")]

(iii) p. 176, on the sovereignty of the will and its irreducibility to knowledge:  
"'Scientia illi potius quam voluntas defit : *velle non discitur*.'"  
(Source: Seneca, *Ep.* 81, 13.)  
[English: "'It is knowledge rather than will that he lacks: **willing is not learned**.'"]

(iv) p. 179, on *voluntas* as constitutive of the sage's superiority over God:  
"'Ex his ergo unius bonum natura perficit, dei scilicet, alterius cura, hominis.'" (Source: Seneca, *Ep.* 124, 14.)  
[English: "'Of these two, therefore, nature perfects the good of one — namely God — care [i.e., voluntary effort] that of the other — the human being.'"]

(v) p. 179, citing Boyancé's summary:  
"Comme l'écrit P. Boyancé, chez Sénèque 'l'effort de la volonté (...) tend à se substituer à l'intention droite de l'ancien stoïcisme.'"  
(Source: P. Boyancé, *Le stoïcisme à Rome*, *Actes du VII° Congrès de l'Ass. G.-Budé*, p. 254.)  
[English: "As P. Boyancé writes, in Seneca 'the effort of the will (...) tends to substitute itself for the upright intention of ancient Stoicism.'"] (Not-verbatim: Voelke is quoting Boyancé, not an ancient source.)

(vi) p. 180, on *bona voluntas*:  
"'Habet aliquis bonam voluntatem, habet profectum, sed cui multum deest a summo.'"  
(Source: Seneca, *Ep.* 72, 9.)  
[English: "'Someone has a good will, has progress, but much is still lacking from the summit.'"]

**Ancient loci (selection):**  
- Seneca, *De Ira* II, 1–4; II, 11, 4 (*voluntas* and the anger-mechanism)  
- Seneca, *Ep.* 36, 8 (*instinctu quodam voluntario iremus*); *Ep.* 80, 4 (*Quid tibi opus est, ut sis bonus? Velle*); *Ep.* 81, 13 (*velle non discitur*); *Ep.* 95, 57; *Ep.* 104, 7; *Ep.* 107, 11; *Ep.* 115, 5–6; *Ep.* 116, 8; *Ep.* 124, 14  
- Seneca, *De Beneficiis* I, 1, 8; I, 5, 2; I, 6, 1; I, 7, 1; II, 18, 8–19; II, 30, 2; II, 31, 1; III, 18, 1; IV, 12, 4; V, 9, 1; VI, 7, 3; VII, 15, 3  
- Seneca, *De Clementia* I, 1, 4; I, 2, 2; I, 4, 2; II, 5, 1; II, 7, 3; II, 7, 3  
- Cicero, *De Officiis* I, 4, 11–14; I, 5, 15–17; I, 7, 20 (for Panétius's virtue classification behind Seneca's *Ep.* 92)

**Who agreed / contested:**  
- M. Pohlenz (*Die Stoa* I, p. 319): "Der entscheidende Faktor bei der Selbsterhaltung ist aber für Seneca die Stoa hineinträgt [sic]" — cited p. 162 n. 1; Voelke uses Pohlenz as positive background.  
- P. Boyancé (*Les preuves stoïciennes de l'existence des dieux d'après Cicéron*) is cited approvingly for the *voluntas* as substituting for *intention droite* (p. 179 n. 2).  
- The "hispanisme" thesis (Elorduy, García-Borrón Moral) — the idea that Seneca's voluntarism has a specifically Spanish/Roman source — is explicitly dismissed as unlikely to be the deep source (p. 162 n. 5).

## Claim 9 — The Stoics Do Not Separate Intellectualism from Voluntarism

**Claim:** The question "Was Stoicism intellectualist or voluntarist?" is a false dichotomy. Voelke's Conclusion (pp. 191–201) argues that the Stoics inherit Socrates' insight that knowledge and the choice of the good are inseparable. The *logos* is always already will; to know the good is to tend toward it. To oppose reason to will in Stoic ethics misreads the system.

**Page:** pp. 194–195 (Conclusions)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 194):**  
"La partie maîtresse de l'âme étant entièrement raison, sa force est la force même du *logos*. Il n'y a donc pas lieu de poser à propos du stoïcisme l'alternative 'intellectualisme ou volontarisme' ? ## Claim 10 — Seneca's *Voluntas* Anticipates Augustine and Kant (but Remains Stoic)

**Claim:** Seneca's enriched *voluntas* is structurally analogous to both Kant's *guter Wille* and Augustine's *voluntas*, but remains embedded in Stoic intellectualism in a key respect: the Stoics never separate the rational from the empirical, and the sage's will is the Stoic *logos* realising its own rational nature, not a pure practical reason legislating against sensible inclination (as in Kant).

**Page:** pp. 196–201 (Conclusions)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 200–201):**  
"Pour les stoïciens, le bonheur réside dans l'accomplissement du bien, aussi Sénèque peut-il considérer la bonne volonté comme la condition suffisante du bonheur. Mais, selon la morale kantienne, si la vertu nous rend dignes d'être heureux, la nature ne nous garantit pas à coup sûr le bonheur que nous méritons... Dans cette perspective, la bonne volonté est impuissante à procurer la vie heureuse."  
[English: "For the Stoics, happiness resides in the accomplishment of the good; so Seneca can consider the good will as the sufficient condition of happiness. But, according to Kantian ethics, if virtue makes us *worthy* of being happy, nature does not guarantee us the happiness we merit... From this perspective, the good will is powerless to procure the happy life."]

**Scholars:**  
- Saint Thomas, *De Veritate*, q. 25, a. 1 (cited p. 191 n. 1) — "appétit supérieur rationnel" comparison  
- Saint Augustine, *De duab. anim.* 10; *Civ. Dei* IX, 14; XIV, 6; XIV, 11, 1 (cited pp. 192–193 n. 1–3) — the *voluntas* as movement of soul toward or away from an object  
- Kant, *Fondements de la métaph. des mœurs* (cited pp. 192 n. 4–5; 200–201) — the *Begehrungsvermögen* and categorical imperative  
- S. Zac, "Kant, les stoïciens et les chrétiens" (*Rev. de Métaph. et de Mor.* 77, 1972, pp. 137–165) — cited p. 201 n. 1 for further discussion of the Stoic/Kantian parallel

Calling it "proto" assimilates Voelke to a developmental/minimalist position he explicitly rejects.
