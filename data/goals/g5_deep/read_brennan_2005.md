## Headline Thesis

Brennan argues that Chrysippus is best characterized — using Brennan's own modern label — as a **compatibilist** (or "soft determinist"): the Stoics held universal causal determinism and simultaneously grounded moral responsibility in the agent's own causal role (autonomy), not in any ability to have done otherwise. The modern "free-will problem," framed around the CDO (Could-Have-Done-Otherwise) principle, is a **post-Stoic development** that arose from a "shrinking of the self" — a conceptual shift Brennan traces through the middle Stoics and especially Epictetus. The cylinder argument (Cic. *De Fato* 41–43; Gellius *NA* 7.2) is the key ancient locus throughout. A secondary but firm conclusion is that Chrysippus **fails** to answer the Lazy Argument, not because he is incoherent, but because he misunderstands the objection.

## Claim 3 — The cylinder argument establishes Stoic compatibilism: "up to us" means originating from one's own character, not the power to do otherwise

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 253–257 (ch. 15, "Necessity and Responsibility") |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 255–256, physically seen):** | Brennan frames two criteria: (AA) An agent is only responsible for an action if the agent **Acted Autonomously** — i.e., the action originated from the agent, not from some external cause. (CDO) An agent is only responsible for an action if the agent **Could Have Done Otherwise**. He argues Chrysippus operates exclusively under AA: "**What makes the agent responsible for their actions is that the actions stemmed from the agent's psychology, not from anywhere else… the agent could not have acted otherwise; but the action is still the *agent's* action, because it came from the *agent's* beliefs and desires.**" (p. 292, ch. 17 — the formulation is restated there more crisply.) |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 253–255, cylinder):** | The cylinder passage from Cicero *De Fato* 41–43 and Gellius *NA* 7.2 is the primary ancient locus. The cylinder rolls in a determinate direction once pushed; the *push* (external impression) is not "up to us," but the *manner of rolling* (the agent's character / assent-disposition) is. Brennan's reading: "up to us" (*eph' hēmin*) = the causal contribution of the agent's own nature, not a power to have rolled otherwise. |
| **Ancient locus** | Cic. *De Fato* 41–43; Gellius *NA* 7.2.1–15 (= LS 62C–D = *SVF* 2.974, 979); Plutarch *Stoic. Rep.* 1055f |
| **Brennan's own claim or report** | Brennan's own reconstruction, explicitly defended as the correct reading of Chrysippus |
| **Who he agrees with / contests** | Explicitly agrees with **Bobzien** (cited in n. 4 to ch. 15, pp. 264–269): references "Bobzien (1998a), 225–226" on non-futile actions and chance/probability. Brennan notes he is not entirely sure Bobzien's probabilistic defense of Chrysippus works, but he endorses her core framing of the AA/CDO distinction (p. 292: "I think Bobzien is right to draw our attention to this difference in conceptions of moral responsibility"). Does **not** contest Bobzien on the one-sided/causative reading of *eph' hēmin*. |

## Claim 6 — The Lazy Argument is a genuine and powerful objection; Chrysippus' co-fated-events reply is "a surprising and disappointing failure"

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 270–287 (ch. 16, "The Lazy Argument") |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 285, physically seen):** | "**I think the Lazy Argument is a very powerful one, and I think that Chrysippus' introduction of 'co-fated' events is a rather surprising and disappointing failure on his part. What disappoints is not so much that he did not solve all the problems, as that he seems to have crucially failed to understand the original complaint.**" |
| **Ancient locus** | Cic. *De Fato* 28–30 (= LS 55S = IG2 II–84); *De Fato* 30 (= *SVF* 2.955, 956 = LS 55S = IG2 II–84); Eusebius *Praep. Evang.* 6.8.265d (= *SVF* 2.998 = LS 62F = IG2 II–93) |
| **Brennan's own claim or report** | Brennan's own verdict — he is **not** reporting a scholarly consensus; this is an independent evaluative claim that goes against apologetic readings of Chrysippus. |
| **Argument reconstructed by Brennan (pp. 283–285):** | The Lazy Argument (explicit form): (1) We deliberate whether to call the doctor; (2) deliberating presupposes the matter is not yet fixed; (3) recovery/death is already fixed; (4) all necessary preliminaries to the fixed outcome are therefore fixed; (5) the doctor-call is not fixed (it's deliberated); (6) therefore the doctor-call is not a necessary preliminary to the outcome. The upshot: "there is no necessary role for [the doctor-call] in the unwinding of the events that will lead to the resolution of the patient's illness." Chrysippus replies that the call and the recovery are *co-fated*. Brennan's rebuttal (p. 284–285): co-fated events are **granted** by both sides as a premiss of the argument; pointing to them does not dissolve the argument, it merely restates the Stoic claim that everything is determined — which is exactly what the objector contests. "It is a piece of the grossest impertinence for the Stoics to think that they are discovering a blunder in the Lazy Argument when they point to the existence of co-fated events." |
| **Who he agrees with / contests** | Contests any apologetic reading of Chrysippus (e.g. implicitly Bobzien 1998a, 225–226, cited in n. 4, whose probabilistic defense of non-futility Brennan finds unclear and probably unsuccessful). Brennan says explicitly (n. 4, p. 286): "I'm not sure exactly how this defense works, because I am not sure how to understand the references to 'a chance' and 'a probability'." |

## Claim 7 — The ancient criterion for responsibility is AA (Acted Autonomously), not CDO (Could Have Done Otherwise); the CDO frame is a modern development explained by the "shrinking of the self"

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 288–294 (ch. 17, "The Evolution of the Will") |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 291, physically seen):** | "**What makes the agent responsible for their actions is that the actions stemmed from the agent's psychology, not from anywhere else… the agent could not have acted otherwise; but the action is still the *agent's* action, because it came from the *agent's* beliefs and desires. If something outside the agent's psychology had been the source of the action — a passing car, for instance — then the agent would not be responsible. But the mere fact that the agent could not do otherwise, given their actual psychological state, does nothing to extricate them from responsibility for the things that they actually did. Autonomy, not the ability to do otherwise, is the issue.**" |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 292, physically seen):** | "I think Bobzien is right to draw our attention to this difference in conceptions of moral responsibility, but I also think that a slight shift of emphasis can help us see how this historical difference arose." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 293–294, physically seen):** | "In antiquity, the agent was the whole thick ball of desires, beliefs, inclinations, tendencies, predispositions, and so on. The only thing external to him was the world at large — other people, animals, inanimate objects, and so on. **But when we exclude the desires from the agent's self, we begin to shrink the agent down to a geometrical point, that is, the 'ego' or 'will'. Genuine responsibility requires me to be completely free, autonomous, and unhindered in my choice — I must be capable, so far as everything external goes, of doing the opposite of what I do.**" |
| **Ancient locus** | No single ancient locus; Brennan is making a historiographical-developmental claim about the trajectory from early Stoicism through Epictetus to modernity. |
| **Brennan's own claim or report** | Brennan's own historical thesis about conceptual change. |
| **Who he agrees with / contests** | Explicitly endorses **Bobzien**'s AA/CDO distinction but proposes a different explanation of its historical origin (not a sudden "shift" to be located historically, but a continuous process of self-shrinkage running "right through the middle of the Stoics," p. 293). Brennan thereby partially parts from Bobzien's framing while keeping her result. Does **not** mention Frede 2011 (published later); his account of the Epictetan contribution is distinct from Frede's "first actual notion of a free will" — Brennan treats Epictetus as a moment *within* the shrinkage process, not as the origin of a genuinely new concept. |

## Claim 9 — The Stoic account faces two further objections it does not resolve: deliberation and Newcomb's Problem

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 280–285 (ch. 16) |
| **Summary (not verbatim, images not OCR'd for these pages):** | Brennan presents two distinct objections to Chrysippus's response to the Lazy Argument. (i) **Deliberation objection** (pp. 280–282): if everything is fated, Chrysippus can show that for any future event there is some prior co-fated condition, but I can never know which token action is the necessary one; I cannot deliberate rationally about which action to perform. "Newcomb's Problem tips the balance towards making me lazy." (ii) **Causal-closure objection** (pp. 282–285): if the sequence of necessary conditions runs all the way back to the determined present, then no action I perform now can genuinely change the outcome; the co-fated structure leaves no room for genuine deliberative openness. Chrysippus's reply amounts to rejecting premiss (2) — deliberation presupposes openness — but Brennan argues this is precisely the point at issue and the Stoics offer no positive account of how to deliberate in a closed world. |
| **Ancient locus** | Cic. *De Fato* 28–30; Aristotle *De Int.* 18b31–33 (cited n. 7, p. 286 = Ackrill trans.); Aristotle *De Int.* 19 18b32; *NE* III.3 (n. 9, p. 286) |
| **Brennan's own claim or report** | Brennan's own evaluative verdict; he is not merely reporting objections. |
| **Who he agrees with / contests** | Contests Bobzien's more favorable reading of Chrysippus's non-futility defense (n. 4, p. 286). |

## Quick-Pass Debate Map Accuracy Check

The debate map (`data/goals/g5/debate_map.md`, Debate A, Brennan row) reads:

> **Brennan 2005** | AGAINST / compatibilism | Chrysippus is a *compatibilist / "soft determinist"* (label applied explicitly); "up to us" = originating from one's own character; cylinder-and-cone. No libertarianism. | Cylinder-and-cone (Cic. *De Fato* 41–43; Gellius *NA* 7.2); Idle Argument | **255–257**

**Assessment:**

1. **"soft determinist" label explicitly applied** — CONFIRMED. Brennan does use the modern labels "soft determinism" and "compatibilism" as his own analytical characterizations.

2. **"up to us" = originating from one's own character** — CONFIRMED and substantially richer: Brennan grounds this in the AA/CDO distinction developed at length in ch. 17 (pp. 288–294), not merely asserted in chs. 14–15.

3. **Cylinder-and-cone** — CONFIRMED as central locus (Cic. *De Fato* 41–43; Gellius *NA* 7.2).

4. **"Idle Argument" listed alongside cylinder as key locus** — PARTIALLY MISLEADING. Brennan's chapter 16 on the Lazy Argument (= Idle Argument) is **not** used to ground the compatibilist reading; it is a separate chapter that concludes Chrysippus **fails** to answer that objection. The debate map conflates the cylinder locus (ch. 15, the positive account) with the Lazy Argument (ch. 16, where Brennan finds Chrysippus wanting). These are distinct claims, and the map's "Contested ancient locus" column runs them together as if they support the same conclusion.

5. **Page 255–257** — CONFIRMED as the core location of the compatibilism claim (ch. 15). But the AA/CDO argument that fully grounds the claim runs through pp. 288–294 (ch. 17), which the quick pass did not capture.

6. **OMISSION — Brennan's negative verdict on Chrysippus and the Lazy Argument** is entirely absent from the quick-pass map. This is a significant omission: Brennan simultaneously holds (a) Chrysippus is a coherent compatibilist AND (b) Chrysippus's specific reply to the Lazy Argument is "a surprising and disappointing failure." The map records only (a).

7. **OMISSION — Brennan's "shrinking of the self" / self-concept thesis** (ch. 17) is absent. This is his main original contribution beyond the compatibilism label.

**Verdict:** No outright misrepresentation in the map, but two significant omissions: the negative verdict on the Lazy Argument (p. 285), and the "shrinking of the self" developmental thesis (pp. 292–294). The "Idle Argument" in the locus column is misleading in implying it supports the compatibilist reading.
