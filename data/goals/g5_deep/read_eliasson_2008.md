## HEADLINE THESIS

Eliasson's book is a **terminological-historical** study, NOT a study of "freedom" or "free will." His governing claim is **deflationary and anti-conflationary**: the cluster of notions modern readers reflexively label "freedom / free will" (*to eph' hēmin*, *to autexousion*, *eleutheria*, *prohairesis*, *to hekousion*, fate, chance, providence) were **distinct issues in antiquity that only gradually and unevenly merged**, and treating them as one "free will problem" both repeats an ancient doxographers' error and imports a modern problem that "is far from clear—at least not to me—" is even present in the sources (p. 2). He therefore insists on translating *eph' hēmin* by a deliberately *non-specific* "depending on us" and reconstructing each author's notion in its own right. The positive payoff: a precise account of **Plotinus' own innovation** — restricting *to eph' hēmin* to the **entirely self-directed activity of the virtuous soul**, severing it from the praise-and-blame frame (pp. 219–222).

He is a Frede student (acknowledgements, p. xi: Michael Frede was his Oxford advisor) and **largely endorses Bobzien's reconstructions of Chrysippus and of the one-/two-sided distinction**, but he **breaks with Bobzien on Alexander** and refuses Bobzien's/Frede's "who-invented-the-will" framing as his own question.

## CLAIM 1 — Method/meta-thesis: "free will" is not a single ancient issue; conflation is the field's central error

|---|---|
| **Page** | 1–4, 14–16 (esp. p. 2) |
| **Verbatim anchor (PDF-verified, p. 2)** | EN: "some readers might still expect that the study would be either an account of Plotinus' theory of some general issue of 'freedom' or 'free will' … In fact, however, this study will do neither of these things. ## CLAIM 2 — The notions merge only *gradually*; the *eph' hēmin / autexousion* equation is a three-stage, NOT a sudden/Christian, event

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 6 (with nn. 30–32, 43–45), 7–9; Nemesius analysis 153–155; intro synonymy flag p. 9 n. 45 |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 7, accent-normalised from .md)** | EN: "the notion of ἐφ' ἡμῖν was also later on to become **closely associated with, explained in terms of, and finally taken to be synonymous with** the notion of τὸ αὐτεξούσιον." And (p. 9): "The Roman Stoic **Epictetus is probably the first one to see it as closely related to yet clearly distinct from** the notion of ἐφ' ἡμῖν. The Aristotelian **Alexander of Aphrodisias is … the first one to clearly state that τὸ αὐτεξούσιον is what the notion of τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν actually refers to**. Later on, these notions appear to have become more or less synonymous." |
| **Ancient loci** | Stage 1 = Epictetus *Diss.* II.2.3, IV.1.56 etc. (related-but-distinct); Stage 2 = Alexander *De fato* (equation; cf. Leroux's cite of *De fato* 182,24, p. 36); Stage 3 = **Nemesius, *De natura hominis* ch. 39, incl. the title and text at 311,7 ff. Morani** = full synonymy (p. 9 n. 45; p. 6 n. 32). On Nemesius ch. 35/EN-III taxonomy, pp. 153–155 (Morani 109.16–104.11), where Eliasson stresses Nemesius does **not** explicitly identify τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν with τὰ ἰσοτελῶς ἐνδεχόμενα, leaving the link "unclear" (p. 155). |
| **Provenance of *autexousion*** | Originally **legal/political** (status of released POWs; God's unchallengeable power), introduced ~1st c. BC, adopted by philosophers "perhaps by the Roman Stoics Musonius and his pupil Epictetus" (pp. 7–8). He **declines to adjudicate** the earliest-philosophical-use dispute, laying out the rival datings in a doxography (p. 8 n. 35): Gauthier-Jolif/Kahn/Bobzien → Epictetus; Bonhöffer/Harl → Musonius; Telfer → Chrysippus; Zierl → Zeno+Chrysippus; Rieth → absent in the Stoa. He notes the Hippolytus SVF II.975 attribution to early Stoics is "**questioned by Bobzien 1998a, 355 n. 74**" (pp. 8 n. 35, 15 n. 79) and treats it as a doxographer's back-projection. |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Continuist, anti-"Christian-invention."** Christian adoption (Methodius, Origen, Hippolytus — p. 9 n. 42) follows the *same pagan trajectory*; no discontinuous Christian coinage. This **aligns Eliasson with Bobzien** (continuous legal→philosophical→synonymous path, completed in Nemesius) and against any reading that makes *autexousion* a Christian theological novelty. He explicitly **brackets Christian authors out of scope** (Origen, Hippolytus): "fully possible to study the Pagan schools … without very much reference to positions defended by Christian philosophers … while the opposite may not [be] the case" (pp. 19–20). |

## CLAIM 3 — Chrysippus: Eliasson *endorses* Bobzien's reconstruction (one-sided, autonomy-based, *not* alternative-possibilities)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 84–87 (4.1.1), 95–97 (4.1.3 conclusions), 217–218 (ch. 7) |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 85)** | EN: "**In what follows I will agree with most of this.** Still, the textual basis … will demand that we slightly modify or specify (i) as well as (iv)." And p. 85: "it is important **not to assume that what is at stake is some idea of responsibility of an agent as demanding alternative possibilities of action**. In fact, there is no evidence that Chrysippus and his opponents … were arguing from any such intuitions, but rather … from some idea of responsibility as demanding the **autonomy of the agent as cause of his own actions**." |
| **Bobzien quoted approvingly (p. 85 n. 18, = Bobzien 1998a, 279)** | autonomy = MR1 (necessary+sufficient for moral responsibility), not MR2 (could-have-done-otherwise). Eliasson takes this over wholesale for the Hellenistic debate. |
| **His own modification** | Chrysippus' notion is **exclusive** (only rational, assent-issuing agents): "an action is ἐφ' ἡμῖν if and only if it occurred by the agent's own impulse" (impulse generic for assent+desire), and in Cicero/Alexander glossed as **acting from one's own nature** (φύσις) (pp. 96–97). He revises Bobzien's condition (iv) — "neither forced nor fully externally hindered" — judging it fits the *Roman* Stoa (Musonius, Epictetus) better than Chrysippus (p. 85, 95). |
| **Greek** | τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν; παρ' ἡμᾶς; συγκατάθεσις (assent); ὁρμή (impulse); ἐλευθερία (kept separate). |
| **Ancient loci** | Cic. *De fato* 39–45; Gellius *NA* VII.2; Plut. *Stoic. rep.* 1055f/1056d; Alexander *De fato* chs. 26/33/35/36/38 (SVF II.979–1006); Origen *De princ.* III / *De orat.* 6 (SVF II.988–990); Nemesius *NH* ch. 35; Clement *Strom.* II (SVF II.992) — collected p. 83 n. 10. |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Agrees with Bobzien 1998a** on the substance; **mild correction** on which condition belongs to Chrysippus vs. the Roman Stoa. Notes **Donini 1999, 738** (Stoics spoke "only in order to respond to … opponents") and warns against over-reading that into denying the Stoics held the view (p. 83 n. 12). Flags **Dobbin 1998, 66–67** as "somewhat confused by the idea that … ἐφ' ἡμῖν depicts the same thing as … ἐλευθερία" (p. 81 n. 1). |

## CLAIM 4 — Alexander of Aphrodisias: Eliasson *breaks with* Bobzien — Alexander only "associates" *eph' hēmin* with choice, does not predicate it of choice; and Bobzien misreads Aristotle

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 76–79 (3.4.1), 79–80 (3.5 conclusions) |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 79)** | EN: "Though Bobzien's accounts of the three contemporary theories of choice might per se be correct, it appears to me that her idea, that Alexander's view is that (i) choice is ἐφ' ἡμῖν, and that this is to be explained by the parallel novelty of (ii) seeing choice … as what is most important for moral appraisal, **rests on a misinterpretation of Aristotle**. I think that while (i) is absent in Aristotle, (ii) … is not." And the corrective: "**Associating it with choice evidently is not the same thing as saying that choice is ἐφ' ἡμῖν. Alexander is in fact merely associating the notion with choice.**" |
| **Aristotle counter-locus (p. 79, PDF-checkable)** | *EN* 1111b4–6 (Διωρισμένων δὲ τοῦ τε ἑκουσίου καὶ τοῦ ἀκουσίου, περὶ προαιρέσεως ἕπεται διελεῖν· οἰκειότατον γὰρ εἶναι δοκεῖ τῇ ἀρετῇ καὶ μᾶλλον τὰ ἤθη κρίνειν τῶν πράξεων): Aristotle *already* makes deliberate choice (προαίρεσις) the best mark of character — so a "new focus on choice over action" cannot explain Alexander's move. |
| **What he grants Bobzien** | The δύνασθαι → ἐξουσία shift (Bobzien 1998a 402, 407: "to be capable of" → "to have the power/authority over"), and that Alexander supplies the "earliest unambiguous" indeterminist-capable phrasing — he reports these accurately (pp. 77–78) but does not endorse the inference that Alexander therefore *predicates* ἐφ' ἡμῖν of choice. |
| **Ancient loci** | Alexander *De fato* (ἐξουσία at 181,6; the equation at 182,24 via Leroux; chs. cited 192/199/211 Bruns elsewhere in scholarship); *Mantissa*; Aristotle *Int.* 13, *Met.* IX (two-sided δύναμις), *EN* III. |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Contests Bobzien 1998b, 159–164** on the "choice is ἐφ' ἡμῖν" reading. **Contests Leroux 1990, 257** who makes αὐτεξούσιον "the very heart of τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν" in both Alexander and Plotinus — Eliasson promises (p. 36 n. 45 / ch. 6) to show "significant differences between Alexander and Plotinus." He does **not** weigh in on whether Alexander is "a libertarian" in the way the Debate-C scholars (Sharples, M. Frede, D. Frede, Donini, Ramelli) do — that is not his question. |

## CLAIM 5 — Plotinus' positive innovation: *to eph' hēmin* = the entirely self-directed activity of the virtuous soul (severed from praise/blame)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 219–222 (ch. 7); built from ch. 6 (VI.8) |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 221–222)** | EN: "What Plotinus ends up defending is a notion of ἐφ' ἡμῖν which states that **only the activities of the virtuous agent can be ἐφ' ἡμῖν**. Thus, it is clear that in Plotinus, this notion no longer relates to the issue of what makes our practice of praise and blame well-founded, but is rather **an aspect of the life of the perfectly virtuous agent**." Key condition (p. 220): "the soul must be **entirely self-directed** in order to have anything depending on itself, for the precise reason that in all other cases the origins not only of our actions but of our reasonings as well are external." |
| **The two-part criticism Plotinus levels (p. 221)** | Against both Stoics and Alexander, that applying our rational faculties to external impressions suffices: Plotinus demands (i) **general normative knowledge** of why the act is right (not just situational deliberation), and (ii) that virtuous activity is done "**for the sake of the inner perfection of the soul alone**," not for external situational facts. |
| **Greek** | τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν; ἀρχὴ αὐτεξούσιος (self-determined principle, VI.8.6.2.5); κατασκευή (constitution, of enslaved things, Enn. III.1.7.20); φύσις (reserved for the normative sense). NB Eliasson's flag (p. 41 n. 55): "**Plotinus never actually states that τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν is 'free' (ἐλεύθερον)**" — a direct check on interpreters (Leroux) who read ἐλεύθερον into VI.8.6.6. |
| **Ancient loci** | Plotinus *Enn.* VI.8[39] (principal); III.1[3] On fate (esp. 7.15, 9.10–11, 10.11–12, 7.20); VI.6[34]; I.4[46].7; III.2[47]. Alcinous *Did.* 153.12–15 as the nearest (but vague) precedent (p. 220–221). |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Contests the whole "free will interpretation" cohort** he catalogues in ch. 2.3.3–2.3.5: Gerson 1994 (155 f., 162 — "free will," mingles Plotinus' terms with his own; pp. 34–35); Rist 1967 ("Man's Free Will"; p. 34 n. 40); Atkinson 1983 (VI.8 = "P.'s study of the nature of free-will"; p. 34 n. 40); Zierl 1995 (αὐτεξούσιος = "der freie Wille"; p. 34 n. 40); Graeser 1972 (Kantian empirical/metaphysical self, "anticipation of Kant"; pp. 37–38); Leroux 1990 (αὐτεξούσιον as higher spiritual freedom = Alexander's; pp. 36–41). Verdict: all import a unitary "free will" that obscures Plotinus' actual, narrower notion. |

He **reports (and partly endorses)** Bobzien on Chrysippus; he **reports (and contests)** Bobzien on Alexander, Leroux/Gerson on Plotinus.
