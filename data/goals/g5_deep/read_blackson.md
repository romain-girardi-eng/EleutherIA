## Headline Thesis

Blackson argues that Michael Frede's identification of the minimum belief a Stoic must hold to possess a notion of a will is **wrong in its specification of the object of choice**. Frede holds that the minimum belief (proposition D) is that adult humans *choose to assent to impulsive impressions*. Blackson argues the correct minimum belief (proposition E) is that adult humans *choose to exercise their ability to use impressions*. On Blackson's reading: (a) Epictetus believed E, not D; (b) the early Stoics probably believed E too; therefore (c) Frede is wrong that the first notion of a will appears only with the late Stoics / Epictetus — the early Stoics very likely had it as well.

## Claims — One Section per Claim

---

### CLAIM 1 — Frede's minimum belief misidentifies the object of choice

**Claim:** Frede's schema of the will (proposition A) requires that in every action, a mental event — a choice or decision — brings about the action. When instantiated for the Stoics, Frede derives proposition D: the adult human *chooses to give assent to an impulsive impression*, and this assent is the willing and impulse to act. Blackson contends D mis-specifies the object of choice. There is a better instantiation, proposition E, in which the object of choice is *exercising the ability of reason to use impressions*, and assent is the downstream outcome of that exercise, not itself the thing chosen.

**Page:** 83–84 (abstract + introduction); formalised pp. 90–92

**Verbatim anchor (p. 83, abstract):**
> "Epictetus did not believe, as Frede argues, that adult human beings choose to assent to impulsive impressions. This gets the object of choice wrong in the minimum belief. He believed that the choice adult human beings make is to exercise their ability to use their impressions."

**Ancient locus:** Stoic soul theory (assent / impulsive impressions); Stobaeus doxography of Stoic ethics (Inwood & Gerson 1997, 216–17) — cited in fn. 24, p. 90

**Agrees/contests:** Contests Frede 2011, pp. 45–46 directly. Partial convergence with Long (2002, p. 214), who also thinks Epictetus's use of *prohairesis* does not mark a substantive departure from early Stoicism — but Long leaves the object of choice "unclear" (fn. 42, p. 96; fn. 722 in .md).

### CLAIM 2 — Frede's schema and how it generates the minimum belief (reconstruction)

**Claim:** Blackson reconstructs Frede's schema step by step to make the argument precise. The schema (A) is general: in every action, there is an ability in the mind, and an exercise of that ability which is the event of the agent choosing to do the action. For the Stoics, "the mind" is replaced by "reason in the soul," and "choosing to do y" is replaced (in Frede's reading) by "choosing to give assent to an impulsive impression." This yields D. The Stoics' own belief, without the choice-to-assent addition, is only proposition C (assent as a willing and impulse, but no *choice* to assent). For Frede, C is not yet a notion of a will; D is the minimum.

**Page:** 89–91

**Verbatim anchor (pp. 90–91, formal propositions):**

Proposition C (what Frede attributes to the early Stoics):
> "C. (for every adult human, h) (in reason in the soul of h, there is an ability, x) (for every action h takes, y) (there is an exercise of x, z): z is the event of h giving assent to an impulsive impression, and this assent is a willing and impulse in h to do y."

Proposition D (what Frede attributes to Epictetus):
> "D. (for every adult human, h) (in reason in the soul of h, there is an ability, x) (for every action h takes, y) (there is an exercise of x, z): z is the event of h choosing to give assent to an impulsive impression and this assent is a willing and impulse in h to do y."

Proposition E (Blackson's alternative):
> "E. (for every adult human, h) (in reason in the soul of h, there is an ability, x) (for every action h takes, y) (there is an exercise of x, z): z happens because h chooses to exercise x in a specific way, this exercise results in the event of h giving assent to an impulsive impression, and this assent is a willing and impulse in h to do y."

**Ancient locus:** Stobaeus / Arius Didymus doxography (Inwood & Gerson 1997, 216–17) for C; Epictetus *Discourses* 4.1 and passim for D vs E.

**Agrees/contests:** This is Blackson's neutral reconstruction of Frede before he contests him. He is not endorsing C, D, or E here — only clarifying what Frede means.

### CLAIM 3 — Frede's argument for Plato and Aristotle (neutral reconstruction)

**Claim:** Blackson reconstructs Frede's argument that neither Plato nor Aristotle had a notion of a will, to show *how* Frede operationalises his schema before turning to the Stoics. For Aristotle, Frede derives proposition B (same structure as A, but with "reason in the soul" substituted for "the mind"). Frede then argues Aristotle did not believe B because, in the tripartite theory, "reason is not made to appear in two roles, first as presenting its own case and then as adjudicating the conflict by making a decision or choice" (Frede 2011, p. 24, cited p. 88). The impetuous person does not exercise reason to choose.

**Page:** 87–89

**Verbatim anchor (p. 88, proposition B):**
> "B. (for every adult human, h) (in reason in the soul of h, there is an ability, x) (for every action h takes, y) (there is an exercise of x, z): z is the event of h choosing to do y."

**Ancient locus:** Aristotle *Nicomachean Ethics* VII.7, 1150b21–22 ("the impetuous is led on by his feelings because he has not deliberated"); *NE* III.2, 1112a13–17; *Eudemian Ethics* II, 1226b6–9 (on *prohairesis* as *hairesis* with qualification).

**Agrees/contests:** Blackson explicitly states he is "not endorsing or contesting" this argument (p. 87). He uses it only as a worked example of Frede's method. He notes that the argument is ambiguous between two readings (does Aristotle deny the impetuous makes *any* choice, or only a *rational* choice?), favouring the second reading as more charitable to Frede (pp. 87–88). Contests Mansfeld (2012, pp. 354–355) who mis-reads Frede as holding that bipartition of the soul "precludes a single will" (fn. 20, p. 89).

### CLAIM 4 — Frede does not offer evidence that Epictetus believed D rather than E

**Claim:** Frede gives no argument that Epictetus believed D (choice to assent) rather than E (choice to exercise the ability to use impressions). Frede's only move is the conjecture that because Epictetus used the term *prohairesis* — which Aristotle had used for a kind of choice — he "intends to include and to highlight the role of choice in the explanation of action" (Frede 2011, p. 44, cited p. 93). But this does not settle whether the object of the choice is the assent or the exercise of the ability. Blackson shows that Frede himself sometimes speaks as if Epictetus believed E (p. 46: "what is up to us, what is a matter of our choice, is how we deal with our impressions"), but then reads this as D in context.

**Page:** 93–96 (Section IV)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 95–96):**
> "Frede does not explicitly consider and rule out E. He is convinced that Epictetus used the term προαίρεσις for the will 'to mark that it is an ability to make choices, of which willings are just products' (p. 46). … I believe a different understanding of Epictetus is possible. … without further argument, it does not follow from Epictetus's use of προαίρεσις to name the ability Zeus gave him that he meant to mark this ability to use impressions as an ability to make choices."

**Ancient locus:** Epictetus *Discourses* I.1.12 ("Zeus gave him 'the use of impressions'"); *Discourses* I.22 (ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν μὲν προαίρεσις καὶ πάντα τὰ προαιρετικὰ ἔργα); *Manual* 1 and 6.

**Agrees/contests:** Contests Frede 2011 pp. 44–46. Agrees with Long (2002, p. 160) that Epictetus names the faculty of "use of impressions" *prohairesis*, but disputes Long's implication that this marks an ability to make choices (fn. 40, p. 95). Notes that Graver (2021, §4.3), Inwood (2022, pp. 403, 419), and Sorabji (2007) are all ambiguous on the object of choice.

### CLAIM 5 — The well-known Epictetan passages support E, not D

**Claim:** Blackson examines the most-cited Epictetan passages and argues none of them states that adults *choose to assent*; all of them are better read as saying that adults *choose the kind of thinking* (scrutinising, dwelling on, deflating, dismissing) they engage in with respect to impressions, and that the outcome of this thinking — assent or non-assent — follows from the thinking, not from a direct choice.

**Page:** 96–98

**Verbatim anchor — passage 1 (p. 96–97, *Discourses* II.18.23–26):**
> "[You can] defeat the impression and not be dragged off by it. Start by not being carried away by its intensity, but say: Wait for me a bit, impression; let me take a look at you and what you are about, let me test you. Next, don't let it lead you on by painting a picture of what comes next. Otherwise, it is off and away, taking you wherever it wishes. Instead, confront it with another impression, a fine and noble impression, and dismiss this foul one. If you accustom yourself to this kind of training, you will see what shoulders you will get, what sinews and strength…"

**Verbatim anchor — passage 2 (p. 97, *Manual* 34):**
> "Whenever the impression of some pleasure comes into your mind, guard yourself against being carried away by it, just as you should do with impressions in general. Let the thing wait a bit, and give yourself a pause. Then think of both times – first the one when you will enjoy the pleasure, and then the one after that when you will be sorry and be angry with yourself. Now contrast them with your joy and self-satisfaction if you abstain. But if you find this the right moment to embark on the affair, do beware that you are not being overwhelmed by its charm and sweetness and allure. Think how much better it is to realize that you have won this victory."

**Verbatim anchor — *Discourses* I.28 (pp. 97–98):**
> "[I]t is the mind's nature (φύσις) to assent to truths, to dislike falsehoods, and to suspend judgement in relation to uncertainties." (I.28.2)
(Greek: φύσις — verbatim present in source)

**Ancient locus:** *Discourses* II.18.23–26; *Manual* 34; *Discourses* I.22 (ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν μὲν προαίρεσις καὶ πάντα τὰ προαιρετικὰ ἔργα); *Discourses* I.28.2–8; *Manual* 1 and 6 (τῶν ὄντων τὰ μέν ἐστιν ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν, τὰ δὲ οὐκ ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν. ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν μὲν ὑπόληψις, ὁρμή, ὄρεξις, ἔκκλισις…; τί οὖν ἐστι σόν; χρῆσις φαντασιῶν).

**Agrees/contests:** Contests Frede 2011, pp. 44–48. Blackson notes that Epictetus does think the adult who is "carried away" has chosen incorrectly, but that "Epictetus does not say here that the mistake this adult made was to choose to assent to the impression" (p. 97). Implicitly supports Long's continuist reading (fn. 42, p. 96).

### CLAIM 6 — If D is the minimum belief, Frede is right about the early Stoics but wrong about Epictetus

**Claim:** Blackson grants as a conditional: if D is the correct minimum belief, then (a) the early Stoics did not hold a notion of a will (they believed C but not D — they do not say they believed adults choose to assent), and (b) Epictetus also did not hold D (the passages above do not support it). So Frede would be wrong about *both*, having invented an intermediate step (D for Epictetus) that the evidence does not sustain.

**Page:** 99 (Section V conclusion)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 99):**
> "If we accept that D is the minimum belief for a Stoic to have a notion of a will, Frede is wrong about Epictetus but right about the early Stoics. To believe D, the early Stoics would need to believe more than C and that impulses are assents to impulsive impressions. They would also need to believe that adults choose to assent to these impressions. They do not say they have this belief in the little evidence we possess for them, and this makes it very plausible to conclude they did not have this belief, and thus that Frede is right that they did not have a notion of a will."

**Ancient locus:** Stobaeus / Arius Didymus (Inwood & Gerson 1997, 216–17; Inwood 1985, 223–242; Long 1976) — listed fn. 49, p. 99 as the primary evidence for the early Stoics on choice and impulse.

**Agrees/contests:** This is a conditional concession to Frede on the early Stoics, under the D hypothesis only. Blackson rejects D as the correct minimum, so this concession is rhetorical/structural, not a final position.

### CLAIM 7 — If E is the minimum belief, both the early Stoics and Epictetus had a notion of a will; Frede is wrong on both

**Claim:** If E is the minimum belief (choosing to exercise the ability to use impressions), then: (a) Epictetus clearly had a notion of a will, because the textual evidence supports E rather than D; (b) the early Stoics *probably* had a notion of a will too, because E requires only the much more commonplace belief that when adults exercise the ability that results in assent, this exercise is something they choose to do. That belief is so natural that it would take positive evidence to think they rejected it; no such evidence exists. Therefore the first notion of a will is not a late Stoic innovation (against Frede) — Epictetus's innovation is pedagogical and terminological, not philosophical.

**Page:** 99–100 (conclusion of Section V)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 100):**
> "In this case, Frede is wrong about both Epictetus and the early Stoics. Given that Epictetus believed E, not D, and that E is the minimal belief a Stoic must possess to have a notion of a will, Frede is right that Epictetus had a notion of a will. Because, however, the early Stoics probably believed E too, Frede is very likely wrong that 'the first time we have any notion of a will' (p. 46) is in the time of late Stoicism. What is new is not the notion of a will but how Epictetus talked about standard Stoic doctrine. He used the term προαίρεσις to help focus the attention of his students on the ability of reason in adult human beings he described as the ability to use impressions and whose exercise the Stoics had probably all along thought is a choice adult human beings make."

**Ancient locus:** All Epictetan loci above; early Stoic sources via Stobaeus (indirect; fn. 49).

**Agrees/contests:** This is Blackson's positive conclusion. Contests Frede 2011, p. 46 ("the first time we have any notion of a will"). Agrees with Mansfeld (1991) and Mansfeld (2012, p. 356) that Chrysippus had a notion of a will (fn. 21, p. 89 — cited sympathetically). Partially convergent with Long (2002, p. 214) on continuity, but goes further by providing the E/D distinction to ground it.

### CLAIM 8 — Epictetus was a gifted teacher, not a philosophical innovator (with respect to the notion of a will)

**Claim:** Epictetus used *prohairesis* to respond to a widespread social/existential fear ("we might ultimately have no control whatsoever over our life," Frede 2011 p. 176) and to the realities of his experience as a slave. The term directed students' attention to what the Stoics had always believed was up to us. This makes Epictetus "more of a gifted teacher than a philosophical innovator" on the will.

**Page:** 96 (§IV)

**Verbatim anchor (p. 96):**
> "This interpretation shows Epictetus to be more of a gifted teacher than a philosophical innovator, but it also fits the most well-known passages we have for what he thought."

**Ancient locus:** *Discourses* I.1.12 (Zeus gave "the use of impressions"); *Discourses* II.18.26 (*askēsis*, "accustom").

**Agrees/contests:** Indirectly contests Frede's narrative of philosophical progress within Stoicism. Agrees with the social-historical reading Long gives (2002, p. 214 cited fn. 42, p. 96): Epictetus named "the autonomous functions of the mind by a single term" for pedagogical reasons, not to correct a theoretical gap in early Stoicism.

### CLAIM 9 — Frede's argument for Aristotle admits two readings; the better one is role-of-reason (not whether impetuous make any choice)

**Claim:** Blackson notes that Frede's argument against a notion of a will in Aristotle is ambiguous between (i) denying that the impetuous make *any* kind of choice, and (ii) denying only that the impetuous exercise *reason* to make a choice. The second reading is better: what matters to Frede is whether reason "appears in two roles" in the theory of the soul. Neither Plato nor Aristotle believed this; hence neither had a notion of a will. This is important because it clarifies how the schema instantiates: the missing ingredient is not "choice" as such but *rational* choice-making as a two-role function of reason.

**Page:** 87–89

**Verbatim anchor (p. 88):**
> "Given Frede's emphasis on the 'roles' of reason (p. 24), the second option allows for a clear view of his argument."

**Ancient locus:** *NE* VII.7, 1150b21–22 ("has not deliberated"); *NE* VII.7, 1150b27–28 ("they follow appearance" / ἀκολουθητικοὶ φαντασίᾳ).

**Agrees/contests:** This is Blackson's charitable reconstruction of Frede against the reading Mansfeld (2012, 354–355) gives.

### CLAIM 10 — The *prohairesis* translation issue is secondary; E preserves the standard "will" translation

**Claim:** Whether *prohairesis* is translated as "will," "moral choice," or "moral character," all existing translations are compatible with E. The translation does not settle the D/E question. Blackson notes that Frede himself says the *prohairesis* "insofar as it accounts for your willing whatever you will to do, can be called 'the will'" (Frede 2011, p. 46) — and this applies equally to D and E, since both make impulses result from choices.

**Page:** 99–100

**Verbatim anchor (p. 99–100):**
> "E, moreover, if it is the minimum belief, does nothing to upset the translation of προαίρεσις as the will in Epictetus's discussions. … D and E both account for the willings and impulses in adults as the outcomes of choices they make."

**Ancient locus:** *Discourses* I.22 (ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν μὲν προαίρεσις καὶ πάντα τὰ προαιρετικὰ ἔργα). Alternative translations: Kahn (1998, p. 252): "moral choice and all its works"; Dobbin (1998): "moral character and all its functions"; Inwood (2022, p. 371): "moral choice and all of its functions."

**Agrees/contests:** Agrees with the standard translation literature (Kahn, Dobbin, Inwood, Long 2002, pp. 218–219) that *prohairesis* refers to the faculty/capacity, not individual acts of choosing. Implicitly contests Frede's inference that naming this capacity marks it as an ability "to make choices" in the strong D sense.

### CLAIM 11 — Blackson's scope is limited; he is NOT claiming to resolve the full Epictetan corpus

**Claim:** Blackson explicitly limits his argument: "My argument is more limited. I show that in some of his most well-known discussions, what motivates him is the belief that adult human beings have an ability to use their impressions and that their exercise of this ability is a choice they make" (pp. 84–85). He does not rule out that other Epictetan passages might support D, and he does not survey the full *Discourses*.

**Page:** 84–85

**Verbatim anchor (p. 84–85):**
> "I do not work through all his discussions. My argument is more limited. I show that in some of his most well-known discussions, what motivates him is the belief that adult human beings have an ability to use their impressions and that their exercise of this ability is a choice they make, not, as Frede argues, that they have an ability to choose to assent to impulsive impressions."

**Ancient locus:** None specific here — methodological caveat.

**Agrees/contests:** Self-limitation; no external disagreement on this point.

## Notes on Integrity and Scope

- All verbatim quotes above are physically present in the `.md` extraction (native PDF text). No reconstruction.
- All "modern labels" ("notion of a will," "free will," "libertarian," "minimum belief," "first notion") are attributed to Frede or Blackson as their analytical vocabulary, never asserted as historical fact.
- Blackson distinguishes what Frede *asserts* (D is the minimum belief; Epictetus had it; the early Stoics did not) from what Blackson *reports Frede saying* he takes the evidence to support. Blackson's own positive claim (E is the minimum; early Stoics probably had it) is carefully hedged with "probably" throughout (pp. 84, 100).
- The article takes no position on whether the Stoics were "compatibilists," whether there was a genuine "free will" in antiquity, or whether Epictetus's account is true. Those are Frede's concerns, adopted as the frame of the debate.
