## HEADLINE THESIS (asserted)

The "free-will problem" was **not discovered** by any ancient philosopher within his own system; it was **inadvertently constructed** in later antiquity out of a confusion (mix-up) of *Aristotelian* and *Stoic* thought — specifically, a (mis-)interpretation of Aristotle's theory of deliberate choice (προαίρεσις) and "what depends on us" (τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν) in the light of Stoic causal determinism and Stoic moral responsibility. The earliest **unambiguous** evidence of awareness of such a problem is **Alexander of Aphrodisias** (*De Fato* + *Mantissa*), and even there it arises only at the collision-point of two systems, **without** a concept of a *will*. (Abstract, p. 133; Results §XIII, pp. 173–175.)

> Verbatim (Abstract, p. 133): "the 'discovery' of the problem of causal determinism and freedom of decision in Greek philosophy is the result of a mix-up of Aristotelian and Stoic thought in later antiquity; more precisely, a (mis-)interpretation of Aristotle's philosophy of deliberate choice and action in the light of Stoic theory of determinism and moral responsibility."

## CLAIM 3 — Two concepts of ἐφ' ἡμῖν: one-sided *causative* vs two-sided *potestative*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | (asserted, the analytic core) "Depending on us" splits into (a) **one-sided, causative** — "x depends on us" = *we are the cause/origin of x*; if x depends on us, **not-x does not**; a concept of causal dependency, NOT of any freedom (though it presupposes freedom-from-force, types 5–6). And (b) **two-sided, potestative** — "x depends on us" entails *not-x depends on us too* (a power for opposites; "up to us"). The two-sided is itself **neutral** between determinism and indeterminism; only on a particular (indeterminist) reading does it make the agent an undetermined "decision-maker." |
| **Page** | pp. 139–142 (the causative/potestative distinction, the walking example, the two readings of two-sided) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | (p. 141) "The one-sided, causative concept of what depends on us is not a concept of any kind of freedom, but of a particular kind of causal dependency." |
| | (p. 140) "Note that the two-sided, potestative ¤fƒ ²mÝn [ἐφ' ἡμῖν] itself entails neither determinism nor indeterminism." |
| **Ancient locus** | Alex. *Fat.* 181.5 (ἐφ' ἡμῖν = power of choosing the opposite); Alex. *Fat.* 180.26–8 (no pre-determining causes); LSJ ἐπί I.1g (potestative attested) |
| **Agree / contest** | Converges with **Inwood 1985, Brennan 2005, Salles 2005** (one-sided/causative reading of Stoic ἐφ' ἡμῖν). The 2001 book pins this on Chrysippus by name (Claim 4). |

## CLAIM 4 — Chrysippus / the early Stoa had ONLY the one-sided causative ἐφ' ἡμῖν; no free will, no freedom of decision

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | (asserted) The early Stoics, "in particular Chrysippus, clearly did not have an indeterminist two-sided conception of what depends on us." Their unitary, *corporeal* mind makes a causally-independent decision-faculty literally unthinkable; assent depends on the individual nature of the mind. Hence "there is in this model no space for free will," and the libertarian "could have done otherwise" condition "would have made little sense to Chrysippus." (1998.) The **2001 book** argues the same in detail: Chrysippus' ἐφ' ἡμῖν / παρ' ἡμᾶς can "only be one-sided and causative"; the freedom he presupposes is merely **freedom-from-force / freedom-from-external-determination**, never freedom of decision. |
| **Page** | 1998: pp. 142–143 (esp. p. 142 "no space for free will"). 2001: pp. 285–286 (Chrysippus' phrase "can only be one-sided and causative"); pp. 289–290 (the two-sided capacity of *assent* is NOT freedom of decision; the freedom presupposed = freedom from force/external determination) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | (1998, p. 142) "There is in this model no space for free will (i.e. for a decision making faculty that is causally independent of the mind's individual nature). … It follows that the libertarian condition for the attribution of moral responsibility, that we have freedom to do otherwise, would have made little sense to Chrysippus." |
| | (2001, p. 289) "Its role is not to enable a person to decide independently of their desires and beliefs, or to ensure indeterminist freedom of decision. Rather, the two-sided capacity of assent elevates rational creatures from non-rational ones." |

## CLAIM 5 — Aristotle's ἐφ' ἡμῖν is two-sided but NOT indeterminist; προαίρεσις is not itself "up to us"

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | (asserted) In *EN* III.2–3, III.5 and the *EE* parallels, Aristotle's "what depends on us" is the *generic range of actions* we deliberate about and choose between; it is two-sided (*EN* 1113b7–8: if doing depends on us, so does not-doing) but carries **no** indeterminism — no claim that the same agent in the same circumstances could choose otherwise. Crucially, for Aristotle the **choice itself (προαίρεσις) is NOT one of the things that depend on us**; it is *of* them. Reading proairesis as itself ἐφ' ἡμῖν is a later (post-Aristotelian) shift. |
| **Page** | pp. 143–145 (Aristotle); p. 144 (proairesis is *of*, not *among*, the ἐφ' ἡμῖν); pp. 159–160 (the later Paraphrase/*Mantissa* 169.38 making προαίρεσις itself ἐφ' ἡμῖν) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | (p. 144) "The choice we make (proaÛresiw [προαίρεσις]) is itself not one of the things that depend on us, and the idea that it was would have been quite alien to Aristotle's thinking." |
| | (p. 144) "But Aristotle's concept of what depends us does not entail indeterminism." |
| **Ancient locus** | Arist. *EN* 1113a10–11, 1112a21–33, 1113b3–1115a3, 1113b7–8; *EE* II.6 (1223a1–9), II.10, 1226a27–8; vs *Mant.* 22, 169.38 (ἐφ' ἡμῖν ἡ προαίρεσις) |
| **Agree / contest** | Anti-anachronist; aligns with Labarrière 2009, Eliasson 2008 on Aristotle. (Cf. Donini 2010, who argues Alexander *distorts* Aristotle's "for the most part" — Bobzien notes Alexander classifies choice-events under "for the most part," not "in equal parts," p. 156.) |

## CLAIM 6 — The indeterminist two-sided ἐφ' ἡμῖν was *manufactured* by Middle-Platonist Aristotle scholarship (the "mix-up")

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | (asserted — the engine of the whole thesis) An "Aristotle scholar" behind the common Middle-Platonist fate-source (preserved in [Plutarch] *De Fato*, Nemesius, Calcidius) **fused three unrelated Aristotelian doctrines**: (i) "what depends on us" from *EN* III.3; (ii) two-sided possibility / capacities-for-opposites from *De Int.* 13 + *Met.* Θ; (iii) the *future contingents* / "as it happens" (ὁπότερ' ἔτυχεν) of *De Int.* 9. Subordinating ἐφ' ἡμῖν to "the contingent" and coining the category "in equal parts" (ἐπ' ἴσης) for it, this synthesis made ἐφ' ἡμῖν readable as **indeterminist** — a reading absent from Aristotle himself. This is the "inadvertent conception." |
| **Page** | pp. 146–153 (§§V–VII); summarised pp. 151–152 (the three-doctrine fusion); pp. 153–155 (Alcinous/Ammonius take the step toward undetermined choice) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | (pp. 151–152) "we have in this Middle-Platonist common source a concept of what depends on us which seems to be the outcome of bringing together and systematising three bits of Aristotelian doctrine: the things that depend on us … from the Nicomachean Ethics, book III 3; the concept of two-sided possibility … from De Interpretatione 13 and Metaphysics Y; and the problem of future contingents … from De Interpretatione 9." |
| **Ancient locus** | Nem. *Nat. hom.* 103–4, 104.6–7, 114.21–4; [Plu.] *Fat.* 571b–d, 570f–572f; Calc. *Tim.* 155–6; Arist. *De Int.* 9 (18a39–b9, 18b9), 13 (22b36–23a6); *EN* III.3 1112a31–3; Alcin. *Didasc.* 179.20–3, 179.31–3; Amm. *In Int.* 130.23–33, 242.24–7 |
| **Agree / contest** | Agrees with **Sharples** that Alexander drew not from the Middle-Platonist texts but from a common source they shared (p. 156 n. 37: "On this point I agree with Bob Sharples"). |

## CLAIM 7 — In Alexander the decisive innovation is δύνασθαι → ἐξουσία (a decision-making faculty); BUT he still stops short of a free will

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | (asserted) Two additions distinguish Alexander's ἐφ' ἡμῖν from the Middle-Platonist account: (i) "to choose" (αἱρεῖσθαι) added to "to act" — motivated by the moral focus on *choice* (Stoic/Epictetan + Platonic Myth-of-Er influence), **not** by a quest for freedom of decision; (ii) the replacement of "being capable of" (δύνασθαι) by "having the power/authority (ἐξουσία) over" — synonymous with being κύριος — which *does* introduce the agent as a **causally undetermined decision-maker**, a step toward a free will. **Yet** Alexander "stops short of a concept of free will": his soul is corporeal and susceptible to causal impact, so there is "no suitable place for an independent decision making faculty." He has collected "all the ingredients" but they "do not add up to choosing and acting from free-will." |
| **Page** | pp. 159–164 (§X, the choice-addition); pp. 163–167 (§XI, ἐξουσία); pp. 171–172 (ingredients but "stops short"); p. 173 (Results) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | (p. 172) "But all these points do not add up to 'choosing and acting from free-will,' since for Alexander the human soul is not separable from the body and in principle susceptible to causal impacts." |
| | (p. 165) "the change to ¤jousÛa [ἐξουσία] in the account may have been of great significance, since it provided a way to express that the agent is a causally undetermined decision maker. The introduction of the term … is then also one further step towards the concept of a free will." |
| **Ancient locus** | Alex. *Fat.* 181.5–6, 180.26–8, 181.14, 184.18–19; *Fat.* 182.22–4 (αὐτεξούσιον = what ἐφ' ἡμῖν "really means"); 33 instances of ἐξουσία per Thillet's index; *Mant.* chs. 22–23; Epict. *Diss.* I 25.2, IV 1.62 etc. |
| **Agree / contest** | Against reading Alexander as a settled libertarian (vs the "seemingly libertarian" framing she allows at p. 134). Cf. Sharples 1983 (Alexander is libertarian as a category, but doesn't establish it) and M. Frede 2011 (the ancestor, but incoherent). |

## CLAIM 8 — Alexander is "almost an isolated case"; freedom-to-do-otherwise was a marginal, volatile phenomenon, and the one-sided ἐφ' ἡμῖν stayed respectable

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | (asserted) Even at the turn of the 2nd c., awareness of a determinism-vs-freedom-to-do-otherwise problem was **not** standard repertory. Three signs: (1) the one-sided causative ἐφ' ἡμῖν was a serious, approved option in non-Stoic (Platonist/Peripatetic, even Neoplatonist) writers — not a Stoic fudge; (2) *Mantissa* ch. 22's "not-being" (τὸ μὴ ὄν) solution is handled so awkwardly it shows unfamiliarity with indeterminist freedom (and makes "depending on us" a defect — a weakness of tension); (3) Alexander's own *De Fato/Mantissa* "steadily vacillate" between determinist, un-predeterminist and indeterminist readings of "power to do/choose opposites." Indeterminist freedom is unambiguously in play **only** where the two-sided ἐφ' ἡμῖν collides with the Stoic causal principle. |
| **Page** | pp. 167–172 (§XII) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | (p. 167) "There are several points that suggest that at his time Alexander is almost an isolated case, and that concepts of freedom to do otherwise are a rather marginal phenomenon without a clear philosophical context." |
| **Ancient locus** | [Plu.] *Fat.* 571d–e (one-sided account); *Mant.* ch. 22 (172.7–9, τὸ μὴ ὄν); Nem. *Nat. hom.* 114.15–16; Plot. *Enn.* III 1.9–10, 2.10, VI 8.7 (Neoplatonist one-sided); Alex. *Fat.* chs. 6, 29, *Mant.* 174.33–5 (clearly indeterminist passages); Alex. *Fat.* 180.29–31 (regret argument → MR2) |

## CLAIM 9 — A free-will problem proper arises only later, in Platonist/Christian THEOLOGICAL determinism (providence + origin of evil) — and there a faculty of *will* enters via Epictetus' προαίρεσις

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | (asserted, the Christian/theological pivot — bears on the thesis's H2/H3) Neither Stoics nor the Peripatetic Alexander face a free-will problem *within* their system (the Stoa connects responsibility to autonomy MR1, not do-otherwise; Alexander just denies pre-determination, having no universal providence). The genuinely felt problem appears in **Middle Platonists and Christians**, who posit an **immaterial soul** initiating action outside the material causal net but still under **divine providence** — so the problem becomes predetermination by God's providence/foreknowledge and the origin of evil. It is **here** that "a faculty of the will is introduced (no doubt influenced again by Epictetus' concept of προαίρεσις)." After Alexander the do-otherwise problem survives mainly in *De Interpretatione* 9 commentaries. |
| **Page** | pp. 172–173 (no FW problem inside Stoa/Alexander); pp. 173–174 (Platonist/Christian theological problem; will introduced); pp. 174–175 (Results) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | (p. 173) "It is in this context that finally a faculty of the will is introduced (no doubt influenced again by Epictetus' concept of proaÛresiw [προαίρεσις]), to warrant the independence of human evil deeds from god's providence or creation." |
| | (p. 172) "The Stoics did not require a concept of free-will, since they did not connect moral responsibility with freedom to do otherwise. … Theirs is the problem of the compatibility of autonomous agency and causal determinism." |
| **Ancient locus** | Alcin. *Didasc.* 153.4–5 (immaterial soul acting through body); Plato *Rep.* 617e (αἰτία ἑλομένου· θεὸς ἀναίτιος — the Myth of Er, recurrent in Platonist fate-theory); Justin *Apol.* 44; Origen named as "may have been aware of it" (p. 175) |

## CROSS-WORK CONSISTENCY (1998 ↔ 2001)

The 1998 article and the 2001 monograph are mutually reinforcing and Bobzien cross-cites her own book repeatedly (e.g. 1998 p. 138 nn. 3–4 → *Det. & Freedom* §§8.1, 8.2, 8.4; p. 142 n. 8 → §6.3.5; p. 161 n. 47 → §7.1). The 2001 book supplies the *named-Chrysippus* grounding that 1998 only gestures at: §6.3.5 (pp. 285–290) argues Chrysippus' παρ' ἡμᾶς / ἐφ' ἡμῶν "can only be one-sided and causative" (p. 285) and that the freedom he presupposes is "freedom from being forced … and freedom from being externally determined" (p. 290), explicitly **not** indeterminist freedom of decision. No contradiction found between the two works.
