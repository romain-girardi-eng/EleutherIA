## HEADLINE THESIS

Stoic determinism is a **physical, causal, teleological** universal determinism (fate = god = providence = nature), and the early Stoa (Chrysippus) had **no concept of free will and no free-will problem**. Their *to eph' hēmin* / *par' hēmas* is **one-sided and causative** ("happens because of us"), grounding moral responsibility in the agent being the *main causal factor* of the action via the faculty of assent — NOT in a two-sided "freedom to do otherwise." The genuinely *modern-looking* problem (causal determinism vs. indeterminist freedom to do otherwise) is **not discovered by anyone within a single system**; it arises only in the **2nd c. AD**, *between* systems, when refined late-Stoic determinism (PHILOPATOR) collides with a two-sided potestative *eph' hēmin* of Aristotelian descent defended by Alexander of Aphrodisias — and even Alexander **"stops short of a concept of free will"** (p. 412). A real free-will problem becomes pressing only later still, and only in **Platonist and Christian** theology, where the determinism is teleological/providential, not causal-physical.

## CLAIM 2 — The decisive distinction: ONE-SIDED CAUSATIVE (*par' hēmas / ex hēmōn*, with γίγνεσθαι) vs. TWO-SIDED POTESTATIVE (*eph' hēmin*, with εἶναι)

| field | content |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 281–285 (definitions 282–284; Chrysippus' usage 284–285) |
| **Verbatim anchor (two-sided)** | "By 'two-sided' I mean that if some occurrent or action x depends on some person, then not-x depends on that person, too … the class of things that depend on us … consists of precisely 50 per cent unrealized possibilities" (pp. 281–282). |
| **Verbatim anchor (one-sided)** | "I believe that where we have the phrase παρ' ἡμᾶς γίγνεσθαι this does not usually denote a two-sided, potestative, concept of dependency, but **a one-sided causative one**." (p. 284). "When I call the phrase 'one-sided', I understand this to entail that if some occurrent or action x depends on some person, then not-x does **not** depend on that person" (p. 284). |
| **Verbatim anchor (verdict on Chrysippus)** | "they contain sufficient information to **rule out** that Chrysippus understood the phrase in an indeterminist, potestative, two-sided way." (p. 283). "here Chrysippus' understanding of παρ' ἡμᾶς and ἐξ ἡμῶν can only be **one-sided and causative**." (p. 285). |
| **Ancient locus** | Cic. *De Fato* 40 (*assent and action depend on us / are in our power*); Eus. *Praep. ev.* 6.8.2, 6.8.25–6, 6.7.33, 6.7.26 (Diogenianus reporting Chrysippus on παρ' ἡμᾶς / ἐξ ἡμῶν γίγνεσθαι); Sextus *M* 5.48 (only passage using both phrases together, treats παρ' ἡμᾶς one-sided, ἐπί+dat. two-sided potestative). |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Supersedes her own earlier paper** (n.100: "The view I expound … is a modification of what I wrote on this topic in Bobzien 1997 and supersedes that publication"). Distances herself from those (incl. some readings in **Long/Sedley 1987**, Sorabji 1980, Botros 1985, cited around the co-fatedness sections) who treat the phrases as interchangeable/two-sided. |

> **This is the central object of the deep-read prompt ("one-sided vs two-sided"). Chrysippus' responsibility = causal self-origination through assent, NOT a power for opposites.**

## CLAIM 3 — Chrysippus' compatibilism rests on the FACULTY OF ASSENT, not on the meaning of *eph' hēmin*

| field | content |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 286 (cf. cylinder/cone analysis pp. 290–296, §6.3.3) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | "The key to Chrysippus' compatibilism is in the end not so much his understanding of phrases like ἐφ' ἡμῖν or παρ' ἡμᾶς, but **the function he gives to the faculty of assent** in this context." (p. 286). The assent-faculty "is a two-sided capacity of the mind (the mind can either give or withhold assent), and whatever motion is the result of the use of this capacity, is … not a forced or externally determined motion, but one for which the agent bears causal responsibility." (p. 286). |
| **Ancient locus** | Cic. *De Fato* 41–43 (cylinder-and-cone; *adsensio in nostra potestate*); Gellius *NA* 7.2.11; Plut. *Stoic. rep.* 1055f–1056a. |
| **Agrees / contests** | Explicitly rejects the "**decision-maker model**" / libertarian reading of the mind (pp. 286–287: "based on the indeterminist libertarian assumption that agents are undetermined in their choices by external and internal causal factors alike") — i.e. against any reading that detaches the agent from character/dispositions. |

## CLAIM 4 — Epictetus shifts *ta eph' hēmin* but stays within compatibilism; he does NOT introduce free will (contrast point for Frede)

| field | content |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 331–338 (§7.1) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | "Philosophically there is perhaps **less of a difference** between Epictetus and the early Stoa than this account makes one think. The underlying general idea of which occurrents (γιγνόμενα) count as depending on us seems to be **the same as for Chrysippus**" (p. 334). For Epictetus "the element of causation is not discussed, and the factor of self-origination is seldom emphasized" but is "presupposed"; he singles out as *ta eph' hēmin* only "those types of things which are in our power **under all possible circumstances and with absolute certainty**" — primarily "the use of impressions" (assents) (pp. 332–333). |
| **Ancient locus** | Epict. *Diss.* 1.1; 4.1.68–75; *Ench.* 1, 2, 5; Musonius (Stob. *Ecl.* II 159.25–160.11, fr. 38 Hense). |
| **Agrees / contests** | **This is the Bobzien⟂Frede fault line.** Bobzien keeps Epictetus inside compatibilist Stoicism; Frede 2011 makes Epictetus the inventor of the "first actual notion of a free will." (Frede not named in this section, but the two readings are directly opposed.) |

## CLAIM 5 — Stoic *eleutheria* (freedom) is ethical/psychological (sage-only) and is NEVER connected to *eph' hēmin* in the determinism debate

| field | content |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 338–344 (§7.2); 345 (§7.2.4) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | "In **none** of the testimonies on early Stoics is there any connection drawn between this concept of freedom and that which depends on us (ἐφ' ἡμῖν). Freedom … belongs exclusively to ethics (including politics); that which depends on us belongs to physics, in particular to psychology." (p. 341). "only sages can obtain this freedom" (p. 339). Even where ἐλεύθερος and ἐφ' ἡμῖν are later linked (Alexander, Nemesius), "ἐλεύθερος **no longer has anything to do with** the early Stoic or the Epictetan technical philosophical sense of the word … there is even in later ancient philosophy **no evidence that the Stoic concept of ἐλευθερία played a role** in the debate about the compatibility of fate and the kind of freedom that is required for moral responsibility." (p. 345). |
| **Ancient locus** | DL 7.32–3, 7.121, 7.175; Zeno's *Republic* (DL 7.32–3); Plut. *Aud. poet.* 33d (Zeno's Sophocles revision); Philo *Quod omnis probus liber* 97; Cic. *Fin.* III 75, *Acad.* II 136. For the later link: Justin *1 Apol.* 43 (80d–e); Alex. *Fat.* 188.21, 189.10; Nem. *Nat. hom.* 105.24. |
| **Agrees / contests** | Blocks the inference (drawn by some) from Stoic "freedom" language to a free-will concept. |

> **Bears on Debate D (ii):** Bobzien here dates the close *eleutheros*↔*eph' hēmin* linkage to **shortly after Epictetus**, with **Justin Martyr (c. AD 100–165)** as "perhaps the earliest surviving evidence" of ἐλευθέρα προαίρεσις entering the fate-vs-*eph'hēmin* discussion (p. 345, *1 Apol.* 43, 80e), echoing Epictetus' προαίρεσις; whether Justin meant it indeterminist "is hard to make out" (p. 345). Tatian: "ἐλευθερία τῆς προαιρέσεως" (*Orat.* 7) (n.42).

## CLAIM 6 — PHILOPATOR (later Stoic, presumably 2nd c. AD): a refined compatibilism with a sharpened Causal Principle; still one-sided, still no free will

| field | content |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 387–396 (§8.5–8.6); 396 (§8.7 opening) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | "PHILOPATOR'S concept of that which depends on us is **one-sided, causative, and compatible with causal determinism. His compatibilism does not differ much from that of Chrysippus**, except, perhaps, in that he introduced a different specified causal principle, and produced a philosophical definition of that which depends on us." (pp. 396–397). |
| **Ancient locus** | Reconstructed mainly from Nemesius *De nat. hom.* and Alexander *De Fato* / *Mantissa*; PHILOPATOR's Causal Principle ≈ "same cause, same circumstances → same effect" (the principle Alexander negates at *Mant.* 174.3–12). |
| **Agrees / contests** | Aligns PHILOPATOR with Chrysippus (continuity of compatibilism); against any reading that finds free will developing *inside* late Stoicism. |

> This is the "**late Stoic compatibilism**" pillar of the deep-read prompt: late Stoicism *refines determinism* (and thereby provokes its critics) but does **not** evolve toward libertarian free will.

## CLAIM 8 — Diachronic conclusion: the free-will problem is "formulated only in the second century AD," *between* systems; a genuine free will is first needed in **Platonist + Christian theology**

| field | content |
|---|---|
| **Page** | 409–412 (§8.7 conclusion) |
| **Verbatim anchor** | "A problem of determinism and freedom to do otherwise thus arises **only in the confrontation of the two philosophical systems**, when later Stoic causal determinism meets late Peripatetic freedom to do otherwise" (pp. 411–412). "the problem of the compatibility of causal determinism and freedom to do otherwise appears to have been **formulated only in the second century AD**." (p. 412). "It is in this context that finally **a faculty of the will is introduced** (no doubt influenced again by Epictetus' concept of προαίρεσις, and … indirectly by the early Stoic concept of assent) … The need for a free will becomes pressing in **Platonist and Christian philosophy**, in the context of the problems of how vice entered the world, and how god's providence and foreknowledge … is compatible with human responsibility … Rather the determinism is now **teleological only, and the context theological**." (p. 412). |
| **Ancient locus** | Middle-Platonist "hypothetical fate" (Alcinous *Didasc.* 153.4–5); Plot. *Enn.* III 1.2; the Christian/Platonist providence-and-evil problematic (unnamed texts, but Origen's milieu). |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Neither Stoics nor Peripatetics "experience within their systems any problem of determinism and freedom to do otherwise"** (p. 411). This is the explicit "**fall**" of the problem-frame: it never was a stable, transmitted problem in antiquity. |
