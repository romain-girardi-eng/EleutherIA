## HEADLINE THESIS

Karamanolis's governing claim (pp. 130, 135–140, 161): the early-Christian theory of **free will is not derived from Scripture** — Scripture lacks both the discussion and the terminology (`τὸ ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν`, `προαίρεσις`, `αὐτεξούσιον`, p. 133) — but is **taken over from the Stoics**, specifically the notion of *will* as Epictetus reconceived `προαίρεσις` (pp. 137–139). The Christians, however, **modify** the Stoic doctrine on one decisive point: against the Stoic view that freedom = siding with the good (so a vicious act is *enslavement*, not free), the Christians (from Justin on) adopt the **Peripatetic/Platonist two-sided conception** (Alexander, Plutarch) on which a free choice is the open ability to choose *either of two opposites*, good **or** evil (pp. 142–143, 146). The trajectory runs Justin→Tatian/Theophilus→Irenaeus/Tertullian→Clement (will = a *faculty*, `τὸ βούλεσθαι`, p. 150)→Origen (we author even our own *temperaments/characters*, pp. 152–157)→Nemesius + Cappadocians (same conclusion **without** Origen's pre-existent intellects, pp. 157–161). On the historiographical "invention of the will" question, Karamanolis **explicitly sides with Bobzien and Frede against Dihle** (pp. 135–136, n. 15).

## CLAIM 1 — The will-concept and its terminology are NOT scriptural; they are Stoic imports

- **Page:** 133 (md 6588–6592, 6593–6609).
- **Verbatim anchor (English, his own prose):** "Scripture not only lacks a discussion of all the important issues in this area but also lacks the relevant terminology which early Christians employ in their writings. In particular, I have in mind the terms *to eph' hemin* (τὸ ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν), *prohairesis* (προαίρεσις), and *autexousion* (αὐτεξούσιον). … All three terms are of Stoic provenance." (p. 133)
- **Original in file:** the three Greek terms are printed verbatim as above (τὸ ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν, προαίρεσις, αὐτεξούσιον), md 6590–6591.
- **Ancient loci he hangs it on:** Aristotle *N.E.* III.1 (prohairesis = "choice"); Epictetus *Disc.* I.4.18–21, I.17.21–8, II.2.1–7, II.18.19–25 (prohairesis recast as a critical disposition); for autexousion, Epictetus *Disc.* 4.1.62, 68, 100; Alexander *On Fate* 182.22–24; SVF II.975 (n. 10, md 7926–7928).
- **Agrees / contests:** explicitly **agrees with** and is "especially indebted to" **Frede (2011)** and **Bobzien (1998a/b)** (n. 14, md 7934–7935); cites them for ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν (Bobzien 1998a: 276–290, 334), προαίρεσις (Bobzien 1998a: 402–406; Frede 2011: 44–48), αὐτεξούσιον (Bobzien 1998a: 332–336, 353–355; Frede 2011: 74–75, 102–104). nn. 7–10.

## CLAIM 2 — The notion of "will" is absent from Plato and Aristotle; it emerges in the Hellenistic schools (the "invention of the will" position)

- **Page:** 135–136 (md 6714–6743), with the historiographical alignment at n. 15 (p. 162, md 7936).
- **Verbatim anchor (English):** "the notion of will is not necessary for explaining human action, as we tend to think today in the wake of post-Kantian philosophy. I side with those who believe that this notion is conspicuously absent from Plato and Aristotle, to say nothing of the Presocratics." (p. 135–136)
- **Verbatim anchor #2 (the explicit scholarly alignment, n. 15, p. 162):** "In this respect I side with Bobzien (1998a, 1998b) and Frede (2011) against Dihle (1982)." (n. 15)
- **Original in file:** [no Greek in this passage — it is a methodological claim].
- **Ancient loci:** Aristotle *N.E.* III.6, 1113a16–18 (akratic does not *choose* against his rational choice, he "fails to stand by it"); Plato *Rep.* X (choice of *lives*).
- **Agrees / contests:** **agrees with Bobzien & Frede** (the will is a post-classical / Hellenistic development); **contests Dihle (1982)** (who located the genuine concept of will later, in Augustine, and denied it to the Greeks). This is the load-bearing **Debate-B** datapoint: Karamanolis places the *origin* of the will-concept with the Stoics/Epictetus, not with Augustine.

## CLAIM 3 — Epictetus' *prohairesis* IS the notion of "will" that the Christians inherit

- **Page:** 138–139 (md 6817–6844).
- **Verbatim anchor (English, his rendering of Epictetus):** "You are neither flesh nor hair but *prohairesis*; if you render that virtuous, then you will be virtuous" (*Disc.* III.1.40) — followed by Karamanolis's gloss: "For Epictetus a human being is his or her *prohairesis*, which we can call volition or will. … Their [the Christians'] notion of will is, I suggest, the one we find in Epictetus." (pp. 138–139)
- **Original in file:** [no Greek printed for *Disc.* III.1.40 — English only]; the term προαίρεσις itself is printed throughout.
- **Ancient loci:** Epictetus *Disc.* III.1.40; II.9.2; *Disc.* I.29.1 (n. 27: "The essence of the good is a certain kind of prohairesis…", md 7953–7954).
- **Agrees / contests:** notes the translation dispute — "Long (2002) translates *prohairesis* as 'volition', while Frede (2011) translates it as 'will'" (n. 23, md 7946); aligns with **Long (2002: 207–220), Sorabji (2007), Brouwer (2020)** (nn. 9, 20–21). He **asserts** the filiation Epictetus→Christians as his own thesis ("I suggest").

## CLAIM 4 — The Stoics held a position Karamanolis calls "Stoic compatibilism"; the cylinder/cone analogy illustrates it

- **Page:** 140, restated 142–143 (md 6910–6932; 7021–7035).
- **Verbatim anchor (English):** "The analogy is meant to illustrate the Stoic compatibilism, that is, the view that one's mind or nature plays the critical or decisive causal role in the quality of the effect." (p. 142)
- **Original in file:** [no original — analytic gloss]; the cylinder/cone sources are listed at n. 29 (Cleanthes/Chrysippus via Cicero *De fato* 39–43 = SVF II.974/LS 62C; Alexander *On Fate* 191.30–192.28 = SVF II.945/LS 55N; Gellius *N.A.* VII.2.6–13 = LS 62D), md 7956–7958.
- **Agrees / contests:** "compatibilism" / "Stoic compatibilism" is **Karamanolis's own label, applied by him** — keep attributed. He relies on **Bobzien (1998a: 259–271)** for the analogy (nn. 30, 38) and **Brouwer (2020)** for fate-as-providence (n. 28). NB he uses "compatibilism" descriptively and does *not* flag it as anachronistic (contrast the EleutherIA KG's hedging convention).

## CLAIM 5 — Justin is the FIRST Christian philosopher to engage free will, and he *conflates* causal determinism with necessity (so his anti-Stoic polemic is "somewhat misplaced")

- **Page:** 140–143 (md 6935–7054); the critical judgement at 142 (md 7021–7035).
- **Verbatim anchor (English):** "Justin's claims against the Stoics are somewhat misplaced, because they distinguished between necessary and fated actions, although some sources conflate the two, just as Justin does." (p. 142)
- **Verbatim anchor #2 — Justin's own argument, Karamanolis's translation (p. 141, *1 Apol.* 43.3–7):** "If mankind does not have the power to avoid the evils (*ta aischra*) and choose the goods in virtue of our ability to choose freely (*prohairesei eleutherai*), then all actions whatsoever are without cause (*anaition*). … We see that the same man does opposite things. If it were fated (*heimarto*) that a man were either wicked or virtuous, he would not be capable (*dektikos*) of opposites…"
- **Original in file:** Justin's key phrase is given in Greek at n. 33: "Justin's phrase **πλειστάκις μετετίθετο** (*1 Apol.* 43.6)" (md 7962–7964) — and Karamanolis notes the verb μετατιθέναι = "change one's mind" (LSJ). The block-quote of 43.3–7 itself is **English-only in the file** (translation, no facing Greek).
- **Ancient loci:** Justin *1 Apol.* 43.1–8 (esp. 43.2, 43.3–7); *2 Apol.* 6.4, 6.9, 7.4–6 (`ep' amphotera trepesthai`, "able to turn either way"); further at 28.3–4, *Dial.* 88.5, 102.4, 141.1 (n. 32); Scripture/Plato support *1 Apol.* 44.1–8 invoking Deut 30:15,19; Isa 1:16–20; Plato *Rep.* 617e (n. 35).
- **Agrees / contests:** he judges the Stoics actually **did** distinguish causal determinism from necessity (siding with **Bobzien 1998a/b** against the conflation, nn. 34, 38), so Justin's charge misfires; he suggests Justin's two-sided ("X or Y") conception was "inspired by Plutarch and Alexander of Aphrodisias" (p. 142, md 7036–7040), citing **Boys-Stones (2007b), Frede (2011: 89–101)**, and **Minnis (2010: 268)** on Justin–Alexander affinities (n. 39). For Justin's *eleuthera prohairesis* he refers to **Amand (1945: 201–207)** (n. 31).

## CLAIM 6 — The Christians' decisive break with Stoicism: a WRONG (vicious) act is itself a *free* act (two-sided freedom), not enslavement

- **Page:** 142–143 (md 7040–7054); reaffirmed for Tertullian p. 147 (md 7240–7250) and Clement pp. 149–150.
- **Verbatim anchor (English):** "A wrong action, then, can count as a perfectly free action according to Justin. … For Justin, however, the choice we make is always free no matter what we decide." (p. 143)
- **Original in file:** Justin's tag is given in transliteration in the running text — "by the ability to choose freely (*prohairesei eleuthera*)"; full Greek not facing.
- **Ancient loci:** Justin *1 Apol.* 43.3–7 (above); Plato *Rep.* 379c, 617e; *Theaet.* 176a (cited p. 143 for "God is not responsible, only man is").
- **Agrees / contests:** he reads this as the Christians siding with **Alexander/Plutarch (Peripatetic/Platonist)** against Stoic single-track freedom — refers to **Frede (2011: 95–101)** "critical assessment" of Alexander's conception (n. 51). This is the central **Debate-D** claim: patristic *autexousion* ≠ Stoic eleutheria.

## CLAIM 7 — Clement makes the will a *faculty* of the mind (`τὸ βούλεσθαι`), the dominant one — a step beyond mere "power to choose"

- **Page:** 148–151 (md 7264–7420); pivot at 150–151 (md 7404–7420).
- **Verbatim anchor (English, his rendering of *Strom.* II.16.77.5):** "The will (*to boulesthai*) then has always the first role. For the other faculties of the mind are meant to be its servants." (p. 148)
- **Original in file (n. 53, p. 163):** "ἅ δὲ μὴ ποιοῦμεν, ἤτοι διὰ τὸ μὴ δύνασθαι οὐ ποιοῦμεν ἢ διὰ τὸ μὴ βούλεσθαι … **προηγεῖται τοίνυν παντων τὸ βούλεσθαι. αἱ γὰρ λογικαὶ δυνάμεις τοῦ βούλεσθαι διάκονοι πεφύκασι**" (*Strom.* II.16.77.2–5) — md 8016–8018. Karamanolis adds the philological observation that Clement uses neither βούλησις (early Stoics) nor βούλημα (Epictetus) but the infinitive βούλεσθαι (n. 53).
- **Ancient loci:** Clement *Strom.* IV.19.124.2–3; *QDS* 14.2–4 (Greek printed at n. 52: "Οὐ χρὴ τοίνυν τὸ ἐξ ἑαυτοῦ μὴ ἔχον … κριτήριον ἐλεύθερον … καὶ τὸ αὐτεξούσιον τῆς μεταχειρίσεως τῶν δοθέντων", md 8011–8015); *Strom.* II.16.77.5; IV.24.153.1 (`pistein hē apistein`); VI.16.135.2–4 (`prohairetikē dynamis`); *Paed.* I.87.1–2 (faith as choice); grace at *Strom.* V.1.7.1–2, *QDS* 21.1–2.
- **Agrees / contests:** **asserts** the Clementine "faculty" step as his own reading; references **Karavites (1999), Havrda (2011b)** (nn. 54, 57) and **Brouwer (2020)** on the βούλεσθαι/βούλησις/βούλημα terminology (n. 53).

## CLAIM 8 — Origen's distinctive advance: we are responsible not only for our *choices* but for our *temperaments and characters*; he keeps Stoic psychology of `propatheiai`/first movements but adds that we never *lose* free will

- **Page:** 151–157 (md 7423–7701).
- **Verbatim anchor (English, his gloss):** "Origen makes an important contribution to the debate by arguing that not only our choices are free but that we also are the authors of our temperaments and characters." (p. 161, Conclusion).
- **Verbatim anchor #2 (Origen's first-movements doctrine, his translation of *Princ.* III.1.4, p. 155):** "let him look at his own affections (*pathē*) and movements and see whether there is not an approval, an assent (*sygkatathesis*), and an inclination (*rhopē*) of the reasoning faculty (*hēgemonikon*) towards this attitude…"
- **Original in file:** the *Princ.* III.1.4 block-quote is **English-only** in the file (Greek not facing); Origen's tag for "by our own choice" is given in Greek at n. 73: "**ἰδίᾳ προαιρέσει ποιοῦμεν** (*Philokalia* ch. 23, 21; SC 226: 204)" (md 8072).
- **Ancient loci:** Origen *Princ.* pref. 4–5 (free will an apostolic truth); whole of *Princ.* book III; pre-existent intellects *Princ.* I.3.8, I.4.1, II.8.4, II.9.2; foreknowledge≠causation *Philokalia* ch. 23 = SC 226:142, 204 (and the cause-of-knowing vs. cause-of-event distinction); first movements *Princ.* III.1.4–5, III.2.2–4; propatheia *Comm. in Ps.* PG 12, 1141D, 1144, 1396AB; never-losing-freedom *Comm. in Rom.* V.10.15; Seneca parallels *Nat. Quaest.* II.38.2–4, *De ira* II.3–5. Plato *Rep.* X / myth of Er (inspiration), with Porphyry *On What is Up to Us* frs. 268–271 Smith.
- **Agrees / contests:** heavily indebted to **Frede (2011: ch. 7, esp. 108–112, 114–116, 122–123)** and **Arruzza (2011: 129–205, 133–143)**; cites **Ramelli (2013: 137–221)** on Origen vs. Valentinian/Stoic determinism, and **Sorabji (2001: 346–351, 358–360)** and **Layton (2000)** on first movements (nn. 62–75). Notes Origen's theodicy (pre-existent intellects) "will meet with criticism," while his theory of free choice "will exert a strong influence" (p. 157).

## CLAIM 9 — Nemesius and the Cappadocians (Basil, Gregory of Nyssa) reach Origen's conclusion WITHOUT pre-existent intellects — a more "naturalist" route

- **Page:** 157–161 (md 7704–7868); summary 161 (md 7888–7899).
- **Verbatim anchor (English, his summary):** "Nemesius and the Cappadocians follow up on this and lend further support to Origen's idea without assuming a pre-existing, disembodied intellect; they rather focus on the natural features of the human mind and body that we need to train and educate…" (p. 161).
- **Original in file (Nemesius, n. 82):** "**ἄνθρωπον ἀρχὴν εἶναι τῶν ἰδίων ἔργων καὶ αὐτεξούσιον**" (*De nat. hom.* 39, 113) — md 8091. (Gregory, n. 91: "**Καὶ ἔσμεν ἑαυτῶν τρόπον τινὰ πατέρες … ἀπὸ τῆς ἰδίας προαιρέσεως**" *Vita Mosis* 328B, md 8116–8118: "we are in a sense fathers of ourselves.")
- **Ancient loci:** Nemesius *De nat. hom.* chs. 35–41 (esp. 36 pp. 106–108; 39 pp. 113–114; 41 pp. 117–120) — inspired by **Galen**, *Quod animi mores…* (n. 84); Basil *Hex.* 6.4–7, *That God is not the Author of Evils* PG 31, 332CD, 341B, 344B–345BD, 348A; Gregory of Nyssa *Against Fate* (GNO III.2, 35.14, 37.14–38.10, 40.23–41.5, 56.17–18), *De anima* (GNO 86–91), *On the Song of Songs* (GNO VI, 345–346), *Vita Mosis* 328B.
- **Agrees / contests:** distinguishes Basil's privation-of-good view of evil from **Proclus** (*On the Existence of Evils*) — Basil makes evil come about "by the will of a human being or of an angel," not as a mere shadow-side-effect (p. 159). Notes Gregory's "freedom from / freedom to" distinction (eleutheria vs. prohairesis, p. 160) and his **naturalist** explanation of phenomena (aligned with Seneca's *iura naturae*, against the biblical theological-omen reading). References **Amand (1945), Mosshammer (1990), Motta (2008), Sharples & Van der Eijk (2008)** (nn. 82–92).

## CLAIM 10 — Both the Gnostics AND their Christian critics operate with a will-concept that is NOT scriptural (the polemic is intra-philosophical)

- **Page:** 135 (md 6700–6704); framing 132–135.
- **Verbatim anchor (English):** "Both the Valentinians and their critics, however, operate with a notion of will and its freedom that does not come from the Scriptures, as I have said." (p. 135)
- **Original in file:** Tatian's anti-autexousion line is given in Greek at n. 40: "**…ἀπώλεσεν ἡμᾶς τὸ αὐτεξούσιον. Δοῦλοι γεγόναμεν οἱ ἐλεύθεροι, διὰ τὴν ἁμαρτίαν ἐπράθημεν**" (*Or.* 11.2, md 7980–7981).
- **Ancient loci:** Irenaeus *Adv. Haer.* I.6.1–3, IV.4.3 (Latin printed n. 47: "*Homo vero rationabilis … liber in arbitrio factus et suae potestatis…*", md 7997–8000), IV.37.1–4, V.6.1, V.29.1; Tertullian *Adv. Marc.* II.6.1, II.6.5, II.9.9, II.10.1; Valentinian three-class doctrine via Irenaeus.
- **Agrees / contests:** on Valentinian free-will doctrine he cites **Dihle (1982: 150–157), Thomassen (2006), Magris (2020)** (nn. 6, 43–45) — note that here he *uses* Dihle as a source on the Valentinians even though he rejects Dihle's main thesis on the will's origin. On Irenaeus, **Amand (1945: 212–223), Fantino (1985), Osborn (2001)** (n. 42).
