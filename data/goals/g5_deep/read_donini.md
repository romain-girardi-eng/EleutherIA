## HEADLINE THESIS

Donini reads Aristotle as **anti-determinist but NOT a libertarian indeterminist**. Two structural claims define his reading:

1. **The "principle of correspondence" reading of De Int. 9** (logoi conform to pragmata): Aristotle does NOT deny bivalence/truth for the future; he *qualifies* it. Future-tensed truth is possible because present states of affairs (`ὅσα οὕτως ἔχει`, 19a34) are "pregnant" with contrary future outcomes and can stand in for the absent future *pragmata*. This restates the traditional Ammonius/Boethius "indeterminately true" reading, refined.

2. **The "unidirectionality of habits" (NE V 1, 1129a11–17 + Metaph. IX 5):** a settled hexis is NOT "of contraries." The virtuous man can will and do *only* good actions, the vicious *only* bad — "always and necessarily," not merely "for the most part." Aristotle's indeterminism is therefore **local and two-tiered**: it operates (a) at the *origin* of character (which we voluntarily form) and (b) on *which particular* action (among those of the same moral quality) is chosen in a situation — but NOT on the moral *quality* of the mature agent's act. The space of contingency is real but "undoubtedly restricted" (p. 94).

On Alexander (debate B): Donini's verdict is **"excellent but tendentious interpreter."** Alexander understood Aristotle correctly in his *commentaries*, but in the polemical *De Fato* he **suppresses Aristotle's "for the most part" frequencies "in quite unaristotelian fashion"** to widen the space of freedom — "reticence and some distortion." Yet (his later, revised view) the *De Fato*'s seeming extreme indeterminism can be rendered **largely coherent** if read as a two-class theory (ordinary agents vs. *phronimos*).

## DEBATE C — ARISTOTLE'S OWN POSITION

### C1 — De Int. 9: Aristotle qualifies (does not abolish) bivalence; the future is "backdated" onto present pragmata
- **Page:** 4–5; resolution at 13–17.
- **Claim:** The chapter has 3 parts (18a28–34 / 18a34–19a6 "deterministic argument" / 19a7–end "Aristotle's reply"). The deterministic argument's "fundamental flaw," from Aristotle's viewpoint, is that "instead of moving from things to establish the truth of statements, the argument assumes as universally valid a principle regarding *logoi* and from there claims to draw conclusions about how things are" (p. 3). Aristotle restores the correct order facts→words.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 15–17):** "the principle of the correspondence of logoi to facts can and must be considered valid also for the future. It was therefore a matter of explaining in what sense the future could be considered a *pragma*: the doctrine of contingency, introduced in 19a9-22 and summarized in the concise and effective formula of 19a34, provides the answer." And on the sea-battle: "Aristotle is not saying that necessarily there will be or there will not be, somewhere on some sea, a sea-battle" — only of *this* determinate impending battle (sense (b), not (a)), p. 16–17.
- **Ancient loci:** Arist. *De Int.* 9, **18a28–19b4**, esp. **19a23–b4**, **19a32–35** (ὅσα οὕτως ἔχει ὥστε... / antiphasis), 19a36–38; 19a12 (τουτὶ τὸ ἱμάτιον / "this cloak"); 18a33 (καθ' ἕκαστα καὶ μέλλοντα). Companion texts: Cic. *De Fato* 28–31 (=SVF III 956) and 20–21 (SVF III 912) on the ignava ratio (p. 5–7).
- **Agrees / contests:** Aligns with **Ammonius & Boethius** (indeterminate truth) and broadly with **G. Seel** (p. 380, "substantially also" his reading, p. 15). **Parts ways with Dorothea Frede** on 19a27–29 — "the point at which I believe I must part ways with D. Frede's analysis in (1970) 67–69 and (1985)² 73ff." (p. 11, n. 21), faulting her Greek (esp. her dropping the particle *ge* at 19a28). Rejects **Gilbert Ryle** (*Dilemmas* 1954, 25–27), **Hintikka** ("momentary correspondence"), **White**, **Waterlow** (p. 17–18, nn. 30, 32).

### C2 — Metaph. E 3 (accidents): the uncaused ἀρχή is at step (3), not the interpolated step (4); Aristotle deliberately did NOT mention the "encounter"
- **Page:** 33–37.
- **Claim:** Modern interpreters (pseudo-Alexander → Sorabji → Heinaman → D. Frede → Weidemann) "interpolate" a step (4) — Nicostratus meeting thugs — and locate the accidental uncaused cause there. Donini: this is "heavy-handed and daring." The accidental factor is already supplied by step (3), *leaving the house to drink*, which is neither necessary nor "for the most part" the consequence of being thirsty.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 36):** "It is no longer interesting to him given that the intervention of the accidental factor was already supplied by one of the previous moments (explicitly mentioned in the text) in the series. ... it is the third moment that introduces an accidental factor — neither always, nor for the most part is leaving the house the consequence of being thirsty: only those who have no water or wine at home would do so."
- **Ancient loci:** Arist. *Metaph.* **E 3, 1027a29–b16** (esp. 1027b5, b11–14); *Metaph.* E 2, 1026b27–33, 1027a8–16, a13, a16; Δ 30, **1025a14ff.** (classic def. of accident, treasure-digging example). pseudo-Alexander *In Metaph.* 454,34–38 H.
- **Agrees / contests:** Adopts **Madigan**'s step-labelling (A–E) but rejects his acceptance of the traditional "spicy food = uncaused cause." Contests **Sorabji** (9), **Heinaman**, **D. Frede** (1982, 281), **Weidemann** for placing the cause at step (4). Notes **Seel** (375) and **D. Frede** (1985¹, 221) "very wisely avoid" the identification but at the cost of not interpreting the example at all.

### C3 — NE III deliberation has TWO objects ("for the most part" AND ad utrumlibet); no contradiction with De Int. 9
- **Page:** 63–66.
- **Claim:** The alleged conflict (NE III 1112b8ff. confining deliberation to "for the most part," vs. De Int. assigning only ad utrumlibet) is dissolved by reading 1112b8–9 correctly. Donini follows **David Ross's** literal translation (preserving the manuscript *kai*) against the Rassow/Ramsauer conjectures adopted by Stewart, Gauthier, Burnet, **Irwin**, Kenny, Dirlmeier.
- **Verbatim anchor (Ross's translation Donini endorses, p. 63):** "Deliberation is concerned with things that happen in a certain way for the most part, but in which the event is obscure, and [my emphasis] with things in which it is indeterminate." → two classes, not one.
- **Conclusion (p. 65):** "there is no real contradiction between NE III and de Int. 9 regarding the object of deliberation. According to Aristotle, deliberation always concerns at least two classes of objects, not one only."
- **Ancient loci:** Arist. *NE* III 3, **1112b8–9**; I 3, 1094b21; VI, 1139a8ff. (τὸ ἐνδεχόμενον ἄλλως ἔχειν); MM 1189b24; *De part. an.* 639a22. — Also (p. 60–62) the **statistical tripartition of the contingent** (50/50 *hopoter' etyche*; ">50% for the most part"; "<50%") at *NE* 19a18–22 mapped onto *De Int.*; Socrates-bathing vs. sea-battle as the two contrasting examples.
- **Agrees / contests:** With **Ross** and **Ackrill (1973)**. Against **Irwin (1980/1985)**, the conjecture-tradition (Rassow, Ramsauer, Stewart, Gauthier, Burnet, Dirlmeier, Kenny), and **Aspasius** (73,5–7). Tentatively even proposes emending *NE* 1112b9 to read ἀόριστον (p. 76–77, n. 58).

### C4 — UNIDIRECTIONALITY OF HABITS (the keystone): a hexis is not "of contraries"; the mature agent's moral quality is necessitated
- **Page:** 89–94 (NE V 1); reinforced 92–94 (Metaph. IX 5).
- **Claim:** This is Donini's most distinctive and emphasised thesis. From *NE* V 1, 1129a11–17: capacities/sciences are of contraries, but **habits are not** — "from health will not issue contrary behaviors, but only healthy ones." Applied to moral hexeis: "a just man can will and do only just actions, an unjust one only unjust actions. And all this, one would say, not simply 'for the most part' either, but **always and necessarily**" (p. 90). Metaph. IX 5 (1048a5–16) is consistent: orexis/proairesis is the *to kyrion* that determines which single effect a rational (contrary-capable) capacity produces, and "each of these processes ... will occur **necessarily**" (1048a14).
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 93–94):** "Decision, in summary, will direct the doctor's procedures according to the constant end envisioned from the moral standpoint, but, each time, it will determine the choice (always in conformity to the moral end) of one or the other possibility open from the technical standpoint. And each of these processes, Aristotle specifies (1048a14), will occur necessarily. The space open to contingency — that is, to the choices possible to the moral agent — is undoubtedly thus restricted."
- **Crucial qualification (p. 100–101):** the habit "determines the moral quality of every action and only in this sense limits the agent's potential choices: it prevents the performance of actions belonging to the opposite moral quality, but it does not dictate what action in each particular situation is to be chosen." → **local indeterminacy survives** (which just act), **global indeterminacy does not** (its moral quality).
- **Ancient loci:** *NE* **V 1, 1129a11–17** (a7–10, a10 βούλονται/ἀδικοῦσι); III 5, **1114a12–21**, 1114b30–32 (cannot drop habit at will; vice analogous to illness); III 8, **1117a18–22** (courage in sudden danger > deliberated courage — habit valued above calculation); *Metaph.* **IX 5, 1048a5–16**; IX 2, 1046b2–24; *Cat.* 13a23–31 (habit-change possible); *NE* I 10, 1100b22–1101a8 (good man keeps character under misfortune).
- **Agrees / contests:** Credits **D.J. Furley** (225–226 n. 15) as the last to see the NE V 1 point clearly ("with whose 'main contention' ... I agree fully," p. 90 n. 29). Against **Gauthier** (who "manages to make the text say the exact opposite," making habit a rational capacity — "patently absurd"). Against **W. Bondeson** (59: that men can act "contrary to their states of character" — Donini: "utterly impossible to share"). Against **Fortenbaugh** (71) and **Irwin (1985)** on a "second proairesis." Against **Hardie (1980)** and **White (39–41)** for grounding indeterminacy in Metaph. IX / to eph' hemin (Metaph. "never mentions to eph' hemin"). On the *stability of character under misfortune* Donini sharply **disagrees with Martha Nussbaum** (336–40) re NE I 10, siding with Botros and Vegetti — though agreeing with Nussbaum (p. 343) that character is "relatively stable, but not definitively inalienable."

### C5 — eph' hemin reconciled with unidirectionality; NE later & more refined than EE
- **Page:** 102–108.
- **Claim:** The apparently libertarian NE III 5 1113b7–14 ("where acting depends on us, not acting also depends on us...") does NOT establish open choice between good and evil for the mature agent. Two readings save Aristotle: (i) en hois = *general classes* of action, referring to the original (pre-habit) indeterminacy; (ii) the habit is "never an absolutely definitive and inalienable possession" — one always retains the remote power to *change* one's habit, so "eph' hemin" holds in that weaker sense. EE II uses eph' hemin more broadly (it enters the very *definition* of the voluntary, EE II 9, 1225b8–10) and so reads "more decisively indeterministic"; but EE lacks the unidirectionality theory and is "less rich." Hence (genetic argument) **NE is later than EE**.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 107):** "In EE (book II), it appears that eph' hemin has a broader meaning than it does in NE and Aristotle's position could thus be more decisively indeterministic; but it is also true, probably, that the whole theory is less rich and elaborate here compared to NE."
- **Ancient loci:** *NE* III 1, **1110a14–17**; III 5, **1113b7–14**; 1114a19ff., b32ff.; V 1; *EE* II 6, 1223a8; 1225a10ff.; **II 9, 1225b8–10**; II 10, 1226a28, b30–32; *Phys.* VIII 4, 255a9ff.; *Top.* V 7, 137a8–20.
- **Agrees / contests:** Follows **Sorabji** (234–37, 274ff.) on eph' hemin ≠ hekousion and on NE-later-than-EE; with **Kenny**, **Woods**, **Heinaman (1986)**, **Düring** on relative chronology. Against **Irwin (1980, 154 n.45; 1985, 319)** and **Loening** (283ff.) on the eph' hemin reading. Notably invokes **Alexander** as Aristotle's best ancient reader who *deliberately stopped short* of the extreme inference (see B-section).

### C6 — Voluntariness ≠ a separate "responsibility"; the "activities of the virtues" are post-habit manifestations
- **Page:** 138–143 (ch. VI).
- **Claim:** Against Irwin, Donini denies Aristotle separates "voluntary" from "responsible" (children/animals act voluntarily AND are subject to sanction by the paterfamilias). And αἱ τῶν ἀρετῶν ἐνέργειαι (1113b5) = actions *expressing* an already-formed virtue, NOT (pace Irwin) the formative exercises.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 138–139):** "to speak of the 'voluntariness' of vice and virtue does not at all entail drawing a distinction between 'voluntariness' and 'responsibility' ... Such a distinction is alien to the text of the Nicomachean Ethics."
- **Ancient loci:** *NE* III 5, **1113b3–7**, b21ff.; III 3, 1111a26; 4, 1111b8ff.; X, 1180a30–b7; II 1, 1103b21–23.
- **Agrees / contests:** Long, explicit running argument **against T. Irwin** (1980 essay; 1985 edition) on both points; sides with **Nussbaum (1986, 285ff.)** against Irwin's "pessimistic view of animals and children"; endorses **Burnet** on 1113b5–6.

## DEBATE B — ALEXANDER OF APHRODISIAS, *DE FATO*

### B1 — Alexander "reticent / distorts" Aristotle in the De Fato: suppresses "for the most part"
- **Page:** **74–77** (ch. III §7) — THIS is the locus the quick-pass map cites; verified correct.
- **Claim:** Alexander is the *originator* of the correct two-class reading of human initiative (in his *In An. Pr.* commentary) but in the *De Fato* he silently drops the "for the most part" class to maximise the agent's alternatives.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 74):** "although — in quite unaristotelian fashion — he emphasizes as much as possible the abundance of alternatives always available to the acting subject, he is careful not to associate the object of deliberation, as Aristotle does in NE III or in de Interpretatione 9, with a precise reference to frequencies of occurrence: he never mentions 'for the most part' events, nor the rival group of ad utrumlibet events."
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 77):** "removing 'for the most part' as the particular object of choice and deliberation ... could mean widening the scope of freedom of will and opening greater spaces of contingency to moral choice. For a philosopher engaged in a harsh polemic against determinism, this was a result worth some reticence and some distortion of Aristotle's thought."
- **The contrast text (verbatim, p. 74–75), Alexander *In An. Pr.* 162,31–163,7:** "the first of the two meanings of the contingent is therefore this ...; and also coming within this meaning should be the actions resulting from an order determined in conformity to decision: since for these too it holds that they are 'for the most part.'" Example: it is "for the most part" that Socrates converses with *someone* but "indefinite" / equally possible that he converses "precisely with that particular person."
- **Ancient loci:** Alexander **De Fato ch. XI, 178,15ff. Bruns**; **ch. XII, 180,12ff.**; *Quaest./Ethical Problems* IV, **160,4–19 Br.** (paraphrase of NE III 1112a21–b12 that "carefully omits" 1112b8–9); vs. **Alex. In An. Pr. 162,31–163,7**; Arist. *NE* III 3 1112b8–9, *De Int.* 9, *An. Pr.* I 13 32b5–10; Ammonius (parallel error). NB Donini notes Alexander writes ὡς ἐπὶ τὸ πλεῖστον (not τὸ πολύ), De Fato 169,30; 170,3.11.19.22.
- **Agrees / contests:** Self-citation (Donini 1987¹, 1987²). The negative-attestation claim ("never mentions") is exactly the corpus-checkable assertion flagged in `arbitrable_debates.md`.

### B2 — The "freedom to do also the opposite" (τὸ ἀντικείμενον) is NOT extreme indeterminism — reconcilable with Aristotle
- **Page:** 165–169 (ch. VII §3).
- **Claim (Donini's REVISED, more charitable position):** Alexander's eph' hemin-defining formula "that for which it is possible to do also the opposite" (τὸ ἀντικείμενον) "lacks any precedent in Aristotle" *as literal phrasing*, but is defensible if τὸ ἀντικείμενον is read as **affirmative/negative opposites (X / not-X, Cat. 11b23)** rather than **contrary opposites (good/bad, Cat. 11b21)**. Then "do also the opposite" = choose another action *still compatible with the end and the agent's character* — not free oscillation between good and evil.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 166):** "all one can prudently attribute to Aristotle based on the passage of NE III 5 is a conception of to eph' hemin as 'what it is in our power to do or to abstain from doing' — which, one will note, is precisely the cautious starting point for Alexander's discussion in On Fate XII 180.4ff."
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 169):** "a wise and entirely virtuous man is not really free to engage in any behavior of his choice, but is doubly constrained, both by the end required of him ... and by the stability of his inclinations and his moral character."
- **Ancient loci:** Alex. *De Fato* XII **180,4ff., 180,20–23**; 196,13–25; *Mantissa* 170,1ff.; chs. **XXVII–XXIX** (199,30; 200,3ff.) on the *phronimos*; Arist. *NE* III 5 1113b7ff., V 1 1129a11–16; *Cat.* **11b17–23**; *Metaph.* Z 7 1032b6–21 (doctor/massage example); *Phys.* VIII 4 255a9ff.
- **Agrees / contests:** **Partially recants** his own earlier (1974, 170–85) verdict of incoherence: "My earlier objections were certainly not entirely unmotivated, but I do think they should be qualified" (p. 166). Engages **R.W. Sharples** (1975², 1983) closely — agrees on much, still dissents on chapter XV. With **Sorabji (1980, 232–234)**.

### B3 — Two-class reading rescues *De Fato*'s coherence — but residual contradictions remain "insuperable" within Aristotelianism
- **Page:** 170–176 (ch. VII §§4–5).
- **Claim:** The "three criteria for action" (kalon/useful/pleasant, ch. XV 185.23–28) and the "do the opposite" claims apply ONLY to **ordinary, not-yet-virtuous agents**, while chs. XXVII–XXIX restrict freedom drastically for the *phronimos*. Read genetically (two moments in the formation of character) the treatise is "substantially consistent" with itself and with Aristotle. BUT: defending the freedom of the *not-yet-virtuous* (incl. the incontinent) gives that freedom **no moral dignity**, producing an "utterly irresolvable contradiction" with the ideal of the *phronimos*.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 175–176):** "the distinction between a class of human agents for whom a broader indeterminist claim would make sense, and another class, for which one would instead have to apply fairly drastic limitations ... will ultimately always produce serious difficulties, and as far as I can see, ones that are insuperable if one seeks to remain within the bounds of Aristotle's philosophy."
- **Reframing of the treatise's aim (p. 176):** "None of the major problems of philosophical coherence posed by On Fate ... relates directly to Stoicism. ... another crucial preoccupation of Alexander's should be evident: that of giving a coherent account of Aristotelianism as an indeterminist philosophy." → De Fato is NOT merely an anti-Stoic tract.
- **Ancient loci:** Alex. *De Fato* **XV 185,21–28**; **XII 180,20ff.**; XXVII (197,17ff; 198,20), **XXVIII 199,7ff.**, **XXIX 199,24ff., 199,31–200,2**; XXXIV **206,12ff.** (κατόρθωμα requires power to do the worse — "no trace whatsoever in the NE"); 178,17ff., 183,30ff., 184,20; *Mantissa* 169–172. Arist. *NE* II 4 1105a28–b9.
- **Agrees / contests:** **Sharples** throughout (concedes Sharples's 1980, 92 n.92 criticism as "justified"; still dissents on ch. XV/XXXIV). Cites **D. Frede (1982, 280)** on the paradox that only rare/exceptional cases escape determination. **Burnyeat** (82ff.) on incontinence/criteria.

### B4 — Fate = individual nature is un-Aristotelian and self-inconsistent (debate B, source/coherence sub-thread)
- **Page:** 159–165 (ch. VII §§1–2) and 177ff. (ch. VIII).
- **Claim:** Alexander's chs. I–VI identify fate with **individual nature** (φυσικαὶ κατασκευαί, 170,14/17), which (a) is not Aristotelian — Aristotle grounds character in education/habituation, not natural constitution — and (b) contradicts ch. XXVII (197,17ff.), where Alexander correctly limits natural endowment to the "capacity to receive virtue." Source candidates: Theophrastus and/or **Galen** (resemblances), but evidence insufficient to decide.
- **Verbatim anchor (p. 161):** "To identify fate with individual nature is therefore not only not Aristotelian; it seems designed to make it very awkward to defend an indeterminist thesis; and Alexander is furthermore utterly inconsistent in his evaluation of natural endowments."
- **Ancient loci:** Alex. *De Fato* VI **169,18–170,8; 170,9–171,17** (Socrates/Zopyrus); XXVII 197,17ff.; *Mantissa* "On Fate" 169–172, 186,29 (Theophrastus, Polyzelus); Arist. *De Gen. et Corr.* II 10 336a23–337a15; *NE* X 9 1179b21.
- **Agrees / contests:** Defends his own 1974 reading against **Thillet (1984)** (first/second-nature defence — Donini: doesn't help) and engages **Sharples (1980, 1983)**, **Verbeke (1968)**, **Todd (1977)**, **Moraux**. In ch. VIII adopts a "small but extremely fruitful" suggestion from **Jaap Mansfeld** to ease the two-part coherence problem.
