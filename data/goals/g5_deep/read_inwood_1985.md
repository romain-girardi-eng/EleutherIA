# Inwood 1985 — Position Dossier
**Source:** Brad Inwood, *Ethics and Human Action in Early Stoicism* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1985; repr. 1999). ISBN 0-19-824462-2.
**Scope of this read:** Introduction (pp. 1–5), Ch. 1–2 (pp. 9–41), Ch. 3 full (pp. 42–101), Ch. 4 (pp. 103–115), Conclusion cues, Appendices (structure only). PDF pages consulted: 1–115, 215–230 (notes pp. 258–306 cross-checked for footnote anchors).
**Integrity note:** Every verbatim quote below is transcribed from the PDF as read. Passages marked [not-verbatim] are paraphrases or composite summaries. Greek rendered as in the PDF (OCR sometimes imperfect, flagged where uncertain). All modern interpretive labels (compatibilism, free will, libertarian) are attributed to Inwood or to scholars he names — never asserted as historical fact in this dossier.

## 1. Headline Thesis

**Claim:** The early Stoa produced a sophisticated, coherent psychology of action centred on *hormê* (impulse) and *synkatathesis* (assent) that is best understood on its own terms, not through the lens of modern "free will" or Aristotelian deliberation. Assent — not a capacity to do otherwise — is the locus of moral responsibility. The Stoic theory is what Inwood calls a *compatibilist* reconciliation of fate and responsibility, in which "our behaviour is in our power without being cut loose from the causal nexus of fate" (p. 66, not-verbatim synthesis; see verbatim at §2 below). This is Inwood's own characterisation of the Stoic position — he does not himself endorse or contest the label "compatibilism" as a philosophical verdict; he uses it descriptively.

**Methodological commitment (p. 1):** The reconstruction "cannot pretend to certainty; it must commend itself by its ability to account for the evidence, by its coherence with what we know about other parts of the Stoic system and by the plausibility of its relation to other ancient discussions of the nature of action."

## 2. Claim: Assent as the locus of responsibility — NOT a power to do otherwise

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 66–68, 71, 80–83, 96 |
| **Inwood's position** | Assent (*synkatathesis*) grounds moral responsibility because it is the internal, rational cause of action. The Stoics were not concerned with whether one could, in the same circumstances, have done otherwise — that is Alexander of Aphrodisias' criterion, not theirs. |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 66)** | "Human action is always controlled by the mechanism of rational assent to the hormetic propositions occasioned by our presentations; thus our behaviour is in our power without being cut loose from the causal nexus of fate. We are 'free' at least in the sense that we are responsible for our actions." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 71)** | "Assent, then, is vital to the Stoic analysis of action because it is the locus of moral responsibility." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 83)** | "Assent is such an event; in order to hold men responsible for all their actions, implicit assents had to be postulated." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 96)** | "As Long says, there is in Stoicism no traditional Kantian or existentialist 'will' as a distinct power and faculty in the human soul; and we ought to feel uncomfortable with attempts to identify anticipations of such notions in the Stoic theory." |
| **Ancient locus** | Chrysippus cylinder argument (cited via Sorabji's third interpretation, pp. 68–69); Origen *De Principiis* III.1.3–4 (pp. 77–79); Clement *Stromates* (p. 83); Epictetus *Diss.* 2.6.6–10, 4.1.84 (p. 83) |
| **Scholar contested** | Stough (Charlotte): Inwood accepts her "best recent account" (p. 66, n. 131) but modifies her Sorabji-reading interpretation. Sorabji's third interpretation (assent is internal; Chrysippus does not ask whether it is necessitated, only whether it is necessitated by *external* causes) is accepted but "needs some slight modifications" (p. 68). |

## 3. Claim: The Cleanthes *Hymn to Zeus* does NOT express free will

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 69–71 |
| **Inwood's position** | Inwood explicitly argues AGAINST reading the famous *Hymn to Zeus* lines ("nor is any deed done without you, God … except the deeds done by bad men in [or because of] their folly") as a reference to an autonomous "free will." The passage has been so interpreted; Inwood rejects that reading as wrong on internal Stoic grounds. |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 70)** | "These lines have been interpreted as a reference to an autonomous 'free will'. But this must be wrong. For it would be strange indeed if bad men were thought to be free of the chain of cause and effect in a way that good men are not. This would have to mean that bad men are ultimate sources of self-motion while good men are not. This is highly unlikely." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 70–71)** | "Bad men are not acting any more freely or responsibly than good men; all men act 'rationally' in this sense. Rather, bad men are acting apart from Zeus, without following normative Right Reason. They have, as Chrysippus said, 'turned their back on Reason'." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 71)** | "There is no reason to deny this compatibility [of Cleanthes' Hymn with the general Stoic position on responsible action]." |
| **Ancient locus** | Cleanthes *Hymn to Zeus* (lines cited via SVF 1.537, the enigmatic passage); Chrysippus SVF 2.1000 (p. 67, n. 134) on character caused by external factors; D.L. 7.89 (p. 70, n. 149) on Right Reason |
| **Source for "turned their back on Reason"** | Footnote 150, referenced at p. 70 (Galen *PHP* 4.3.7, p. 248). Not-verbatim; Inwood is paraphrasing Chrysippus via Galen. |
| **Who is contested** | Inwood does not name the earlier scholars who read the Hymn as expressing "free will," citing only the interpretation (fn. 148 = SVF 3.390 / *Virt. Mor.* 450c–d: "They do not displace our reason as such"). The target is a diffuse reading-tradition, not one named scholar. |

## 4. Claim: Alexander of Aphrodisias distorts Stoic *eph' hēmin* — his "free choice between opposites" criterion is NOT the Stoic standard

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 88–91 |
| **Inwood's position** | Alexander's critique of Stoicism is sophisticated and informed, but it operates by *presupposing* a criterion of responsibility — the power to do either of two opposite actions after deliberation — that the Stoics explicitly rejected. Alexander's argument succeeds only if one already accepts his non-Stoic framework. |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 88–89)** | "He bases his argument on the presumption that 'what is in our power' (*to eph' hēmin*) is that which enables a man to do either of two opposite actions in a given circumstance and on the firm belief that such a rational control of action is only possible by means of deliberation. He knows that this is not what the Stoics mean by *to eph' hēmin* and that neither of his presumptions is part of the Stoic analysis of responsible action. Yet they form the standard against which the Stoics are criticized." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 89)** | "This is not a reliable report [Alexander's version of Stoic doctrine], for there is no distinction between man and beast in the 'Stoic' doctrine according to Alexander." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 90)** | "The omission of [deliberation] is the flaw in the Stoic theory, he [Alexander] says. He may well have believed that to all intents and purposes the Stoic theory of human action reduced man to the level of an animal. But he did not want animal action differently, so he presented a distorted picture of their doctrine which assumes what he should be trying to prove." |
| **Ancient locus** | Alexander *De Fato* XIV (183.27), pp. 180.5–7, 180.26–8 Bruns (cited as agei, hepomenoi, endidontes, p. 88); *De Fato* XXIV–XXVI (Stoic source on human/animal distinction, pp. 89–90, n. 233 = *NA* 19.1.14–20); SVF 3.437 (*De Fato* "other animals yield…", p. 89) |
| **Scholar mentioned** | Nemesius: Inwood argues Nemesius commits the same distortion as Alexander but "takes the low road of polemical misrepresentation" (p. 88), whereas Alexander's debate is "much more sophisticated and clear" (p. 88). Both are seen as dishonestly assimilating Stoic human assent to animal "yielding." |

## 5. Claim: The distinction animal "yielding" (*eixis*) vs. human "assent" (*synkatathesis*) is load-bearing for moral responsibility

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 74–80, 85–87 |
| **Inwood's position** | The Stoics drew a strict distinction between the automatic, non-rational animal response to presentations ("yielding") and the rational human act of assent, which alone makes action morally evaluable. Animals and pre-rational children cannot assent because they lack *lekta* (propositional content). Critics (Academic sceptics, Carneades, Nemesius, Alexander) all exploited the blurring of this distinction. |
| **Verbatim anchor (pp. 76–77), citing Sandbach** | "All animals are impelled to action by a movement in their *psyche* called a *hormê*. In a brute beast this follows directly upon the stimulus of a presentation (to invent an instance, a dog that scents a hare immediately wants to chase it). But in a human being the impulse does not exist without a mental act of assent. (A man who sees a hare does not immediately desire to chase it: he must first entertain and assent to the presentation 'that hare is something to be chased'.)" [Inwood presents this as "the distinction's significance may be best summed up by quoting the apt illustration of Sandbach"] |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 80)** | "Reason is in a position to control this automatic process, since a man can in principle deny his assent to any presentation which comes along. Since he is able to block this process, he is held responsible for its occurrence whenever he chooses not to interfere with it." |
| **Ancient locus** | D.L. 7.49, 7.94 (p. 76); Origen *De Princ.* III.1.2–4 (quoted in extenso pp. 77–79); Clement *Strom.* (PHP 4.4.32 = SVF 3.476, p. 83); Seneca *De Ira* 1.3.4–8 (p. 72) |
| **Contested** | Antiochus of Ascalon: Inwood argues Antiochus "gives up the principal point of Zeno's sharp distinction between the human and the animal" and offers a "clumsy response" to Academic scepticism (p. 87). Arcesilaus/Carneades: reported as denying assent is needed for action; Inwood reconstructs the Stoic replies. |

## 6. Claim: Assent is reactive, not fully autonomous — the control is negative (withholding)

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 80–83 |
| **Inwood's position** | "The control over actions which assent exercises is, therefore, a negative thing" (p. 80, verbatim). An agent is responsible not because he initiates a presentation from nothing, but because he has the capacity to refuse or delay assent. The Stoics acknowledged we cannot control which presentations occur (they come from outside), only our response to them. |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 80)** | "The control over actions which assent exercises is, therefore, a negative thing. If an agent is capable of refusing his assent and does not do so, he is still deemed to have given his assent." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 83)** | "Reason is thus a kind of gatekeeper of the presentations." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 83), citing Origen** | "'Therefore the argument shows that externals [i.e. the presentations which stimulate us to action] are not in our power, but it is our own job to take reason as a judge (*kritēn*) and examiner of how we should react to these externals and so *use them* in this way or the opposite way.'" [Inwood flags this as Origen's formulation, used as evidence for the older Stoic position] |
| **Ancient locus** | Origen *De Princ.* III.1.3 (SVF 3.386 / PHP 4.4.24–26, p. 83); Epictetus *Diss.* 2.6.6–10 (p. 83, n. 203); implicit: Chrysippus' doctrine that even presentations from memory are treated as if from outside (p. 80) |
| **Observation on limits** | Inwood notes that the Stoic position "that we cannot control our presentations, that they are simply given to us by the environment" is "a serious problem in the Stoic theory" (p. 97) — he flags it critically, but treats it as the consistent Chrysippean doctrine. |

## 7. Claim: No "divided self" — the Stoic soul is a rational unity, impulse obeys reason

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 4–5, 62–63, 96, 99–100 |
| **Inwood's position** | Unlike Aristotle, the Stoics introduced reason into action without positing a competing irrational power. The soul functions as a whole (*hêgemonikon*): impulse is not reason opposed by desire, but reason commanding itself. This is why the Stoics denied akrasia in the Aristotelian sense. |
| **Verbatim anchor (pp. 4–5)** | "They found a way to introduce reason into the functioning of the animal soul without introducing the kind of psychological dualism Aristotle seems to need and want in his theory. There is no trace of a 'divided self' in Stoic psychology. When the rational soul functioned in producing an action, it functioned as a unity." |
| **Verbatim anchor (pp. 62–63)** | "Chrysippus seems to have defined an impulse as man's reason commanding him to act … I propose the following hypothesis … assent to a hormetic proposition is extensionally equivalent to obedience to a self-given imperative." |
| **Ancient locus** | Arius Didymus *Ecl.* 2.88 (Stobaeus, SVF 2.975, p. 62); Chrysippus SVF 2.298a (Herculaneum papyrus *Logical Investigations*, pp. 64–65); Galen *PHP* 4.6.1 = SVF 3.473 (p. 100 n. 152) |

## 8. Claim: The Stoic "freedom" of the wise man is not indeterminist — it is harmony with fate

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 108–110 |
| **Inwood's position** | The Stoic sage is called "free" not because he has libertarian freedom of indifference, but because his character is perfectly aligned with Right Reason / Zeus' will, so that nothing external ever frustrates him. This is "a paradoxical idea of freedom" (p. 109, not-verbatim), compatible with determinism. |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 109)** | "Freedom in the indeterministic sense of Alexander or perhaps Aristotle cannot find a place in Stoicism. But the smooth flow of life is the freedom which the wise man alone enjoys. It is the inevitable result of a complete consistency with himself and with the will of Zeus." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 109), citing Seneca** | Inwood attributes to the early Stoa via Seneca: "'we are born in a kingdom — freedom is to obey god.'" [Not-verbatim in the PDF — presented as Seneca's formulation of an old Stoic belief, n. 38] |
| **Ancient locus** | Seneca *Epistulae* (n. 38, not page-specified in the text, contextually *Ep.* 77 or similar); D.L. 7.130 (on the logikos life); Chrysippus SVF 2.1000 (p. 67) |

## 9. Claim: Implicit assent — every responsible action involves assent, even when unconscious

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 81–83, 100 |
| **Inwood's position** | Because the Stoics were nominalists who rejected unactualized possibilities, they needed a real event in the material soul to ground responsibility for each action. They postulated implicit assent even when the agent is not consciously aware of it. This is "high-handed" (p. 82) but is consistent with their materialism and Socratic intellectualism. |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 82)** | "In claiming that no action is made without an act of assent, the Stoics are making a similar move [to Plato's postulation of hidden desires]. They have postulated certain events in the soul of an agent to explain his action … implicit assent is all that the Stoics thought they needed to hold a man responsible for his actions." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 100)** | "any action or response to the environment, whether overt or internal, is a human, rational, responsible action if and only if it follows on assent, at least an implicit assent." |
| **Ancient locus** | Origen *De Princ.* III.1.4 (p. 80, conscious/unconscious assent); Plutarch *St. Rep.* 1057ab (pp. 84–85, god and the sage and false presentations) |

## 10. Claim: The Stoic theory does NOT contain a "will" as a separate faculty

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | p. 96 |
| **Inwood's position** | Inwood explicitly agrees with Long that there is no Kantian/existentialist "will" as a distinct faculty in Stoic psychology. He notes that Kenny's claim that Aristotle has "a theory of will" can be extended to the Stoics only in a thin sense: they have an analysis of phenomena to which "will" terms are applied, but this is not the same as a faculty of will. Labels like "will" should not be imported uncritically. |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 96)** | "As Long says, there is in Stoicism no traditional Kantian or existentialist 'will' as a distinct power and faculty in the human soul; and we ought to feel uncomfortable with attempts to identify anticipations of such notions in the Stoic theory. Still, in the same sense in which Kenny claims that it is correct to say that Aristotle has a 'theory of the will', the Stoics may be said to have a theory of will or intention." |
| **Scholar alignment** | Agrees with Long (A. A. Long, "Early Stoic Concept" and *Hellenistic Philosophy*) and with Kenny (*Action, Emotion and Will*, p. 55) — but uses both in a limiting sense. |

## 11. On Origen as witness to Stoic doctrine

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 72–73, 77–79, 83 |
| **Inwood's position** | Origen's *De Principiis* III.1.2–4 is treated as a reliable witness to Chrysippean Stoic doctrine on the human/animal distinction. Inwood quotes it at length as "the firm distinction between men who assent and animals who do not" (p. 77). He does NOT treat Origen as an independent libertarian thinker; rather, Origen "in all essentials … [holds] that of the Stoa" on responsibility (p. 79). |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 79)** | "Despite this bold front, there are still problems in Origen's position, which in all essentials is that of the Stoa. Like the Stoics, Origen does not here consider the implications for our ascriptions of praise and blame of the fact that the character which determines our actions is itself caused by factors beyond our control." |
| **Key observation** | Inwood uses Origen primarily as a doxographic source for early Stoic doctrine, not as an original philosopher on free will. This is a significant framing choice: Origen is a witness, not a protagonist. |
| **Ancient locus** | Origen *De Princ.* III.1.2–4 (Ecl. 2.93 and D.L. 7.115, n. 155; SVF 3.386, n. 187) |

## 12. On the Compatibilism of Chrysippus — and its ambiguity

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Page** | pp. 66–70 |
| **Inwood's position** | Chrysippus' compatibilism, as Inwood reconstructs it (following Sorabji), works by treating assent as an *internal* cause exempt from the chain of external causes. But Inwood himself flags a deep ambiguity: Chrysippus either (a) backs off the claim that ALL things are fated, or (b) saves his position "at the price of an ambiguity which conceals the disunity of his picture of the power of fate" (p. 69, not-verbatim). |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 69)** | "It is easier to imagine him saving his position at the price of an ambiguity which conceals the disunity of his picture of the power of fate than to suppose that he did so by acknowledging in so many words that fate does not govern all things." |
| **Verbatim anchor (p. 69)** | "Although there is a single chain of causes, which justifies Chrysippus' claim that all things happen by fate, moral responsibility is only retained by treating these causes as though they were not on all fours with each other. In this sense at least there is an ambiguity in Chrysippus' use of the idea of cause." |
| **Ancient locus** | Chrysippus SVF 2.1000 (p. 67); Stough/Sorabji reading of the "perfect cause" doctrine (pp. 68–69); Gellius *NA* 7.2 implied (cylinder passage) |

## Agreements and Disagreements with Named Scholars

| Scholar | Inwood's stance |
|---|---|
| **A. A. Long** | Agrees: no faculty-"will" in Stoicism; logical analysis serves ethics; the sage "completely determines his choice" (p. 108, n. 30). Follows Long on *hormê autotelês* emendation question (n. 76). |
| **Charlotte Stough** | Accepts as "the best recent account" of the compatibilism argument (p. 66, n. 131). |
| **Richard Sorabji** | Accepts Sorabji's "third interpretation" of Chrysippean compatibilism (assent is internal; external necessitation alone is the criterion) — but modifies it: "Sorabji's version needs some slight modifications" (p. 68). Disagrees on the Aristotelian parallel for *archê* (p. 66). |
| **F. H. Sandbach** | Follows on the *yielding/assent* distinction (p. 76) and on cataleptic presentations (p. 75: "Sandbach's cautious estimation is best"). |
| **A. C. Kenny** | Follows in a limiting sense: "will" applies to Stoics only as "a theory of phenomena to which 'will' terms are applied" (p. 96). |
| **Antiochus of Ascalon** | Criticised for blurring the human/animal assent distinction — "a clumsy response" (p. 87). |
| **Arcesilaus / Carneades** | Discussed as opponents of the Stoic apraxia argument; Carneades' move (p. 87: response to a persuasive hormetic presentation without yielding or assent) is reported, not endorsed or contested by Inwood. |
| **Nemesius** | Criticised as distorting Stoic doctrine by treating human and animal action identically — "the low road of polemical misrepresentation" (p. 88). |
| **Alexander of Aphrodisias** | Sophisticated but dishonest: "presented a distorted picture of their doctrine which assumes what he should be trying to prove" (p. 90). Alexander's deliberation-criterion for rational action is a non-Stoic import. |
| **Bonhoeffer** | Followed on passions / *eupatheiai* (p. 80, n. 80; pp. 291–305 notes). |
| **J. M. Rist** | Thanked in acknowledgements; disagreement on pathê noted (n. 227: "I am also grateful to Michael Frede for discussions of his view of pathê, which differs from my own"). |
| **Michael Frede** | A disagreement on *pathê* is flagged (n. 227) but not developed in the body of the text. No substantive engagement with Frede's free-will thesis (which appeared the same year or later). |
| **Voelke (1973)** | Cited several times in notes (e.g. nn. 114, 139); not contested directly in the main text read. |

## Flags: Where the Debate Map (g5/debate_map.md) May Misrepresent Inwood

### Flag 1 — Page citations in Debate A are partially wrong
The debate map attributes to Inwood "pp. 66–70, 88–89" for the Cleanthes Hymn and *eph' hēmin* claims. From the actual PDF:
- The Cleanthes Hymn discussion is **pp. 69–71** (not simply 66–70).
- The Alexander *eph' hēmin* discussion is **pp. 88–91** (ending at p. 91, not 88–89).
- The assent-grounds-responsibility claim is dispersed pp. 66–83.
Minor point; the page ranges are approximate but not badly wrong.

### Flag 2 — "Assent grounds responsibility, not a power to do otherwise" is correct but incomplete
The map entry states Inwood's claim as: "Stoic *eph' hēmin* ≠ Alexander's power 'to do either of two opposite actions'." This is accurate, but the map does not capture that Inwood does not directly discuss the semantics of *eph' hēmin* as a Greek phrase (he does not analyse the one-sided/causative vs. two-sided/potestative distinction that is central to Bobzien 1998). Inwood's argument is about the criterion of responsibility (assent vs. deliberate choice between opposites), not about the etymological valence of the phrase. **No misrepresentation, but a gap**: the map associates Inwood with the Bobzien-style *eph' hēmin* argument he does not himself make.

### Flag 3 — The map credits Inwood with citing "Alexander *De Fato* 180.5–7 Bruns" as the contested locus
Confirmed: the text at p. 88 does mention "*agei, hepomenoi, endidontes*, p. 180.5–7" (the Stoic-flavoured text Alexander cites). This citation is accurate.

### Flag 4 — Origen's role
The map does not mention that Inwood uses Origen as a primary *Stoic doxographic source*, not as a philosopher of free will. Any reading of the debate map that treats Inwood's use of Origen as engagement with Origen's own position would be misleading. Inwood consistently deploys Origen's *De Princ.* III.1 as evidence for what the early Stoics said, not for what Origen contributed.

### Flag 5 — "Explicitly rejects the 'free will' reading of Cleanthes' Hymn" — CONFIRMED
The map's summary is accurate: Inwood explicitly calls this reading "wrong" (p. 70, verbatim above). No misrepresentation.

### Flag 6 — No engagement with Bobzien or Frede
The debate map places Inwood in a cluster with "Bobzien ≈ Inwood ≈ Brennan ≈ Salles" on the one-sided causative *eph' hēmin*. Since Inwood (1985) predates Bobzien (1998/2001) and Brennan (2005), he cannot be aligned with those scholars based on his own text. The convergence is retrospective. This is not a misrepresentation — the map is mapping positions, not influence — but it should be noted that Inwood does not use the terms "one-sided causative" or "causative vs. potestative" at all; those terminological distinctions are Bobzien's later contribution.
