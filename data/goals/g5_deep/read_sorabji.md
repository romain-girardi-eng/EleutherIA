## 0. CRITICAL FLAG — quick-pass map misrepresents this scholar/work

`data/goals/g5/debate_map.md` (line 37, 45) attributes to **"Sorabji 2017"** the thesis that *"the will has four strands (rationality, freedom, power, perversion by pride), assembled only in Augustine; none of the four as a cluster is in Epictetus → Frede is wrong"*, and tags it as the relevant Sorabji position for debate B.

That **four-strands-of-the-will** thesis is **NOT in *Necessity, Cause and Blame* (1980)** — the work named for this deep-read. It is a *different and much later* Sorabji argument (developed in *Emotion and Peace of Mind*, Oxford 2000, and esp. "The Concept of the Will from Plato to Maximus the Confessor," in T. Pink & M.W.F. Stone eds., *The Will and Human Action*, 2004; cf. *Self*, 2006). Confirmation from the 1980 text itself:

- Full-text search of the 1980 book finds **no "strand(s)" of the will**, no "four strands", no *boulēsis*, and **no concept of "the will" as a faculty at all**. The single occurrence of "strand" is unrelated ("one strand in Aristotle's thought", p. ~ch. on equity).
- In 1980 Sorabji works **entirely** in Aristotle's own action-vocabulary — *to eph' hēmin* (up to us), *to hekousion* (the voluntary), *prohairesis* (deliberate choice), *archē* (origin) — and **deliberately avoids the modern "will"**. There is no genealogy of "the will" here.
- "Free will" appears in 1980 only as a label Sorabji **reports** others using (Ross "comes closest, though he does not go all the way, towards postulating free will," p. 26 n.1 / p. 229; Furley on the Epicurean swerve "which guarantees free will," p. 19 n.57; index entry "Free will and determinism"; bibliographic ref to Huby, "The first discovery of the free will problem," 1967). Sorabji does **not** in 1980 himself assert an "invention/discovery of the will."

## HEADLINE THESIS

Aristotle is an **indeterminist** (Sorabji's term) who held that human actions are **caused but not necessitated**: they need not have been "necessary all along," yet need involve **no break in causation**. Against the scholarly orthodoxy (Loening, Ross, Gauthier, Furley, Huby, Dodds, et al.), Sorabji argues that Aristotle was **not** ignorant of the determinism-vs-responsibility problem: he saw the clash and treated it as telling **against** determinism, not against voluntariness. The genuinely Hellenistic novelty was not the *invention* of determinism or the *discovery* of the clash, but the Stoics'/Diodorus' **persistence** in endorsing determinism after the clash was already widely recognised — which forced the new "soft"-determinist reply. Throughout, Sorabji himself rejects all the arguments for determinism and rejects the claim that determinism is compatible with moral responsibility.

## CLAIM 3 — The wedge: "caused" ≠ "necessitated" (the book's load-bearing philosophical move)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | ch. 1 (p. 4), ch. 2 (pp. 26–27); applied to action at ch. 14 (pp. 228–233) |
| **Verbatim anchor (Eng.)** | "Actions may be caused yet not necessitated, coincidences necessitated yet not caused." (p. xi, Introduction) — and ch. 2 opening: "some things may be caused without being necessitated … if true, [this] free[s] us from one of the determinist's most ancient and powerful arguments — the argument that if events are not necessitated, they must be uncaused, inexplicable, and hence mysterious." (pp. 26–27) |
| **Ancient locus** | *Metaph.* VI 2–3 (1027a7–8: accidents have only accidental causes; 1027a29–b14); *De Int.* 9; *GC* I 11; *NE* III 5 (1114b3–25). Sorabji notes Aristotle himself *wavers*: in *Metaph.* VI 3 he (uncharacteristically) implies what is caused **is** necessitated, the "uncharacteristic thesis" Sorabji says is "quietly forgotten in the ethical discussions" (p. 248). |

## CLAIM 5 — The Stoic *eph' hēmin* ("up to us") move, and its modern echo (Schlick)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | p. 86 (item (iii) of the 5 positions in the ancient debate) |
| **Verbatim anchor (Eng.)** | "The most important moral concept is that of an action's being up to us (eph' hēmin). It is claimed that an action is up to us, if it is in accordance with our impulse (hormē), and occurs through us (di' hēmōn). ## CLAIM 7 — Aristotelian action is **caused but not necessitated**; "fresh starts" (Ross/Furley) means *failure of necessitation*, NOT *break in causation*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | ch. 14, pp. 227–233 (esp. 229–232) |
| **Verbatim anchor (Eng.)** | "in calling deliberation and deliberate action an archē, Aristotle is simply claiming that they influence events. There is no sign that he thinks they are 'fresh starts', if that means that they are uncaused." (p. 229) — and the worked case: "The child's action, as just described, is ideally suited to supply what he wants. It is caused, without being necessitated. The cause is an internal one (the child's feelings), though the causal antecedents can stretch back indefinitely far." (p. 232) |
| **Ancient locus** | *De Int.* 9 (18b26–19a22, *archē* of the sea battle at 19a7); *Phys.* VIII 5–6 (258a1–2, self-mover; 258b–259b, animals not *kuriōs* self-movers); *NE* III 5, 1114b3–25 (character & responsibility, *sunaitioi pōs* 1114b23); *NE* VI 2 (1139a31–3, deliberation→prohairesis→action chain). *archē* is **not** reserved for the first member of a causal chain (*An.Post.* I 13). |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Contests W.D. Ross** ("fresh starts"; "comes closest, though does not go all the way, towards postulating free will," *Aristotle* 1923/1959:196) and **David Furley** (*Two Studies in the Greek Atomists*, 1967:184–95, 232–5: internal origin = *uncaused* cause traceable no further back than the agent). Sorabji's counter: an internal *archē* can sit in a chain reaching back to external antecedents (parents, upbringing, law); what makes inborn-character involuntary is not unbroken causation but that it leaves **no stage at which it was "up to the man" (ep' autōi)** what character he had (pp. 230–231). Also engages Hardie 1968, Gauthier, A.A. Long 1971. |

| **Ancient locus** | *NE* III 5, 1113b7–8 (dual possibility); III 5, 1113b20–1, 1114a18–19; *EE* II 6, 1223a2–7; II 9, 1225b8; II 10, 1226b31–3; *NE* V 8, 1135a24 (voluntary = "up to him"); *NE* VI 4, 1140a10–16. ## CLAIM 9 — Against the orthodoxy of Aristotle's "nescience": Aristotle DID see the clash; the Hellenistic novelty was *persistence*, not discovery

| | |
|---|---|
| **Page** | ch. 15, pp. 243–248 (esp. 244–245, 247) |
| **Verbatim anchor (Eng.)** | "Aristotle himself, so far from failing to observe any incompatibility between determinism and our ordinary ways of thinking about conduct, actually tended to see such incompatibilities too readily." (p. 245) — and: "there was a new development, but … it was not the invention of determinism, nor the realisation that determinism clashed with ethical presuppositions. | **Ancient locus** | *De Int.* 9, 18b31–3, 19a7–8 (efficacy of deliberation; ancestor of the **Lazy Argument**); *NE* III 5, 1113b21–30 (punishment/honours → conduct is up to us); *NE* III 5, 1114a23–9, *EE* II 6 1223a10, II 11 1228a5 (no praise/blame for the necessary). The Lazy Argument's later career: Cicero *De Fato* XII 28–XIII 30; Ps.-Plutarch *De Fato* 574e; Alexander *De Fato* 16; **Origen** *Contra Celsum* II 20; Eusebius *PE* VI; Chrysostom; Firmicus Maternus — "Many of these texts are collected by David Amand, *Fatalisme et Liberté dans l'Antiquité Grecque*, Louvain 1945." |
| **Agrees / contests** | **Contests the "composite" orthodoxy** explicitly attributed (p. 243 n.1) to **Ross, Gauthier, Pamela Huby ("The first discovery of the free will problem," 1967), Furley, Adkins, Dodds, D.J. Allan, Tannery, Dover, Pohlenz, Gould, Loening** — i.e. the view that Aristotle was unaware of the determinism/responsibility problem because universal determinism had not yet been propounded. Sorabji's method-point: Aristotle's **dialectic** (starting from *endoxa*, *Top.* I 1 100a29–b23) makes the voluntariness of some actions a near-unchallengeable *endoxon*, so for him the clash counts against determinism (pp. 246–247). |

## NET BEARING ON THE DEBATES

- **Debate B ("invention of the will" / free will):** This 1980 work is a *counterweight* to "invention/discovery of the will/free will" narratives — but only **partially and on its own terms**. Sorabji here (i) **declines** the category "the will" entirely, doing the work with *eph' hēmin*/*hekousion*/*prohairesis*; (ii) **rejects** Huby's "first discovery of the free will problem" (Epicurus) and the broader nescience thesis, locating awareness of the determinism/responsibility clash already in Aristotle; (iii) treats "free will" only as others' label. NB: this is the *early* Sorabji. His **later** work (2000/2004) is where he affirmatively analyses the *concept of the will* and its "strands" assembled by Augustine — a position the quick-pass map wrongly back-projected onto the 1980 book.
- **Debate A (Aristotle on responsibility):** Sorabji's distinctive, citable contributions are (a) caused-but-not-necessitated as the key to Aristotelian voluntariness; (b) "fresh starts" = failure of necessitation, not rupture of causation (vs Ross/Furley); (c) Aristotle saw the determinism clash and weaponised it against determinism (vs the Ross–Gauthier–Huby–Furley–Dodds orthodoxy).
