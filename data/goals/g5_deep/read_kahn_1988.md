# Position Dossier — Kahn 1988, "Discovering the Will: From Aristotle to Augustine"

**Full citation:** Charles H. Kahn, "Discovering the Will: From Aristotle to Augustine," in *The Question of 'Eclecticism': Studies in Later Greek Philosophy*, ed. J. M. Dillon and A. A. Long (Berkeley/Los Angeles: University of California Press, 1988), ch. 9, pp. 235–259.

**Debate targeted:** Debate B (origins of the notion of (free) will — who invents it, when, in what tradition).

**Integrity notes:** All modern labels (free will, libertarian, volition, invention) are Kahn's own analytical vocabulary or his attribution of others' views; they are recorded here as attributed, not endorsed as historical facts. Verbatim quotes are transcribed from the PDF images. Where page numbers are legible in the scan they are cited as "p. N" referring to the printed page numbers in the article (235–259). The article is a contribution to a 1988 edited volume; the research was carried out 1979–1980 and presented at Oxford in June 1980; Kahn acknowledges that Dihle's book (1982) allowed him to finalize his conclusions (p. 235, footnote block).

## CLAIM 1 — Framing thesis: there is no single concept "the will"; the emergence of the will is a labyrinth of four distinct problems

**Page:** 235  
**Verbatim anchor (English — article written in English):**  
> "Unfortunately, there is no *single* concept designated by *the will* in modern usage. Hence the historical problem of the emergence of the will is not a single problem, but a labyrinth of problems where different threads lead in different directions."

**Kahn's four perspectives (p. 235):**
1. The theological concept — begins with Augustine, culminates in Aquinas and the medieval voluntarists; the will of God precedes and structures the human will.
2. The post-Cartesian concept — *volition* as an inner mental event or act of consciousness that is cause or condition for outer bodily action.
3. The Kantian/post-Kantian concept — the will as self-legislation, the noumenal self; Schopenhauer, Nietzsche.
4. The special topic of free will versus determinism — cuts across all three, traceable to Aristotle and Epicurus, but: "the question debated by Luther and Erasmus, whether the will is slave or free, was originally discussed by Greek philosophers without any reference to the will at all" (p. 236).

**Ancient loci:** None attached to this framing claim; it is a meta-historiographical argument.  
**Who Kahn agrees/contests:** He contests the assumption shared (in different ways) by Kenny, Voelke, Dihle, and (implicitly) anyone who treats "the will" as a single referent for historical inquiry. He states that "the authors of these two books [Voelke 1973; Kenny 1979] would probably accept the view that the ancients did not have 'our' concept of the will" (p. 235).

## CLAIM 2 — Aristotle lacks a concept of will: he has four distinct, independent notions that no single concept unifies

**Page:** 239–242  
**Verbatim anchor:**  
> "To say that Aristotle lacks a concept of will is to say, first of all, that these four notions (or at least the last three) are conceptually independent of one another: there is no *one* concept that ties together the voluntary, *boulēsis* or desire for the end, and *prohairesis*, deliberate desire for the means. But it is precisely the role of *voluntas* in Aquinas to perform this work of conceptual unification." (p. 241)

**Kahn's four Aristotelian notions (pp. 240–241):**
1. *to eph' hēmin* — what is up to us, in our power to do or not to do.
2. *hekousion* — the voluntary: done spontaneously, intentionally, neither in ignorance nor under compulsion.
3. *prohairesis* — deliberate rational choice; determines means to an end; narrower than the voluntary.
4. *boulēsis* — rational desire for the end (the good or happiness); part of the desiderative faculty but intrinsically rational.

**Ancient loci cited:**  
- Aristotle *NE* 6.2.1139b4 (cited at p. 241): *prohairesis* is "desiderative reason or rational desire, and it is as such a principle that the human being is the source and origin [*arche*] of his actions."
- Aristotle *NE* 3.2.1111b26–29; 3.5.1113b3–4 (footnote 4, p. 241).
- Aristotle *Eud. Ethics* 2.7.1223b29ff. (footnote 7, p. 242): Aristotle explicitly rejects voluntary action as reducible to *boulēsis*.

**Who Kahn agrees/contests:** Kahn agrees with Dihle that Aristotle lacks the concept of will, but for different and more precise reasons (the conceptual disconnection of the four notions). He disagrees with the inference drawn by Irwin/Kenny (not named here but implicit) that *boulēsis* already constitutes a will-equivalent: for Kahn, the disconnection of *boulēsis* from *prohairesis* and from the *hekousion* is precisely what shows the gap.

## CLAIM 3 — The first major innovation toward the will is Chrysippus's theory of assent (*sunkatathesis*)

**Page:** 246–248  
**Verbatim anchor:**  
> "The first major innovation is the Stoic theory of action as worked out by Chrysippus in the third century B.C., in which the notion of consent or assent, *sunkatathesis*, plays a decisive role." (p. 246)

And, on the structural role of *sunkatathesis*:  
> "For *sunkatathesis* in the Stoic theory of human action plays exactly the same role that *consensus* and 'the command of the will' play for St. Thomas." (p. 247)

**Kahn's argument:** Stoic *sunkatathesis* is not a free act in the Cartesian (mental substance) sense because Stoics are materialists and assent is a change in the tension of *soul-pneuma* in the heart. But once taken over into Neoplatonic and Christian frameworks (the soul as immaterial entity), Chrysippus's assent-doctrine "will become the focal point of the concept of volition or 'willing' that we find in Augustine, Aquinas, and Descartes" (p. 247). The Stoic contribution is structural, not terminological.

**Ancient loci cited:**  
- *SVF* 2.73, 980, 3.169 (footnote 9, p. 247); for the distinction between human and animal action (assent intervenes in humans): *SVF* 2.988 (p. 288, lines 1–26), cited via Inwood.
- Epictetus *Handbook* opening: *eph' hēmin* passage quoted (pp. 253–254) — Kahn does not give a chapter ref for the *Handbook* text but cites Discourses 3.5.7; 1.22.10; 1.12.9; 1.1.23 (p. 252) for *prohairesis*.

**Who Kahn agrees/contests:** Kahn cites Inwood 1985 as "the fullest and clearest exposition known to me" (footnote 9, p. 247) and endorses Inwood's account of the Stoic psychology of action. He agrees that *sunkatathesis* grounds freedom and responsibility in the Stoic system, but treats this as a structural anticipation of *voluntas*, not as a free will in any later sense.

## CLAIM 4 — The second landmark is the Latin translation of Greek philosophy; *voluntas*/*libera voluntas* in Lucretius is the earliest attested phrase "free will"

**Page:** 248–251  
**Verbatim anchor:**  
> "The best example of this is also the earliest occurrence of *voluntas* in philosophical Latin, in the famous discussion of the swerve of soul atoms in Lucretius Book 2. There are two very striking features of this text: (2a) It focuses on the term *voluntas*, which appears four times in forty-three lines, while there is no trace of any corresponding term in the relevant texts either of Epicurus or of any Greek Epicurean, and (2b) *voluntas* is described as *libera*, 'free,' in an apparent anticipation of the modern phrase 'free will,' whereas most ancient discussions of moral freedom, both in Greek and in Latin, do not present the will as the direct subject of freedom." (p. 248)

**Kahn's argument:** *Voluntas* carries different semantic connotations from Greek *boulēsis*: in pre-philosophical Latin *voluntate sua* = spontaneously, of one's own accord; *voluntarii* = volunteers (military). Cicero's rendering of *hekousion* as *voluntarium* therefore semantically links the voluntary and the will in a way that has "no parallel" in Greek. This is a genuine Latin-language contribution to the emergence of the will-concept, not a sign of Roman psychological distinctiveness (contra Pohlenz). The link between freedom (*libertas*) and will (*voluntas*) becomes "fixed as the standard formula for the human power of decision in virtue of which we are responsible for our actions" only in Latin and above all in Augustine (p. 251).

**Ancient loci cited:**  
- Lucretius *DRN* 2 (swerve passage), with *voluntas* four times in 43 lines (p. 248); Kahn reads *voluntas* for *volvptas*, following "the usual correction" (footnote 16, p. 249).
- Epicurus *Letter to Menoeceus* = Diogenes Laertius 10.133: *to par' hemas adespoton* ("what is up to us, subject to no master") (p. 249).
- Epicurus, papyrus fragments (*On Nature*): *di' hemon auton ten aitian onomazontes* / *to ex hemon auton pos prattomenon* (cited from David Sedley's edition, footnote 17, p. 249).
- *SVF* 2.984, 998, 999, 1007 (footnote 18, p. 249): Chrysippus used the same *to eph' hēmin* terminology.
- Cicero *Tusc.* 4.6.12 (footnote 5, p. 242): *voluntas* as standard Latin rendering of *boulēsis*, cited via Voelke.
- Plotinus *Ennead* 6.8.4–6: *eph' hēmin*, *to autexousion*, *to eleutheron* used as "roughly interchangeable" (p. 251, footnote 24).
- Tertullian *De anima* 21.6: translates *autexousion* as *libera arbitrii potestas* (p. 251, footnote 23).

**Who Kahn agrees/contests:**  
- Contests Pohlenz's view that the will was "essentially a Roman invention" reflecting Roman national character (p. 248, footnote 13 = Pohlenz vol. 1, 274, 319): Kahn attributes this rather to "peculiarities of the Latin language."  
- Agrees with Gauthier that Cicero's rendering of *hekousios* as *voluntarius* was a "mistake" with philosophical consequences, though Kahn is more measured ("maladresse") — see p. 248.  
- Notes Dihle's characterization of "a lack of psychological refinement in the Latin vocabulary" (p. 248, footnote 15 = Dihle, 133) without fully endorsing the value judgment.  
- On *autexousion*: Kahn notes it appears in Epictetus and Plotinus but "just when this term was introduced, I do not know" (p. 251, footnote 21); he notes Hippolytus (SVF 2.975) ascribes it to Zeno and Chrysippus but "that surely reflects the terminology of the later doxographer." Kahn does not commit to a chronology for *autexousion* — an explicit epistemic caveat.

## CLAIM 5 — Epictetus's *prohairesis* is a "fundamentally new idea," not merely the Aristotelian term it borrows; it is Stoic *voluntas* in Greek dress

**Page:** 252–255  
**Verbatim anchor:**  
> "It seems clear that Epictetus has used this rather old-fashioned term to express a fundamentally new idea, much the same idea that Seneca had recently expressed by *voluntas*." (p. 252)

And on *prohairesis* as true self:  
> "Traditional Greek terminology offers no appropriate term for this intense preoccupation with the inner life, the late Stoic parallel to a Cartesian *cogito* or focus on consciousness. So Epictetus takes an old word and fills it with his personal meaning. Perhaps if he wrote Latin he would have used *voluntas*, as Seneca did." (pp. 253–254)

**Kahn's argument:** Epictetus develops two equivalent notions: (a) *chresis ton phantasion* — rational use of impressions, a vivid phrase for the rational testing of impressions to see whether they deserve assent; (b) *prohairesis* — made central in a way it "apparently played no significant role in early Stoic theory" (p. 252). By identifying the true self (*to eph' hēmin*, what is "up to us") with *prohairesis*, Epictetus shifts the locus of personal identity from *nous* (reason) to moral commitment, and this shift is "momentous for the evolution of the idea of person and selfhood" (p. 253). The Stoic Sage's *prohairesis* is characterized as "a part of God which he has given to us" (*Discourses* 1.17.27) — a theological coloration that anticipates Augustine.

**Ancient loci cited (Epictetus, as cited by Kahn):**  
- *Discourses* 3.5.7; 1.22.10; 1.12.9; 1.1.23 (p. 252).
- *Handbook* opening (unnamed but quoted at p. 253–254): "Some things are in our power [*eph' hēmin*], some things are not…"
- *Discourses* 1.17.27: *prohairesis* as "a part of God which he has given to us" (p. 254).
- *Discourses* 4.7.20: "In every case, I want and prefer [*mallon thelo*] what God wants…. I simply share his will [*haplos sunthelo*]" (p. 254–255).
- *Discourses* 4.10.16: "There is nothing easier to manage than a human soul. What is needed is to will [*thelesai dei*]" (p. 255).

**Seneca parallel (p. 255):**  
- Seneca *Epistulae* 80.3–4: "Whatever can make you good is in your power. What do you need in order to be good? To will it [*velle*]." (p. 255)

**Who Kahn agrees/contests:**  
- Agrees with Dihle that Epictetus's *prohairesis* draws on the broader Hellenistic idiomatic use of the term for "character, attitude, conduct" (footnote 25, p. 252, crediting a letter from Dihle and Rist 1975 for useful observations).  
- Explicitly distances himself from Frede (not named in this article, since Frede 2011 postdates it): Kahn does not claim Epictetus has "a concept of free will"; he describes the innovation as one of inner identification and moral self-definition, using the language of *voluntas*-equivalence.  
- Contests Voelke implicitly by finding the Stoic voluntarist strand not in the early Stoa's *hormē/sunkatathesis* as a "will" per se, but only in the late Stoic convergence (Seneca/Epictetus).

## CLAIM 6 — Augustine synthesizes Stoic assent, Latin *voluntas*, Neoplatonic incorporeality, and Pauline divided-self into a genuinely new doctrine of will; but Dihle's exclusively theological account is one-sided

**Page:** 255–259  
**Verbatim anchor (critique of Dihle):**  
> "And so, at the end, we return to Dihle's thesis about the biblical and theological origins of the concept of will. That does not apply, however, to what we found in Chrysippus's theory of assent, in Lucretius's and Seneca's discussions of *voluntas*, or in Epictetus's doctrine of *prohairesis*." (p. 259)

**Verbatim anchor (Dihle acknowledged):**  
> "Dihle has documented in detail what we always suspected: that the concept of the will as we find it developed in Augustine and Aquinas presupposes biblical religious experience as one of its indispensable conditions. But there were other conditions as well." (p. 259)

**Kahn on Augustine's three sources (p. 255–259):**  
1. From Neoplatonism: the notion of a purely intelligible, noncorporeal domain of reality (contrasts with Stoic corporeal *pneuma*).
2. From St. Paul (*Romans* 7:15): the sense of the divided self — "I do not do the good I will [*thelo*], but I do the evil which I will not [*ou thelo*]" (p. 255). Kahn: "It was by meditation on these words of St. Paul that Augustine developed the notion of will that Kierkegaard found lacking in Socrates."
3. From the Stoic/Latin tradition: *sunkatathesis*, *voluntas*, and the link between willing and consenting.

**Ancient loci cited:**  
- Augustine *Confessions* 8.5 (quoted at length, pp. 256–257): *voluntas*, *libido*, *voluntas nova*, two wills in conflict.
- Augustine *Confessions* 8.8–8.9 (pp. 256–257): *mox ut vellem, possem* — "as soon as I had the will to do so, I should have willed it wholeheartedly"; *non ex toto vult* — "it does not fully will"; *non plena imperat*; "So there are two wills in us."
- Augustine *Confessions* 9.1: *nolle quod volebam, et velle quod volebas* — "All you asked of me was to deny my own will and accept yours" (p. 258).
- Augustine *De libero arbitrio* 2.1.1 and 2.1.3: *liberum arbitrium voluntatis* (footnote 20, p. 250).
- Kierkegaard, *The Sickness unto Death*, pt. 2, ch. 2 (footnote 27, p. 256): the passage about sin lying in the will, not the intellect.
- Aquinas *ST* Ia.IIae.15.4; Ia.IIae.77.3; I.77.5; I.77.8; I.81.3; I.82.1–2; I.83.3; I.83.4; I.84.4; Ia.IIae.8.2; Ia.IIae.24.3; Ia.IIae.77.6; Ia.IIae.6.5; Ia.IIae.6.7; Ia.IIae.17.7; Ia.IIae.9.1 (passim, pp. 241–245).

**Who Kahn agrees/contests:**  
- **Partially agrees with Dihle:** Biblical/theological tradition is "one of its indispensable conditions" for the Augustine/Aquinas concept. Kahn praises Dihle's book as "a rich treasury of scholarship and insight" (p. 237).  
- **Explicitly contests Dihle's account as one-sided and implicitly Hegelian:** "his deliberate alignment on the theological perspective gives Dihle's picture of the intellectual development a Hegelian, even a providential structure, as if the history of Greek thought from Plato to St. Paul, from Philo to Plotinus, amounted to the gradual accumulation of a set of problems to which Augustine's theory of the will was to offer the definitive solution" (p. 237). This is Kahn's sharpest explicit critique of Dihle.  
- **Agrees that Augustine "begins but does not complete" the systematic theory** (p. 237–238): "Augustine was a religious genius, but he was not a professionally trained philosopher: he had neither the inclination nor the technical equipment to formulate his conception of the will within the framework of a systematic theory of human action."  
- **On Gauthier:** Kahn neither endorses nor dismisses Gauthier's position that all traits of Augustine's will are "found earlier in the Stoics." He cites it as "making a serious point" while calling Gauthier's specific chronological conclusion ("eleven centuries after Aristotle") "eccentric" (p. 238).

## CLAIM 7 — The free-will-versus-determinism debate was conducted by Greek philosophers "without any reference to the will at all"

**Page:** 236  
**Verbatim anchor:**  
> "However, and this is paradoxical but nevertheless true, the question debated by Luther and Erasmus, whether the will is slave or free, was originally discussed by Greek philosophers without any reference to the will at all."

**Kahn's argument:** Perspective 4 (the free-will problem) "cuts across all three of the [other] traditions" and "can be clearly traced back to Aristotle and Epicurus" — but the Greek discussion did not frame it in terms of a *will* at all. This is Kahn's sharpest statement on Debate B's relationship to Debate A: what looks like a "free will" problem in antiquity was not framed as a will-problem by the ancients.

**Ancient loci:** None attached at this point; Kahn refers to the Luther/Erasmus debate as a later reception phenomenon.  
**Who Kahn agrees/contests:** This claim is compatible with Bobzien's later thesis (1998) that the free-will problem was "inadvertently constructed" from a combination of Aristotelian and Stoic elements; Kahn reaches an adjacent conclusion from a different route (terminological/conceptual analysis rather than Bobzien's genealogical reconstruction). Kahn does not mention Bobzien (she publishes after 1988).

## CLAIM 8 — *Autexousion* enters philosophical Greek as the technical term for "free will" via Epictetus and Plotinus; it lacks a fixed Greek antecedent and its introduction date is unknown to Kahn

**Page:** 251  
**Verbatim anchor:**  
> "When in Roman times, Greek philosophy developed a technical expression for free will that went beyond phrases like 'what is up to us,' the term most generally employed is *to autexousion*, which simply means 'what is in one's own power,' as in Cicero's *in nostra potestate*. Just when this term was introduced, I do not know."

**Kahn's observations:**  
- Found in Epictetus (five occurrences, citing Voelke, footnote 22, p. 251) and in Plotinus.  
- Tertullian (shortly after 200 CE) reports *autexousion* as a technical term and translates it as *libera arbitrii potestas* ("free power of decision," *De anima* 21.6; footnote 23, p. 251).  
- Hippolytus (*SVF* 2.975) ascribes it to Zeno and Chrysippus, "but that surely reflects the terminology of the later doxographer" (footnote 21, p. 251).  
- In Plotinus *Ennead* 6.8.4–6: *eph' hēmin*, *to autexousion*, *to eleutheron* are used as "roughly interchangeable" (footnote 24, p. 251).  
- The metaphor of freedom (*adespoton*, "subject to no master," in Epicurus) "seems never to have hardened into a technical expression in Greek" (p. 251).  
- "It seems that it was only in Latin, and above all in Augustine, that the terminology of freedom [*libertas*] became fixed as the standard formula for the human power of decision in virtue of which we are responsible for our actions" (p. 251).

**Ancient loci:**  
- Epicurus/Diogenes Laertius 10.133: *to par' hemas adespoton* (p. 249).  
- Epictetus: five occurrences of *autexousion* (Voelke, footnote 22).  
- Plotinus *Ennead* 6.8.4–6 (footnote 24).  
- Tertullian *De anima* 21.6 (footnote 23).

**Who Kahn agrees/contests:** Kahn's explicit agnosticism on the chronology of *autexousion* is a genuine epistemic caveat. He does not contest Eliasson (2008) or Frede (2011) — both postdate this article. His observation that Hippolytus retrojects the term onto Zeno/Chrysippus aligns with Bobzien's later skepticism about early Stoic *autexousion*. His finding that the freedom-terminology solidifies in Latin (Augustine) rather than in Greek partially aligns with Dihle, but his insistence that the Stoic strand is equally indispensable contests Dihle's purely biblical account.

## CLAIM 9 — Epictetus anticipates Augustine in spiritual attitude, but his conception remains man-centered, not God-centered; the theological inflection that makes Augustine decisive comes from the biblical tradition

**Page:** 254, 258–259  
**Verbatim anchor (on Epictetus's limit):**  
> "For even if Epictetus's conception does have some distinctly theological overtones, his basic notion of achieving moral invulnerability by restricting our concerns to what is in our power is essentially an ideal of rational autonomy that is man-centered rather than God-centered." (p. 259)

**Verbatim anchor (on why biblical tradition tips the balance):**  
> "But of course the God of Abraham and Moses, of Jesus and St. Paul, issues his commands to mankind in no uncertain terms. Hence it is in this tradition that we naturally find a view of human will (as distinct from reason or desire) emerging as an overall attitude of obedience or disobedience to the will of God on the part of the whole person. And it is this view which is first fully articulated by Augustine." (p. 259)

**Ancient loci:**  
- Epictetus *Discourses* 4.7.20: Epictetus's alignment with God's will in quasi-volitional terms (p. 254–255).
- Paul, *Romans* 7:15 (p. 255); Augustine *Confessions* 8.5, 8.8–8.9, 9.1 (pp. 256–258).

**Who Kahn agrees/contests:** This is Kahn's synthesis. He holds that both the Stoic pagan tradition AND the biblical tradition are "indispensable conditions" for the full concept of will in Augustine/Aquinas, against Dihle's one-sidedness (biblical only) and against what would become Frede's competing one-sidedness (Stoic/pagan only, Epictetus is decisive). Kahn's position is structurally intermediate: multi-causal, overdetermined.

## Quick-Pass Error Flags (debate_map.md cross-check)

The debate map entry for Kahn (Debate B row) reads:

> "Kahn 1988 | Intermediate / developmental | The will is 'discovered' on a trajectory Aristotle → Latin *voluntas* (Cicero/Lucretius/Seneca) → Epictetus → Augustine; Epictetus a major authority. | Aristotle (*prohairesis*); Cicero/Lucretius/Seneca (*libera voluntas*); Epictetus; Augustine | null (not held locally; KG + 3rd-party only)"

**Flags:**

1. **"Aristotle (*prohairesis*)" as ancient locus is misleading.** Kahn's central claim about Aristotle is precisely that *prohairesis* does NOT constitute a concept of will — it is one of four *disconnected* notions. Listing *prohairesis* as the Aristotelian anchor without the caveat misrepresents the argument. Kahn's ancient locus for the Aristotle section is *NE* 6.2.1139b4 and 3.2.1111b26–29, but the *point* made at those loci is that the four concepts are not unified.

2. **"Cicero/Lucretius/Seneca (*libera voluntas*)" compresses two distinct Kahn claims.** Kahn treats Lucretius as the locus of the *earliest occurrence* of *libera voluntas* (p. 248) — and argues this is genuinely anticipatory of the modern phrase. Cicero is treated differently: he rendered *hekousion* as *voluntarium*, creating a semantic link between the voluntary and *voluntas* (p. 241). Seneca comes later (third landmark, p. 251 ff.) as the Latin parallel to Epictetus's *prohairesis*. Grouping them as one undifferentiated "Cicero/Lucretius/Seneca" trajectory flattens Kahn's staged argument.

3. **"Epictetus a major authority" is accurate but incomplete.** Kahn's claim is stronger: Epictetus's *prohairesis* is a *new idea* using an old term, structurally equivalent to *voluntas*, and marks the third landmark in a four-stage model. The map entry implies Epictetus is an authority *cited* by Kahn, whereas in fact Epictetus is the *subject* of Kahn's third stage — a positive landmark in the developmental trajectory, not a source Kahn appeals to.
