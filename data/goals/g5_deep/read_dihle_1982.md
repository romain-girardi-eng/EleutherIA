## HEADLINE THESIS

Dihle argues that **the Greeks, throughout their philosophical analysis of human action, never developed a distinct concept of *will*** — action was always explained through intellectual cognition (knowledge of means and ends) plus instinct/emotion, with deliberate choice (προαίρεσις) presupposing knowledge of "something definite." A genuine *concept of will* — will as a faculty separate from both intellect and emotion — first enters Western thought implicitly through **Biblical/Pauline anthropology** (obedience to the will of a creator-God who freely disposes of the future) and is **formally invented as a philosophical tool only by St. Augustine** (c. A.D. 400), by fusing Biblical voluntarism with Neoplatonic ontology and grounding it in introspection. He concedes partial, unsystematized approximations in **late Stoicism (Seneca, Epictetus)** and in **Roman law**, but insists none of these reached the clear philosophical notion.

## CLAIM 2 — προαίρεσις is intellectualist: choice presupposes knowledge of something definite

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | The concept of choice (προαίρεσις) rests on the assumption that intention depends on knowledge of "something definite"; because knowledge refers only to what *exists* and the future is not open to direct cognition, choice cannot ground a will separate from cognition. |
| **Page** | pp. 69–70. |
| **Verbatim anchor (orig + EN)** | "The concept of choice (προαίρεσις) is based on the assumption that intention, psychologically as well as with regard to moral judgement, depends on the knowledge of something definite." (p. 69) |
| **Ancient locus** | Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* III (προαίρεσις = deliberate choice of means and ends); Dihle: "Aristotle tried to handle the problem … by strictly separating the knowledge of the means and ends of action from the habitual disposition to act in a certain way, yet without the concept of will" (p. 70). |
| **Agrees / contests** | Directly grounds Claim 1; sets up the contrast with Augustine (Claim 6). |

## CLAIM 4 — "Free Will" is a Biblical, not a Greek, conception (the two-cosmologies argument)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | What modern scholarship calls *Free Will* — the belief in "man's ability to decide and to act both ways, regardless of the intellectual effort spent on the assessment of the aim" — is foreign to the Greek view (where freedom = unhindered intellectual choice of means/ends) and "already underlies, without being made explicit, the anthropology of the Old Testament." The two notions track two cosmologies: an eternal rule-governed cosmos (Greek, → προαιρετική choice) vs. a creator who freely interferes with his creation (Biblical, → will). |
| **Page** | pp. 71–72. |
| **Verbatim anchor (EN)** | "Consequently, freedom realized in the free choice of the means and ends in human activity, as the Greeks put it, considerably differs from the Christian concept of Free Will which already underlies, without being made explicit, the anthropology of the Old Testament." (pp. 71–72) — "It entails the belief in man's ability to decide and to act both ways, regardless of the intellectual effort…" (p. 72) |
| **Ancient locus** | Old Testament anthropology generally; "stiffheartedness" / hardening of Pharaoh's heart, Exod. 7ff; Gen. 2:21, 6:5 (will, not intellect, subject to moral judgement); Josephus' classification of Jewish sects by attitude to fate/freedom (pp. 75–76). |
| **Agrees / contests** | The label "Free Will" is explicitly Dihle's modern-scholarship framing ("usually referred to, in modern scholarship, by the term Free Will," p. 68). This is **POLE 1** of debate B: the libertarian "act both ways" freedom is Christian/voluntarist in origin, not Greek/Stoic — the position **Frede contests** by locating the first free-will notion in Stoic Epictetus. |

## CLAIM 5 — St. Paul implicitly introduces the concept of will but lacks any term for it

| | |

| **Claim** | Paul's underlying concept is unmistakably *a will* — distinct from all intellectual achievement and from unconscious emotion — that responds to God's commandment and determines action; but Paul coined no term for it, using θέλω and γινώσκω indiscriminately, and even the *will of God* has no fixed term (θέλησις, θέλημα, βουλή, βούλησις, εὐδοκία, γνώμη used as near-synonyms). |

## CLAIM 6 — St. Augustine *invents* the philosophical concept of will

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | The notion of will as an analytic/descriptive tool used in philosophy "from the early Scholastics to Schopenhauer and Nietzsche" was invented by Augustine, by (a) grounding it on Biblical anthropology (man as image of God, Gen. 1:27) and introspection, (b) separating will from both potential and achieved cognition, and (c) making will partake in the very act of cognition (triad *memoria–intelligentia–voluntas*), so that for the first time one notion of will serves both theology and psychology. |
| **Page** | p. 123 (the invention thesis); pp. 124–127 (the construction); p. 134 (Augustine vs. predecessors). |
| **Verbatim anchor (EN)** | "It is generally accepted in the study of the history of philosophy that the notion of will, as it is used as a tool of analysis and description in many philosophical doctrines from the early Scholastics to Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, was invented by St. Augustine." (p. 123) — "St. Augustine separated will from both potential and achieved cognition." (p. 125) — "From St. Augustine's reflections emerged the concept of a human will, prior to and independent of the act of intellectual cognition, yet fundamentally different from sensual and irrational emotion…" (p. 127) |
| **Ancient locus** | Augustine *De Trinitate* (triad *memoria/intelligentia/voluntas*; psychology self-sustaining, p. 126); *Confessiones* (introspection, p. 127); Gen. 1:27; contrast with **Marius Victorinus** *In ep. ad Eph.* 1.1 (primacy of thought over will) and Porphyry's Chaldean triad *esse/vivere/intellegere* (pp. 123–127). Latin: *mens (νοῦς)*, *voluntas*, *mala voluntas* (perverted will = cause of sin, p. 128). |
| **Agrees / contests** | Opens by reporting this as the **generally accepted** view ("It is generally accepted…"), then supplies his own reconstruction of *how*. He distinguishes Augustine sharply from **Marius Victorinus** (whose will-concept stays ontological/theological, not anthropological). This is the climactic POLE-1 claim that **Frede 2011 explicitly displaces back to Epictetus** ("aim completely different from Dihle's"). |

## CLAIM 7 — Late Stoic / Latin approximations: Seneca's "vague voluntarism" and Roman law — but NOT the concept

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | Dihle concedes genuine approximations he is careful to deny full status: (a) **Seneca** "did realize that will should be grasped independently of both cognition and irrational impulse" but "did not proceed to a clear notion of will" — *velle non discitur* shows voluntas is not, like προαίρεσις, an instructed faculty; (b) **Roman law** used *voluntas* in a strict terminological sense for centuries (causa Curiana, 93 B.C.); (c) **Epictetus'** προαίρεσις "came very near to our concept of good or ill will." None of these is systematized into the philosophical concept before Augustine. |
| **Page** | pp. 134–135 (Seneca, Roman law); **note p. 215, n. 24** (Epictetus). |
| **Verbatim anchor (orig + EN)** | Seneca: "telle [*velle*] non discitur, i.e., voluntas is not an instructed, or disciplined, faculty." (p. 134) — "Seneca did realize that will should be grasped independently of both cognition and irrational impulse. But he did not proceed to a clear notion of will." (pp. 134–135) — "Seneca's vague voluntarism was never systematized into a clear concept of will, either by himself or by later Roman authors. Roman philosophy … did not develop the distinct notion of will before St. Augustine." (p. 135) — Epictetus (note): "So προαίρεσις, at least in the language of Epictetus, came very near to our concept of good or ill will, though always pointing to a phenomenon of entirely intellectual activity." (n. 24, p. 215) |
| **Ancient locus** | Seneca *Ep.* 16.5f, 16.6; *velle non discitur* (Sen.); Cicero on *voluntas* = προαίρεσις/βούλησις; causa Curiana (Crassus v. Q. Mucius Scaevola, 93 B.C.); Epict. *Diss.* 1.22.10, 1.22.103, 4.6.34; *Ench.* 1.5. |
| **Agrees / contests** | Credits **Max Pohlenz** (*Stoa* II [1949] 139f, 164; NAWG 1941) for first noting Seneca's voluntaristic *velle/voluntas* — Dihle **builds on Pohlenz** but limits the result ("vague," "never systematized"). The Epictetus concession (n. 24) is the precise hinge where **Frede disagrees**: Frede reads Epictetus' προαίρεσις as already the *first notion of a free will*; Dihle insists it remains "a phenomenon of entirely intellectual activity." |

## EXPLICIT SCHOLARLY ENGAGEMENTS (named)

- **Max Pohlenz** — followed/credited on Seneca's *velle/voluntas* voluntarism and on Cicero's "voluntaristic" rendering (pp. 134–135 + nn. 70, 76; *Stoa* II 1949). Dihle accepts the observation, restricts its scope.
- **A. J. Voelke** (*L'idée de volonté dans le stoïcisme*) — cited (nn., pp. 215–216); Dihle **resists** Voelke's stronger attribution of a will-concept to the Stoics (Claim 3).
- **W. Theiler** (*Vorbereitung des Neuplatonismus*; *Porphyrios und Augustin* 1933) — cited on the Neoplatonic background to Augustine.
- **H. von Campenhausen** — cited approvingly (p. 79) on two-wills antagonism being absent from John.
- **Reporting vs. asserting:** the Augustine-invention thesis is *reported* as "generally accepted" (p. 123) before Dihle adds his own genetic account; the term "Free Will" is flagged as *modern-scholarship* usage (p. 68). Dihle *asserts* the no-Greek-will thesis (Claims 1–3) and the Pauline/Augustinian genesis (Claims 5–6).

## DEBATE-B POSITIONING (for the synthesis)

Dihle = **POLE 1**: the concept of *will*, and the libertarian "act-both-ways" *Free Will*, are of **Biblical/Christian** origin, terminologically realized only by **Augustine (c. 400)**; Greek philosophy (incl. Aristotle and the Stoics) is intellectualist and lacks a will-faculty. Late-Stoic/Latin material (Seneca, Epictetus, Roman law) is *proto-voluntarist* but unsystematized.
Opposed by **Frede 2011** (first free-will notion is Stoic/Epictetan, *autexousion* Stoic, "aim completely different from Dihle's"); by **Irwin/Kenny** (Aristotle already has the concept — reject the shared "late invention" premise); **Fürst** mediates (relocates the decisive systematic step to Origen, 3rd c.).
