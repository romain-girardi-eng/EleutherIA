# Salles 2005 — Position Dossier

**Full title:** Ricardo Salles, *The Stoics on Determinism and Compatibilism*, Ashgate, 2005. 144 pp.  
**Source file:** `Ricardo Salles - The Stoics on Determinism and Compatibilism-Ashgate (2005).md`  
**Covers:** Debate A (Stoic free will / *eph' hēmin* / compatibilism)  
**Note on labels:** All modern labels below — "compatibilism", "hard determinism", "libertarian", "incompatibilist", "free will" — appear in the book as Salles's own scholarly characterisations, not as historical claims about the ancient authors' self-understanding. Salles is explicit that the Stoics "do not seem to have used the term 'freedom' (ἐλευθερία)" in their compatibilist defence (p. xiv).

---

## 1. Headline Thesis

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Stoics are both **determinists** and **compatibilists**; their compatibilism is a philosophically serious position that resists hard-determinism (which denies responsibility despite necessity) and libertarianism (which saves responsibility by rejecting determinism). | xiii–xiv | "they were also compatibilists, that is, they contended that prior necessitation does not preclude on its own that we genuinely deserve praise or blame for the actions we perform. Thus, the Stoics depart from two other positions." | — |

Salles frames the book as a *reconstruction* of the Stoic compatibilist response through analysis of (a) Stoic determinism and (b) Stoic theories of responsibility. He gives the Stoics the onus of proof against the incompatibilist intuition.

## 2. Stoic Determinism: Two Foundations

### 2A. Everything has a cause (bivalence argument)

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chrysippus argues from bivalence to universal causation: if propositions about future occurrents are already true or false, then every future event must have a present cause. | 3–9 | "If there is movement without a cause, it is not the case that every proposition ... will be either true or false." (Cicero *De Fato* 20) | Cic. *De Fato* 20, 26 |
| Prior truth and prior necessitation are **logically independent**: Chrysippus holds (P2) — every future proposition is now true or false — but this does not by itself entail necessity. Salles insists Chrysippus is not committing to (Pn) alone. | 6–7 | "What Chrysippus is not committed to is simply the idea that prior truth is sufficient for establishing prior necessitation." | Cic. *De Fato* 20, 26; DL 7.65 |

### 2B. Causation is necessitating (everlasting recurrence)

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| The "same causes, same effects" principle is grounded in the **orthodox doctrine of everlasting recurrence** (Zeno, Cleanthes, Chrysippus). Transcyclical indiscernibility entails that every actual causal relation is necessary: if c brought about e in one cycle, it must do so in every cycle. | 19–29 | "It is important to recall that the principle of regularity ('same causes, same effects') is not hypothetical but factual." | Nemesius, *De nat. hom.* 111, 26–112, 1 (LS 52C); Praep. ev. 15.14.1 (SVF 1.98); Origen, *Cels.* 4.28 (SVF 2.626) |
| The regularities involved in Stoic determinism are **transcyclical**, not intracyclical. This is a philosophically peculiar but coherent basis for strict causal necessity. | 28–29 | "Transcyclical regularities such as this one differ substantially from the regularities that are empirically accessible to us." | — |
| Salles sides with Sharples (1981) against the view (Rist, LS) that the Stoics dissociated fate from necessity: **everything fated is also causally necessary**, though Chrysippus distinguishes two senses of necessity. | xvi–xvii, 83–84 | "I shall go further and claim that in Stoic philosophy there are two senses of necessity." | Boethius, *In Ar. Int.* 2.234, 27–235, 4; DL 7.75 (LS 38D); Chrysippus ap. Zeno in Stob. *E* 1.138, 16–17 (SVF 1.89; LS 55A) |

## 3. The Four Stoic Theories of Compatibilism

Salles's central structural contribution is identifying **four distinct theories** in Stoic thought, three of them explicitly compatibilist:

### 3A. T₁ — Chrysippus' Internality Theory (Chapter 3)

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T₁** rebuts the "externalist objection" (attributed by Salles to Epicurus): that if determinism is true, everything we do is ultimately determined by external factors alone, removing the basis for praise and blame. | 33–49 | "Premiss (2)*, in particular, is contentious. In fact, it constitutes the target of Chrysippus' counter-argument." | Cic. *De Fato* 40–44; Gellius, *NA* 7.2.11 |
| Chrysippus distinguishes **perfect/primary causes** (internal: the nature and force of the agent's mind) from **proximate/auxiliary causes** (external: the impressor). The latter triggers assent but does not determine it alone. The cylinder's rollability is not given by the push; analogously, the agent's assent is not determined by the external stimulus alone. | 42–49 | "'As therefore he who pushes a cylinder gives it the beginning of its motion, but does not give it the power of rolling; so an impressor when it strikes will ... impress and as it were stamp its appearance on the mind, but assenting will depend on us.'" (Cic. *De Fato* 43) | Cic. *De Fato* 43; Gellius, *NA* 7.2.11 |
| Salles defends Chrysippus against the "deeper externalist objection" (my innate character was externally determined): the peculiar quality (ἰδία ποιότης) is coextensive with the agent's existence — there was never a prior moment when it was imposed from outside. | 47–49 | "of all the instants of time that belonged in succession since the beginning of the present cosmic cycle, there is not any one of them at which the agent himself takes on from outside the peculiar quality." | Simplicius, *In Ar. de an.* 217, 36–218, 2 (SVF 2.395; LS 28I) |
| **Against Bobzien**: Salles notes Bobzien herself concedes the deeper objection is "a powerful criticism" that Chrysippus "cannot successfully meet" — Salles proposes a different defence via temporal coextensiveness. | 47–48 | Bobzien 1998, p. 298–9: "this is a powerful criticism." | — |

### 3B. T₃ — Chrysippus' Reflection/Responsibility Theory (Chapter 4)

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T₃** addresses the incompatibilist claim that responsibility requires the **specific dual capacity to do otherwise** (φεrform or not-perform at the very time t). Chrysippus denies this: it suffices for responsibility that the action flows from a **fully rational impulse** (ὁρμή based on prior *krisis*/reflection). | 51–68 | "an action's depending on me does not require that I could have acted otherwise (either in those very circumstances or at some other time)." | Alexander, *DF* 13 (181, 14–182, 4); Nemesius, *N* 35 (105, 6–106, 11) |
| The Chrysippean formula: "what happens **through us** (δι᾽ ἡμῶν) **by fate** depends on us (ἐφ᾽ ἡμῖν)." Actions that occur in accordance with proper human nature (*krisis* + impulse) depend on us despite being necessary and fated. | 51–54, 61–62 | "Hence, that which happens through us by fate depends on us." (Nemesius *N* 35, 106, 10–11) | *N* 35: 105, 6–106, 11; *DF* 13: 181, 14–182, 4 |
| *Krisis* in the relevant sense is **critical acceptance of impressions** (second sense), not mere verbalized assertion. A fully rational impulse requires prior all-things-considered reflection about whether the action is appropriate. | 55–62 | "someone who has acted on the basis of a critical acceptance of an impression must have gone through the following process: (a) an impulsive impression was received ... (b) I reflect on the all-things-considered appropriateness of the action." | Clement, *Strom.* 2.20.111 (SVF 2.714); *DF* 11: 178, 17–27 |
| **Salles' key claim on sufficiency**: T₃ asserts only a *sufficient* condition for responsibility, not a necessary one. It leaves room for unreflective agents also being responsible (addressed by Epictetus). The full rational impulse is *sufficient* but not required. | 68, 90 | "The Chrysippean theory examined in this chapter does not state a necessary condition for responsibility... Its purpose is to establish, rather, that an incompatibilist is not entitled to maintain... that causal determinism inevitably yields full external determination." | — |
| **Chrysippus–Frankfurt parallel**: Salles argues Chrysippus anticipates Frankfurt's compatibilism. Both hold that responsibility derives from action based on prior all-things-considered reflection, and that the specific capacity to do otherwise is irrelevant. The disanalogies (fate is internal and factually active in Stoicism; Maria's device is external and counterfactually active) are not substantive for the nature of responsibility. | 63–68 | "Frankfurt and Chrysippus explain moral responsibility by appealing to factors that are substantially the same." | Frankfurt 1969; Cic. *De Fato* 43; *N* 35 |

### 3C. T₂ — Chrysippus' Modal Theory (Chapter 5, §5.4)

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T₂** is Chrysippus' modal revision of Diodorus' system. It preserves room for **specific alternate possibilities** (forever-counterfactual events can still be "possible") while maintaining that every factual event is causally necessary. This responds to the first premiss of the incompatibilist argument: "determinism rules out the capacity to do otherwise." | 82–89 | "Chrysippus proposed a new modal system, T₂, that could replace Diodorus' in that it could accommodate both the specific capacity to act otherwise and the specific necessitation of factual actions." | Boethius, *In Ar. Int.* 2.234, 27–235, 4; DL 7.75 (LS 38D) |
| **Against the standard reading (LS, Reesor, Sorabji, Bobzien)**: Salles argues T₂ is *not* a theory of responsibility imposing the specific capacity as a necessary condition. It is a pure modal/metaphysical theory. Its function is to show that determinism is *compatible* with specific alternate possibilities, not to assert that responsibility *requires* them. | 85–89 | "It is controversial, however, that Chrysippus also intended T₂ as a theory of responsibility (either legal or modal). The modal notions it defines... are not presented by the theory itself as related to the concepts of responsibility and of what depends on us." | *DF* 33: 205, 10–11 (with methodological reservations); *DF* 26: 196, 25–197, 3 |

### 3D. T₂ and T₃ as complementary

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| T₂ and T₃ form a **two-pronged strategy** against a single Aristotelian incompatibilist argument: premiss (1) "determinism rules out the capacity to do otherwise" is rebutted by T₂; premiss (2) "responsibility requires the specific capacity to do otherwise" is rebutted by T₃. | 87–89 | "The strategy used by Chrysippus was to reject the conclusion by casting doubt on each of the premisses, one by one." | *EN* 1113b7–8; *EE* 1226a20–28; *De Int.* 9, 19a7–11 |
| The Aristotelian incompatibilist argument is the genuine historical target — contra Bobzien (1998), who locates the incompatibilist target in Middle Platonists (late 1st / early 2nd c. CE). Salles argues the argument is Aristotelian "both in spirit and in doctrine." | 78–82, 88–89 | "Aristotle's views on responsibility, according to which it requires the specific capacity to do otherwise, turn him into an incompatibilist diametrically opposed to T₃." | *EN* 1113b7–8; *EE* 1226a20–28; *De Int.* 9 |

## 4. Attribution of T₃ to Chrysippus (Chapter 5, §5.1–5.2)

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salles defends attribution (A): T₃ is Chrysippean. He opposes Bobzien's (C) — T₃ is late, Philopatoran, responding to Middle Platonists. | 69–77 | "Although we cannot know for certain... there are dialectical and philosophical reasons for believing that the correct interpretation is (A)." | *N* 35: 105, 12–13 (Nemesius names Chrysippus, Philopator, and others) |
| The absence of Chrysippus' name in *DF* 13 is explained by the political context: Alexander dedicated *De Fato* to Septimius Severus and Caracalla (admirers of Marcus Aurelius/Stoicism) and avoided naming Stoics at all to please the emperors. | 72–73 | "Alexander's dedication is probably a sign that he wanted to please the emperors... To openly lambaste the Stoics in DF would have been against his own intentions and interests." | *DF* 164, 3 (dedication) |
| The four major differences between T₁ and T₃ (regularity principle, 'through us' definition, human-nature specification of internal cause, two vs. one external factors) are explained by the **different dialectical targets** of the two theories, not by a difference in author. | 73–78 | "T₁ and T₃ do differ in their specification of the internal cause of what depends on us. But this difference derives from the fact that they intend to rebut different objections." | — |

## 5. Epictetus' Fourth Theory (Chapter 6)

| Claim | Page | Verbatim anchor | Ancient locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Epictetus adds a **fourth theory** (not compatibilist in explicit framing) to complement T₃: responsibility for **unreflective action** (precipitancy/προπέτεια). He argues via a Normative Argument: precipitancy is contrary to human nature; human nature is normative; therefore precipitancy is blameworthy and cannot exculpate. | 91–110 | "Precipitancy is at variance with human nature; but given the normative character of human nature, what is at variance with it ought to be avoided and is, therefore, morally blameworthy." | Epictetus, *D* 1.28, 1.18, 1.6.18–22; 2.18 |
| **Salles' distinctive claim on Epictetus**: Epictetus is the **first Stoic** to make some form of dual capacity presupposed for responsibility — but it is the *general* capacity (to act differently at some other time), not the *specific* dual capacity (to do otherwise at the exact time t). Epictetus is thereby **consistent with Chrysippus**, who only denies the specific capacity is required. | 110 | "This is the first time in Stoic thought that some form of dual capacity is strongly presupposed in accounting for responsibility. But there is no sign anywhere in Epictetus that responsibility requires the specific capacity to do otherwise." | Epictetus, *D* 2.18; *Ench.* 2; *D* 4.1.131 (Cleanthes) |
| Epictetan *eleutheria* (freedom): compatible with specific determinism; the sage aligns desires with fate so they are never frustrated. This is **Stoic freedom**, distinct from responsibility (which is available to all). | 110–111 | "I will only have the capacity to do what I want if I restrict the scope of things that I want to do to the set of things that I am predetermined to do." | *D* 4.1.131 (Cleanthes' Hymn); Ench. 53; DL 7.121 (SVF: ἐλευθερία = ἐξουσία αὐτοπραγίας) |

## 8. Flags on the Quick-Pass Debate Map (data/goals/g5/debate_map.md)

The debate_map entry for Salles reads:

> **Salles 2005** | AGAINST / compatibilism | Stoics = determinists AND compatibilists; depart from BOTH hard-determinists and libertarians (Epicurus, Alexander). Three compatibilist theories of Chrysippus. | Cylinder; Idle Argument (Cic. *De Fato* 28–30); Epictetus on *eph' hēmin* | xiii–xiv

**Flags:**

1. **"Three compatibilist theories of Chrysippus"** — correct in number (T₁, T₂, T₃) but the map omits the crucial fact that **Salles argues T₂ is not a responsibility theory at all**: it is a purely modal/metaphysical theory. Saying "three compatibilist theories" without qualification misrepresents the internal architecture: T₂ is not compatibilist in the sense of directly arguing for responsibility compatibility; only T₁ and T₃ are responsibility theories.

2. **"Idle Argument (Cic. *De Fato* 28–30)"** — The Idle Argument is analysed at *De Fato* 28–29 (F 28–9 in Salles's notation), not 28–30. Salles distinguishes two readings of the objection (pp. 11–13) and discusses Chrysippus' confatalia (*De Fato* 30) as the reply. The map's bracketing of 28–30 is slightly imprecise but not seriously misleading.

3. **"Epictetus on *eph' hēmin*"** — Salles does not focus on Epictetus' use of *eph' hēmin* as a locus; he focuses on *prohairesis*, *parakolouthēsis*, and the Normative Argument. The term *eph' hēmin* as a technical Stoic concept receives much more sustained attention in Bobzien 1998 (and in the broader debate). The map's attribution is not wrong — Epictetus does use *eph' hēmin* — but it suggests Salles engages with the semantic debate about *eph' hēmin* in the way Bobzien does, which he does not. Salles's Epictetus chapter is about moral responsibility for unreflective action, not the semantics of *eph' hēmin*.

4. **No explicit engagement with *autexousion* or the Greek→Christian shift** (Debate D). Salles's book is entirely restricted to the Stoic debate on determinism and compatibilism; it has no position on autexousion, Justin Martyr, Origen, or the patristic reception. The debate_map does not attribute such a position, which is correct.

5. **Salles' explicit disagreement with Frede (2011)** is not captured in the map's agreement/disagreement lines. The map notes "Salles explicitly contra Frede" but cites "Destrée/Salles/Zingano 2014, ch. 8" — a later work. The 2005 book does not address Frede 2011 (published later), but Salles' continuous-compatibilist reading of Epictetus is implicitly opposed to any "invention of free will" reading. The map's note on Salles contra Frede refers correctly to the 2014 essay, not this 2005 book.
