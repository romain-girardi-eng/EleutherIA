## HEADLINE THESIS

Irwin **rejects the "late invention of the will" thesis**. Against Ross, Gauthier, MacIntyre, Dihle, Kahn (and, in effect, the later Frede), he ASSERTS that **Aristotle already possesses a concept of the will** — *boulēsis* understood as essentially rational desire — and that Aquinas is therefore *justified*, not anachronistic, in attributing a concept of the will to Aristotle. The genuine ancient/medieval dispute is **between two views of a will both sides share** (intellectualism vs. voluntarism), *not* a dispute about whether the will exists. In the 2007 volume Irwin extends this: **Augustine is fundamentally an intellectualist and a compatibilist eudaemonist, not a voluntarist** — the voluntarist will that medievals later read out of Augustine is not Augustine's own settled position. On the metaphysical axis Irwin himself ASSERTS a **compatibilist** account of responsibility (siding with the Stoics and Aristotle, against Alexander of Aphrodisias and Epicurus).

## CLAIM 1 — "Concept of the will" is not the property of voluntarists; denying Greeks "the will" is a mistake

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | Critics who deny Greek philosophers a "concept of the will" often really mean only that the Greeks are not *voluntarists*. That is both a historical and a philosophical error: voluntarism vs. intellectualism is a debate **between two views of the will held by disputants who share the concept**. So lacking voluntarism ≠ lacking the will. |
| **Page** | Irwin 1992, p. 467 (=`.md` lines 839–856). |
| **Verbatim anchor (English)** | "It would be both a historical and a philosophical mistake, however, to claim that Greek philosophers lack a concept of the will, if we simply mean that they are not voluntarists. For the debate between voluntarism and intellectualism is a debate between two views of the will, among disputants who share a concept of the will." (1992, p. 467) |
| **Ancient locus** | Frames the *NE* III material that follows; no single locus. |
| **Agrees / contests** | CONTESTS Ross (*Aristotle*, 5th ed. 1949, p. 199), Gauthier–Jolif (*L'Éthique à Nicomaque*, 2nd ed. 1970, Comm. p. 218: "in the psychology of Aristotle the will does not exist"), MacIntyre (*Three Rival Versions* 1990, p. 111), and the "late invention" line of Dihle (*The Theory of the Will in Classical Antiquity*, 1982) and Kahn ("Discovering the will", 1985). |

## CLAIM 2 — *boulēsis* IS the will: it is essentially rational desire (the rationalist reading of Aristotle)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | Aquinas's two interpretive moves — (i) *boulēsis* = will (*voluntas*), (ii) *boulēsis* is *essentially rational* desire, a capacity distinct from sensory desire — are defensible as the best account of Aristotle's remarks on desire and action. The "anti-rationalist" reading (all desire, incl. *boulēsis*, sits in the non-rational part) cannot explain the argument of *NE* i 13, where incontinent conflict requires one impulse belonging to the part "rational in its own right." |
| **Page** | Irwin 1992, pp. 456–461, esp. p. 460 (=`.md` lines 365–435); restated Irwin 2007 §256 ("How is the Will Rational?", concluding "Aquinas is justified in supposing that Aristotle recognizes the will", 2007 line 9324). |
| **Verbatim anchor (English)** | "Aristotle's claims about the connexion between prohairesis and boulesis tend to support Aquinas's view that Aristotle recognizes an essentially rational form of desire; and so Aquinas is justified in supposing that Aristotle recognizes the will." (1992, p. 458) |
| **Ancient loci (Greek)** | *De Anima* iii 9, **432b5** (division of *boulēsis* from other desires); *DA* **433a22–25** ("the only way we can be moved in accordance with reason is to be moved in accordance with *boulēsis*"); *DA* **434a11–14** (incontinence as overcoming of *boulēsis*); *Rhetoric* **1369a2–7** (*boulēsis* = rational desire aiming at the good); *NE* **1102b13–1103a2**, **1102b17–21** (the two *hormai* of the incontinent), **1111b10–20** (*prohairesis* close to but not identical with *boulēsis*); *Topics* **146a36–b6** ("desire without pain"). Aquinas: *ST* Ia q80 a2; De Ver. q22 a4; *in DA* §§802–3. Aspasius, *in EN* (CAG 19) **68.27–32**. |
| **Agrees / contests** | AGREES WITH Aquinas (and reads Aristotle *with* him). CONTESTS the "anti-rationalist" reading held by **Gauthier** (whom Irwin repeatedly notes undermines his own anti-rationalism), J. A. Stewart (1892) and Burnet (1900). Notes Ross/Burnet/Rodier prefer to locate the will in *prohairesis* rather than *boulēsis* (1992 n.25). |

## CLAIM 3 — Aquinas explains Aristotle's account of *voluntary* action and responsibility *better than Aristotle does himself*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | Aristotle's bare conditions for the voluntary (*hekousion* = not by force, not from ignorance, origin in the agent) over-generate: they cover animals, children, sudden and incontinent acts. To explain why only the "complete form of the voluntary" grounds praise and blame, Aristotle *needs* something like Aquinas's appeal to the will (deliberative control / consent), even though he does not state it. So Aquinas's importation of the will "actually explains Aristotle's intention more clearly than Aristotle explains it himself." Irwin is careful: this does not *prove* Aquinas right, only that the Aristotelian theory requires the supplement. |
| **Page** | Irwin 1992, pp. 461–467, esp. p. 466 (=`.md` lines 788–796). |
| **Verbatim anchor (English)** | "While Aquinas at first seemed to be mistaken in intruding his own concept of the will into Aristotle's discussion of voluntary action, further examination shows that he actually explains Aristotle's intention more clearly than Aristotle explains it himself." (1992, p. 466) |
| **Ancient loci (Greek)** | *NE* **1111a22–6** (def. of voluntary; animals & children act voluntarily); **1110a15–18** (origin in us); **1113b30–1114a7** (drunkenness/carelessness: we were in control of *not* getting drunk); **1113b3–7** (virtue and vice up to us); **1111b9–10** ("sudden" actions without decision); **1110b13–14** (we are "an easy prey"). *Physics* **255a5–10** (animals' internal origin). Aquinas, *ST* 1-2 qq6–17, esp. q6 a2; *in EN* §§391, 403, 428, 436, 439, 496, 508. Nemesius, *De Nat. Hom.* (ed. Morani) 325–6 = 117.23–118.1. |
| **Agrees / contests** | AGREES WITH Aquinas; notes the connexion deliberation↔control is drawn explicitly by **Nemesius**, less so by Aristotle. |

## CLAIM 4 — The intellectualism vs. voluntarism dispute is a dispute *within* the concept of the will (Aquinas vs. the "voluntarist" critique)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | One may grant Aquinas that *boulēsis* matches his conception of the will yet still deny that this is an *adequate* conception, on the "voluntarist" ground that the will must be independent of beliefs about the good (so Aquinas "stays too close to Greek intellectualism"). Irwin REPORTS this voluntarist objection and the intellectualist reply (a will severed from the good has no basis for rational choice). His point: this very dispute *presupposes a shared concept of the will* — which is why refusing the Greeks the concept makes the history of the debate unintelligible. |
| **Page** | Irwin 1992, pp. 467–468 (=`.md` lines 827–856); cf. Irwin 2007 §269 on the use of "voluntarism"/"intellectualism" (2007 line 20621). |
| **Verbatim anchor (English)** | "According to this 'voluntarist' criticism of Aquinas, the will must be independent of beliefs about the good, and we must conclude that Aquinas stays too close to Greek intellectualism." (1992, p. 468) |
| **Ancient locus** | Conceptual; hangs on the *boulēsis*/*prohairesis* material above. |
| **Agrees / contests** | Positions Aquinas (intellectualist) against later (medieval, Scotist) voluntarism. In 2007 Irwin pursues this through Scotus (§§359–400) as the developed voluntarist alternative. |

## CLAIM 5 (2007) — Augustine is an INTELLECTUALIST, not a voluntarist; "we have no reason to attribute voluntarism to Augustine"

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | This is Irwin's most pointed 2007 thesis bearing on Debate B. The *prima facie* voluntarist Augustine (the self/will as a "third thing" choosing between rational and non-rational desire, free even against the apparent greater good — the reading that "inspires mediaeval voluntarists") is *not* Augustine's settled view. On reflexion Augustine **accepts the Stoic intellectualist account**: the will is not free to pursue anything other than happiness; sin is a false belief about happiness; even the Fall of Adam and Eve is explained eudaemonistically (misguided love of *freedom*), not by deliberate rejection of the known greater good. Hence Augustine "accepts Stoic intellectualism and avoids voluntarism." |
| **Page** | Irwin 2007 §§217–218 (the voluntarist *appearance*, pp. 398–402), §§223–224 (the intellectualist verdict, pp. 409–411); decisive sentence at the **end of §224** (2007 lines 21122–21126). |
| **Verbatim anchor (English)** | "We have no reason, therefore, to attribute voluntarism to Augustine. … He accepts Stoic intellectualism and avoids voluntarism." (2007 §224, p. 411) |
| **Verbatim anchor (Latin, quoted by Irwin)** | *Lib. Arb.* iii 3: "nisi esset voluntarius, atque in nostra positus potestate, neque laudandus … neque culpandus homo esset cum ad inferiora detorquet quasi quemdam cardinem voluntatis" (1992 n.11; 2007 §218 n.17, rendered: "unless the movement by which the will is turned … were voluntary and placed in our power, a human being would neither be praiseworthy … nor blameworthy"). *Duab. Anim.* 14: "voluntas est animi motus cogente nullo ad aliquid vel non amittendum vel adipiscendum" (2007 §225 n.61, "the will is the movement of the soul, with nothing forcing it, towards not losing something or towards getting something"). *Conf.* viii 22: "ego eram qui volebam, ego qui nolebam. Ego ego eram" ("it was I who was willing and I who was unwilling; I was I"). |
| **Ancient loci (Latin/Augustine)** | *De Lib. Arb.* i 21, iii 2–3; *De Duab. Anim.* 14; *Conf.* vii 5, viii 22; *CD* v 9–10, ix 4f–g, xiv 5b–6a, 11, xv 21–22; *Prop. Rm.* 13–18, 60; *Retract.* i 15.3, i 23.2; *De nupt. et concup.* i 31; *Trin.* xi 15, xv 41. NT roots: Rom. 7:15–20, Gal. 5:17, Sir. 15:13. |
| **Agrees / contests** | AGREES WITH the Stoic intellectualist reading; treats the second-order-desire / "identification" reconstruction (explicitly cited as **Frankfurt**, "Freedom" 16–19; **Charles Taylor**, "Agency" 15–18; **Nagel**, *VN* 118, 122–3) as the strongest case *for* a voluntarist Augustine — then rejects it. References debates in Burnaby, Te Selle, Wetzel (2007 §224 n.54). |

## CLAIM 6 (2007) — Augustine on freewill & determination is a COMPATIBILIST; predestination is consistent with responsibility

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | Augustine "offers a compatibilist defence" of freedom against the threat of divine predestination (the strongest determinism). Freedom resides in the unforced *consent* of the will; that the will's causal role is itself caused (by antecedent causes, by God's foreknowledge and the created order of causes) is "irrelevant … to the claims about freedom that matter for responsibility." Augustine answers Alexander-type incompatibilism by distinguishing types of necessity (the necessity that excludes freedom vs. the harmless necessity to which even God is subject). |
| **Page** | Irwin 2007 §225 "Freewill and Determination", pp. 412–413 (2007 lines 21129–21222). |
| **Verbatim anchor (English)** | "Augustine offers a compatibilist defence (CD v 9). He argues that divine foreknowledge does not take away acting on our will." (2007 §225, p. 413) — and: "Since Augustine holds this compatibilist view about freedom, it is not surprising that he takes his views about grace and predestination to be consistent with freedom and responsibility for sin." |
| **Ancient loci** | *CD* **v 9–10** (foreknowledge & the order of causes; types of necessity); Origen, *Contra Celsum* **ii 20** (Stoic refutation of the Lazy Argument, invoked vs. Celsus); Rom. 9:16. Alexander, *De Fato* (incompatibilist objection). |
| **Agrees / contests** | AGREES WITH Augustine's compatibilism; REPORTS, and judges unsuccessful, **Alexander's** incompatibilist objection that mere parallel causal role (as in animals' impulses) cannot ground *moral* responsibility. |

## CLAIM 7 (2007) — Irwin's OWN metaphysical verdict: compatibilism is correct; Alexander & Epicurus are mistaken to require indeterminism

| | |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | Independently of exegesis, Irwin ASSERTS his own view: the Stoic (and Aristotelian) **compatibilist** account of responsibility is sound; **Alexander is "mistaken"** to read Aristotle as committed to indeterminism, and Epicurus's incompatibilist argument rests on a questionable doctrine of causation (that the "origin" of an action is its *earliest sufficient condition*) that neither Aristotle nor the Stoics need accept. Freedom can consist in "acting in the light of recognition of reasons for doing x rather than y," which "allows a compatibilist account of responsibility." Aristotle's own account "is closer to the Stoic position than to Alexander's." |
| **Page** | Irwin 2007 §147 "Why we should Reject Compatibilism" [chapter heading is the *incompatibilist's* slogan; Irwin's verdict is the reverse] pp. 263–264; §148 pp. 264–265; §172 p. 306 (lines 15848–15855); §173 p. 307 (line 15890). The "Alexander is mistaken" verdict is stated at 2007 §213, p. 389 (line 19964). |
| **Verbatim anchor (English)** | "Our discussion of the Greek moralists has found that the Stoic conception of freedom and responsibility captures the Aristotelian conception as well, and that Alexander is mistaken in arguing that Aristotle either affirms or is committed to an indeterminist account of freedom." (2007 §213, p. 389). And: "This appeal to rational choice between alternatives allows a compatibilist account of responsibility." (2007 §172, p. 306). And: "Aristotle's account of responsibility is closer to the Stoic position than to Alexander's." (2007 §173, p. 307). |
| **Ancient loci** | Epicurus: DL **x 134** ("better to follow the myth of the gods than be a slave to the fate of the physicists"); *On Nature* = Arr. 34.27–30 = LS 20 C (self-undermining argument); swerve evidence in Lucretius/Cicero (2007 §149 n.30). Stoics: Alex. *De Fato* 13 (182.12 = LS 62G "by fate through the animal"); the Medea / oak-of-Pelion example (§169). Alexander: *De Fato* 14 (183.24–185.7, 185.21–186.3); *Mantissa* 172.25–173.21; *Quaest.* iii 13. |
| **Agrees / contests** | ASSERTS compatibilism; CONTESTS **Alexander of Aphrodisias** and **Epicurus** (incompatibilist/indeterminist) and, by extension, **Susanne Bobzien**'s reading insofar as it stresses Stoic non-libertarian freedom (Irwin cites Bobzien, *DFSP* ch. 7, 335–6, on Epictetus/*autexousiotēs*, 2007 §172 n.82 — as reference, not as a target). Cites **Susan Sauvé Meyer**, "Responsibility" 234–40, in support of the compatibilist reading (§172 n.83). Notes Sharples's doubts about Alexander's accuracy (§172 n.79). |

## CROSS-WORK CONSISTENCY

1992 and 2007 are fully consistent and mutually reinforcing. 1992 = the *Aristotle/Aquinas* leg (the will already present as rational *boulēsis*). 2007 = the same thesis embedded in a continuous narrative, plus the *Augustine* leg (Augustine too is, on reflexion, an intellectualist compatibilist, not the proto-voluntarist medievals took him for) and Irwin's *own* compatibilist adjudication of the determinism debate (pro-Stoic, contra Alexander/Epicurus). The genuine voluntarist break, for Irwin, comes only later (Scotus, 2007 §§359–400) — i.e. voluntarism is the late development, the *will itself* is not.
