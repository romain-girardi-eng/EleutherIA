## DEBATE A — Did the early Stoa have free will? What is *to eph' hēmin*?

The question is reframed three ways by the literature: (1) Did the early Stoa have a *libertarian free will*? — near-unanimous **NO**. (2) Did they have a notion of *will / volition*? — split. (3) What does *eph' hēmin* mean? — one-sided causative (the dominant reading) vs the two-sided potestative the dominant camp says is read back anachronistically.

| Scholar | Stance | Key claim | Contested ancient locus | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bobzien 2001** | AGAINST free will | Early Stoa = teleological causal determinism + cylinder-based causal responsibility + *eph' hēmin* = *di' hēmōn* (one-sided causative). Modern free-will problem is anachronistic. | Chrysippus cylinder (Cic. *De Fato* 39–44 + Gellius *NA* 7.2.11); *synkatathesis* | 276–282, 330–357, 396–412 |
| **Bobzien 1998** | AGAINST (inadvertent) | The free-will problem was not discovered but *inadvertently constructed* by an Aristotelian/Stoic "mix-up" no later than early 2nd c. CE; earliest unambiguous awareness = Alexander. Two senses: one-sided causative (Stoic) vs two-sided potestative (Peripatetic). MR1 autonomy vs MR2 do-otherwise. | *to eph' hēmin*; Alexander *De Fato*; *eph' hēmin* → *autexousion* | 133–142, 187–189, 375–412 |
| **Inwood 1985** | AGAINST free will | Explicitly rejects the "free will" reading of Cleanthes' *Hymn*; Stoic *eph' hēmin* ≠ Alexander's power "to do either of two opposite actions". Assent grounds responsibility, not a power to do otherwise. | Cleanthes *Hymn to Zeus*; *synkatathesis*; Alexander *De Fato* 180.5–7 Bruns | 66–70, 88–89 |
| **Brennan 2005** | AGAINST / compatibilism | Chrysippus is a *compatibilist / "soft determinist"* (label applied explicitly); "up to us" = originating from one's own character; cylinder-and-cone. No libertarianism. | Cylinder-and-cone (Cic. *De Fato* 41–43; Gellius *NA* 7.2); Idle Argument | 255–257 |
| **Salles 2005** | AGAINST / compatibilism | Stoics = determinists AND compatibilists; depart from BOTH hard-determinists and libertarians (Epicurus, Alexander). Three compatibilist theories of Chrysippus. | Cylinder; Idle Argument (Cic. *De Fato* 28–30); Epictetus on *eph' hēmin* | xiii–xiv |
| **Gourinat 2005** | AGAINST / continuist | Defends Chrysippus' coherence; reads Epictetan *prohairesis* cautiously — neither modern free will nor mere decision. | *prohairesis* in Epictetus; Chrysippus on fate | (PDF not page-consulted; quote null) |
| **Frede 2011** | QUALIFIED FOR (late) | Agrees the *early* Stoa had no free will, but **Epictetus** produced "our first actual notion of a free will" by enriching the assent-theory with an inner life. *autexousion*: Musonius → Epictetus → (from Justin) Christians. | *synkatathesis*; *prohairesis* in Epictetus; *autexousion*; *eleutheria* | ch. 2–6, esp. 2770–2773 |
| **Voelke 1973** | FOR volition (proto) | A genuine *idée de volonté* already in early Stoicism, grounded in *hormē* + *synkatathesis*. (Pre-Bobzien classic.) | *hormē*, *synkatathesis*, *hēgemonikon* | image-scan PDF, no OCR; quote null |

**Agreement / disagreement lines**
- **Bobzien ⟂ Frede** (the fault-line): agree the early Stoa lacked free will; **disagree** on whether a genuine free will later emerges *within* Stoicism (Frede: yes, Epictetus) or only by anachronistic mix-up (Bobzien: inadvertent, Alexander-era).
- **Bobzien ≈ Inwood ≈ Brennan ≈ Salles** converge on the one-sided/causative *eph' hēmin* and Stoic compatibilism.
- **Salles explicitly contra Frede** (Destrée/Salles/Zingano 2014, ch. 8): causal-continuist reading of Epictetus vs Frede's "first free will".
- **Voelke vs Dihle/Frede/Bobzien**: the older "Stoics had volition" reading that the later debate problematises.

## DEBATE B — Origins of the notion of (free) will: who invents it, and when?

An origin-hunting frame (KG node `debate_origins_notion_of_will_modern_paradigm` marks it as a *contested historiographical paradigm* — positions attributed, not endorsed). Splits five ways on WHO and WHEN.

| Scholar | Stance (pole) | Key claim | Contested ancient locus | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dihle 1982** | POLE 1 — Augustine, late, Christian, voluntarist | The Greeks never developed a distinct concept of will; Augustine *invents* the philosophical notion of *voluntas*, via Paul + Neoplatonism. Criterion is explicitly voluntaristic. | Augustine *De civ. Dei* / *De Trin.* / *De lib. arb.*; Aristotle *NE* III (*boulēsis*) | 68, 123, 144 |
| **Sorabji 2017** | POLE 1-aligned, anti-Frede | The will has *four strands* (rationality, freedom, power, perversion by pride), assembled only in Augustine; **none of the four as a cluster is in Epictetus** → Frede is wrong to find free will there. | Epictetus *Disc.* (*prohairesis*); Augustine (assembler) | 63 |
| **Frede 2011** | POLE 2 — Epictetus, earlier, Stoic/pagan, rationalist | The *first actual notion of a free will* is Stoic, paradigmatically Epictetus, built by enriching assent-theory. *autexousion* is Stoic; Christian only "from Justin Martyr onwards". Origen a *transmitter*, deviations from Platonism not Christianity. Aim "completely different from Dihle's". | Epictetus *Disc.* 4.1; Origen *De princ.* III.1; *prohairesis* / *autexousion* / *eleutheria* | 1, 6, 74, 76, 121 |
| **Kahn 1988** | Intermediate / developmental | The will is "discovered" on a trajectory Aristotle → Latin *voluntas* (Cicero/Lucretius/Seneca) → Epictetus → Augustine; Epictetus a major authority. | Aristotle (*prohairesis*); Cicero/Lucretius/Seneca (*libera voluntas*); Epictetus; Augustine | null (not held locally; KG + 3rd-party only) |
| **Fürst 2022** | POLE 3 — Origen, 3rd c., first systematic libertarian | Mediates: receives Stoic/Chrysippean compatibilist terminology but, on Platonist + biblical grounds, moves "from determinism through compatibilism into libertarianism, which Origen first systematically conceived". Coins *christlicher Kompatibilismus* / *kompatibilistischer Libertarismus*. | Origen *In Hier. hom.* 18,3 (τὸ γὰρ αὐτεξούσιον ἐλεύθερόν ἐστι); *De princ.* III.1 | 178 |
| **Irwin 1992** | Earliest pole — no late invention | Aristotle *already* has a concept of the will (*boulēsis* as rational desire, per Aquinas); the intellectualism/voluntarism dispute is a debate *between two views of a shared concept*, not an invention. (Aligns with KG-attributed Kenny.) | Aristotle *NE* III (*boulēsis* / *prohairesis*); Aquinas *ST* Ia q80 a2 | 468 |

**Agreement / disagreement lines**
- **Frede ⟂ Dihle** (explicit): Frede states his aim is "completely different from Dihle's" (p. 6) and displaces the origin from Augustine *back* to Epictetus.
- **Sorabji ⟂ Frede** (explicit rebuttal): the four strands cluster only in Augustine; Epictetus lacks the cluster → POLE 1-aligned against POLE 2.
- **Irwin (& Kenny) ⟂ both Dihle and Frede**: reject the shared "late invention" premise; Aristotle already has the concept.
- **Fürst mediates** Dihle/Frede: keeps the Stoic-reception channel (with Frede) but relocates the decisive *systematic* libertarian step to Origen (3rd c.), earlier than Augustine (vs Dihle), later than/distinct from Epictetus' compatibilist Stoicism (vs Frede).
- **Data flag (left unedited):** `scholar_position_kahn_will_emerges_seneca_epictetus`'s `validity_assessment.rationale` mis-states Frede as arguing the will was "already present in Aristotle" — that is Irwin/Kenny's view; Frede explicitly denies it (Frede 2011 pp. 94–96). Flagged for correction, untouched per no-auto-fix rule.

## DEBATE C — Is Alexander of Aphrodisias a libertarian? (De Fato reception)

A spectrum from "genuine libertarian" to "confused dead-end", plus two flanking positions.

| Scholar | Stance | Key claim | Contested ancient locus | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sharples 1983** | FOR (hedged category) | Alexander *has* a libertarian (not soft-determinist) conception of responsibility, but "libertarian" is a deliberate historiographical category, not Alexander's term (debate runs in terms of *to eph' hēmin*). | Alexander *De Fato* 181.5; 180.26–8; 199.9; 211.32 Bruns | 9 |
| **Sharples 1983** | FOR but unestablished | Treats power-for-opposites as "unqualified, unrestricted possibility", yet "faces very real difficulties in combining his libertarian position with an account of the rational element … and does not really solve it" (*Mantissa* XXII). Stipulates, does not establish. | *De Fato* (power for opposites, 180–211 Bruns); *Mantissa* XXII (170–172) | 22 |
| **Bobzien 1998** | "Seemingly" only / inadvertent | Alexander = "earliest unambiguous evidence" of a free-will *problem* (192.22 "same causes, same effects"), but it arose *inadvertently* between systems, without a concept of the will. | *De Fato* 192.22–4; *eph' hēmin* 181.5, 180.26–8 | 137 |
| **Bobzien 1998** | Stops short | "Has collected all the ingredients … but all these points do not add up to choosing and acting from free-will"; "stops short of a concept of free will" because the soul is corporeal. | *De Fato* two-sided *exousia* (180.26–8, 181.5); corporeal soul; *Mantissa* XXII–XXIII | 171–172 |
| **Bobzien 1998** | Conceptual shift | The specific innovation: *dynasthai* → *exousia* ("authority over"), introducing a decision-making faculty (Epictetan+Aristotelian fusion); but Alexander himself has no free-will problem (denies pre-determination). | *De Fato* *eph' hēmin* (181.5, 180.26–8); shift to *exousia* | 173–174 |
| **M. Frede 2011** | Confused ancestor / dead-end | "It is in Alexander that we find the ancestor of the notion that to have a free will is to be able, in the very same circumstances, to choose between A and B … Unfortunately … not able to provide a coherent account." Genuine notion is Stoic/Epictetan. | *De Fato* XIV (183.27), XXVIII (199.16), XXXVIII (211.27), 192.22; Carneades (Cic. *Fat.* 23, 31) | 99–100 |
| **M. Frede 2011** | Two confusions | The position rests on (i) misreading Stoic determinism, (ii) a mistaken desert-based notion of merit → a philosophical error, not an advance. | *De Fato* 192.22ff; *Mantissa* XXII (ability-to-do-otherwise = weakness) | 100–101 |
| **Donini 2010** | Tendentious interpreter | Correct on Aristotle in the *Prior Analytics* commentary, but in the polemical *De Fato* "reticent" — suppresses Aristotle's "for the most part" frequencies "in quite unaristotelian fashion" to widen contingency. Apparent libertarianism partly a polemical artefact. | *De Fato* XI (178.15), XII (180.12); vs *In An. Pr.* 162.31–163.7; Aristotle *NE* III, *De Int.* 9 | 74–77 |
| **D. Frede 1982** (flank) | Not a fair trial | Alexander "has not given the Stoics a fair trial"; his case rests on "dramatizing" determinism (invariance→invariability→ironclad necessity; coins *katēnankasmenōs*) and misreading Stoic causal theory. Libertarianism rhetorically inflated. | *De Fato* fate=efficient cause (169.18), *katēnankasmenōs* (185.15, 190.8…), 192.18; Cic. *De Fato* 36, 41–44 | 276–294 (esp. 287–289, 293) |
| **Ramelli 2014** (flank) | Live ancestor (positive) | Alexander a robust anti-determinist and constructive **source** for Origen's *autexousion*; Origen "very probably knew Alexander's works"; both refuted Stoic determinism with very similar arguments (praise/blame, prayer). | *De Fato* (praise/blame, prayer); Origen *Peri Archōn* III.1; formula ἦν ποτε ὅτε οὐκ ἦν | 238 (237–289) |

**Disagreement axes**
1. **Is "libertarian" apt?** Sharples: yes (as a category). M. Frede: yes but incoherent. Bobzien: only "seemingly" — he stops short.
2. **Establish or merely stipulate/assert?** Sharples, D. Frede, Donini converge on **stipulated, not established**; Ramelli treats it as substantive.
3. **Ancestor or dead-end?** M. Frede = confused dead-end; Bobzien = inadvertent way-station (real free will comes later, in theological determinism); Ramelli = live ancestor transmitted to Origen.

Frede 2011 pp. 95–101 and Donini pp. 74–77 not yet dedicated argument nodes. Loci anchor to existing corpus passages `passage_alex_fat_1..38` and `passage_cic_fat_*`.

## DEBATE D — *eph' hēmin* / *autexousion* semantics and the Greek→Christian shift

Two sub-questions: (i) Is *eph' hēmin* "free will"? (ii) How did *autexousion* shift from Greek philosophy into Christian use?

### Point (i) — Is *eph' hēmin* "free will"?

| Scholar | Stance | Key claim | Contested ancient locus | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bobzien 1998/2001** | AGAINST — not free will | One-sided causative (Chrysippus: 'x because of us') vs two-sided potestative; translating either as "free will" begs the question. Indeterminist reading only in Alexander. | Cic. *De fato* 40–43; Gellius *NA* 7.2; Plut. *Stoic. Rep.* 1055f | 139–142; 282–287 |
| **Bobzien 1998** | Aristotle two-sided ≠ indeterminist | Aristotle's *eph' hēmin* is two-sided (EN 1113b5–8) but neutral toward (in)determinism; indeterminist reading is a later development. | Aristotle *NE* III.5, 1113b5–8 | 142–143 |
| **Eliasson 2008** | AGAINST — collapses distinctions | Translating *eph' hēmin/eleutheria/autexousion/prohairesis* as "free will" makes distinct ancient issues indiscernible; they were separate, only gradually merged. | Aristotle *EN* III; Chrysippus; Epictetus | 1–3, 14–16 |
| **Frede 2011** | *eph' hēmin* ≠ free will, but locates will in Epictetus | *eph' hēmin* is a *precondition*; a free will needs in addition a notion of will + of freedom (*eleutheria*). Even Aristotle lacked it. First actual notion: Epictetus (not Alexander). | Aristotle *EN* III; Epictetus | 6–9 |
| **Labarrière 2009** | AGAINST anachronism | Resists reading *eph' hēmin* through modern "subjectivity/liberty"; criticizes Gauthier's *autexousion* dating as "strongly marked by Christian literature". | Aristotle *EN* III.5–7 (1113b7–14); Epictetus; Alexander | 7–8 |

**Disagreement:** Bobzien vs Frede on **when** the first concept of free will arises — **Alexander** (Bobzien) vs **Epictetus** (Frede). They agree *eph' hēmin* itself is not "free will".

### Point (ii) — The Greek→Christian semantic shift (*autexousion*)

| Scholar | Stance | Key claim | Contested ancient locus | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bobzien 1998** | Continuous, not Christian invention | *autexousion* legal/political → philosophical (Musonius/Epictetus), supersedes *eph' hēmin* by Nemesius (c. 390–400). Christian "free-will problem" is theological (providence/evil), distinct from Alexander's physical-causal one. | Epictetus *Diss.* II.2.3, IV.1.56–100; Nemesius *De nat. hom.* 39; Origen *De princ.* III.1 | 165–167, 173–175 |
| **Eliasson 2008** | Three-stage equation | Epictetus (related but distinct) → Alexander (explicitly equates) → Nemesius (synonymous). Christian adoption (Justin, Origen, Hippolytus, Methodius) follows the same pagan trajectory; no discontinuous invention. | Epictetus *Diss.* II.2.3; Alexander *De fato*; Nemesius 39 (311.7 Morani); Methodius *De autexousio* | 6 n.23, 7–9, 153–155 |
| **Frede 2011** | Stoic channel; Justin first | Christians got *autexousion* from Stoicism via Platonism. Justin first attested Christian user; Tatian coins *eleutheria tēs prohaireseōs*; Origen's *De princ.* III.1 proceeds "along standard Stoic lines" (title *autexousion*, body *eph' hēmin*). Clement→Origen shift is the key moment. | Justin *1 Apol.* 43–44; Tatian *Or.* 7.1; Origen *De princ.* III.1; Clement *Strom.* | 74–75, 102–105, 104, 112–113 |
| **Long & Sedley 1987** | Stoic agency marker | *apo* ('from') a stronger marker of personal agency than *di* ('through') in Stoic Greek — cited by Labarrière re Alexander's terminological appropriations. | Origen *De orat.* VI.1 = SVF II.989; Alexander *De fato* XXIV, XXVI | vol. II, 310 (quote null) |
| **Labarrière 2009** | Stoic vocabulary turned against Stoa | Alexander mixes Aristotelian + Stoic vocabulary as deliberate polemic; *eph' hēmin* requires a two-sided "yes/no" the Stoic *sunkatathesis* model destroys. | Alexander *De fato* XIV, *Mantissa* XXII–XXIII; Origen *De princ.* III.1–3 | 18–22 |

**Consensus on (ii):** *eph' hēmin* and *autexousion* are distinct but related; their equation is gradual (complete in Nemesius), not sudden; Christian adoption flows from the pagan tradition; orthodox patristic line = Justin → Origen → Methodius → Nemesius.
