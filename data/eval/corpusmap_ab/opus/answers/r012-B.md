ANSWER:
The corpus mainly supports Augustine's own verdict: a clarification. It also preserves scholarly dissent.

1. Book III's anthropology. The synthesis of De Lib. Arb. 3.20-22 separates natural ignorance and difficulty, which are not blameworthy and are where progress begins, from penal ignorance and difficulty, which come from voluntary neglect and are punishment for sin [synthesis_aug_natural_penal]. The Latin of III.18-21 itself is not in the corpus. Only 1.11.21 and 3.4.9 are held [c91f039f-2616-4c5c-ba9c-822e459d08b5; 3e7b416a-93fe-4f6b-baa9-642f99ab730e].

2. Augustine's later reading. He says Pelagius used his Book III dictum "quis enim peccat in eo, quod nullo modo cauere potest?" He then points to what follows it: acts done in ignorance, and acts done "by necessity, where a man wills to do right and cannot" (Rom 7; Gal 5), are also condemned [561d0edd-4bc6-4fe3-9efb-f99715287c1b]. He says grace went unmentioned because it was not the subject, and the books were written against the Manichees, not the Pelagians [75ab6db0-fb86-49d2-a510-f90b7c3b8bf4; 818a6c6e-7f24-4e5f-94df-07cb8997292f]. The will must be freed by prevenient grace [75ab6db0-fb86-49d2-a510-f90b7c3b8bf4]. The work already taught that man could fall by free choice but not rise, so he wrote "as if already against them" [4a65bea1-32ed-4bfb-8acc-022c64ce3b98]. He also limits his early definitions of sin to sin that is not also a punishment for sin [6543022a-12dd-4929-8a91-f7e84e5fc0f1; 527d4f7a-1fee-477e-8166-c5a5aabd6470].

3. The case for a shift. Moon places the "radical shift" in Ad Simplicianum (396-397), after De Lib. Arb. The Pelagian debates "only served to solidify" it [scholarly_argument_moon_augustine_s_development_on_pre_2]. Gorday sees a development toward a more deterministic reading [scholarly_argument_gorday_augustine_s_development_on_fre_1].

Verdict: the penal-difficulty idea gives real continuity. The prevenient-grace emphasis, though, is a later development that the Retractationes read back into the work.
