Andresen argues for continuity. He places Justin in the "orthodox" Middle Platonism of Plutarch and Atticus [scholarly_argument_andresen_justin_s_philosophical_classif_0; pub_andresen_1953_justin_platonismus]. He calls Justin's anti-Stoic arguments "weithin identisch" (largely identical) to Middle Platonist ones: the soul is free in its choices, while the consequences of those choices fall under heimarmene [scholarly_argument_andresen_heimarmene_fate_and_free_will_1]. His key parallel sets 1 Apol. 43.2 ("if all is by fate, nothing is eph' hêmin") beside Alcinous, Didask. 26 [argument_justin_equals_alcinous_heimarmene; argument_justin_antifatalism]. He does note one difference: Alcinous argues metaphysically, Justin ethically [scholarly_argument_andresen_methodological_distinction_met_4]. He also notes that sin and grace play no role for Justin [scholarly_argument_andresen_sin_and_grace_in_justin_s_theo_3].

Karamanolis stresses discontinuity, or at least independence. He reads the Christians as reworking these ideas in their own way, not just borrowing vocabulary from Middle Platonism or Stoicism [pub_karamanolis_2021_philosophy_early_christianity; scholarly_argument_karamanolis_status_of_early_christian_phil_1]. For him, to eph' hêmin, prohairesis and autexousion come from the Stoics, and Christians adopted them because they shared Stoic theological assumptions [scholarly_argument_karamanolis_free_will_and_divine_providenc_0]. The KG summary of his chapter does not name Justin explicitly.

The texts support both sides. 2 Apol. 7 rejects Stoic fate by necessity but grounds praise in the created nature's ability to turn either way [0e8c1f8b-5a0b-53a8-8e92-8fcb98ce6936]. It gives autexousion to angels as well as humans in a demonological theodicy, which has no counterpart in Alcinous [argument_justin_angel_fall; scholarly_argument_minns_as_justin_s_term_for_free_will_2]. Bobzien finds no autexousion in Alcinous's account of what depends on us [scholarly_argument_bobzien_autexousion_as_philosophical_t_6].
