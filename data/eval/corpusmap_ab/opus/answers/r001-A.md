1 Apol. 43 argues that if everything happens καθ' εἱμαρμένην, "οὐδὲ τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν ἐστιν ὅλως" and praise or blame would make no sense. It grounds responsibility in προαίρεσις ἐλευθέρα [c2b1124e-da5c-55e6-9549-79033bc71a2f; argument_justin_antifatalism]. In 2 Apol. 6–7 (SC numbering 1.II.6) Justin denies that humans act or suffer καθ' εἱμαρμένην, in a passage that criticises the Stoics [db157437-31b2-58fa-b9fa-f30b58f4c4af]. The KG says αὐτεξούσιον is the 2 Apol. 7 term, as in the angel-fall argument [argument_justin_angel_fall; argument_justin_martyrs_apologetic_argument_for_free_will_45c2dde2], but the retrieved passage is cut off before the word appears.

Continuity (Andresen): Justin's anti-fatalism is "weithin identisch" with Middle Platonist critiques. 1 Apol. 43.2 mirrors Alcinous, Didask. 26 (τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν οἰχήσεται): the soul is free in its choices, but their consequences fall under heimarmenē [argument_justin_equals_alcinous_heimarmene; scholarly_argument_andresen_heimarmene_fate_and_free_will_1; scholarly_argument_andresen_justin_s_philosophical_classif_0; pub_andresen_1953_justin_platonismus]. Boys-Stones also counts Justin among the witnesses to hypothetical fate [scholarly_argument_boys_stones_justin_martyr_as_middle_platon_7].

Discontinuity (Karamanolis): early Christians form a distinct philosophical school [scholarly_argument_karamanolis_status_of_early_christian_phil_1]. They took up ἐφ' ἡμῖν, προαίρεσις and αὐτεξούσιον, which he calls Stoic terms, because they shared Stoic theological assumptions [scholarly_argument_karamanolis_free_will_and_divine_providenc_0]. The KG has no Karamanolis node that deals with Justin's two Apologies in particular.

Assessment: the argument structure of 1 Apol. 43 fits Middle Platonism. The move to αὐτεξούσιον, used for created angels and humans [7b029b3b-d680-4362-a0ea-8ef4b2aef215], is new, and Bobzien notes that the word became a technical term only later [scholarly_argument_bobzien_autexousion_as_philosophical_t_6].
