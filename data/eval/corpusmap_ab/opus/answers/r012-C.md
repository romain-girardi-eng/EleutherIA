ANSWER:
The corpus supports "clarification with a real hinge" rather than either pure answer.

Continuity: De Lib. Arb. III.20-22 already treats ignorance and difficulty in two ways: as the soul's natural, blameless starting point (3.22.64 "inde incipit anima proficere") and as a just penal condition following voluntary neglect [synthesis_aug_natural_penal]. Gorday cites Book III precisely for the bondage of the will as a penal condition [scholarly_argument_gorday_augustine_s_development_on_fre_1]. In the Retractationes Augustine claims continuity: he wrote "antequam Pelagiana heresis extitisset ... uelut iam contra illos" [4a65bea1-32ed-4bfb-8acc-022c64ce3b98]. Grace went unmentioned only because the topic then was the Manichaeans' denial that evil comes from free choice, not grace [818a6c6e-7f24-4e5f-94df-07cb8997292f; 75ab6db0-fb86-49d2-a510-f90b7c3b8bf4]. He also points out that Book III already said that things done through ignorance or necessity are blameworthy (Rom 7; Gal 5:17) [561d0edd-4bc6-4fe3-9efb-f99715287c1b].

Shift: these are retrospective self-readings. The KG's anti-Pelagian argument node calls the change a dramatic move from early optimism about free choice to compatibilism centred on grace [argument_augustines_antipelagian_argument_grace_necessity_0b29401f]. Bonaiuti and Moon place the break at Ad Simplicianum (396-397), before Pelagius. For them, subjection to concupiscence erased the capacity for rehabilitation that De Lib. Arb. had left open. Moon attributes the shift to Manichaean pressure, with the Pelagian controversy only "solidifying" it [scholarly_argument_bonaiuti_augustine_s_doctrine_of_origin_0; scholarly_argument_moon_augustine_s_development_on_pre_2].

Verdict: the penal-ignorance framework of III.18-22 survives. What changed, c. 396, was making grace prevenient and fully gratuitous. Pelagianism forced that change into the open; it did not cause it. The corpus does not hold the Latin text of III.18-19 itself.
