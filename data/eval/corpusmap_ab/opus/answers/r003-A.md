The KG treats Sir 15:11-20 as one of the earliest structurally complete arguments for human moral agency in Jewish wisdom, predating the technical vocabulary of autexousion/liberum arbitrium [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]. Ben Sira (Hebrew c. 190-175 BCE; Greek translation by his grandson c. 132 BCE) [work_sirach_a3b4c5d6; person_ben_sira_i9j0k1l2] argues in four steps: (1) "Do not say" one's sin comes from God, who does not do what he hates (vv. 11-12); (2) God created humans and left them "in the power of their own deliberation" (ἐν χειρὶ διαβουλίου αὐτοῦ), the Greek rendering Hebrew yetzer as a neutral deliberative capacity, not the later rabbinic evil inclination (v. 14); (3) life and death are set before each person, echoing Deut 30:19 (v. 17); (4) divine omniscience is compatible with choice and God commands no one to sin (vv. 18-20) [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0; concept_bechirah_c1d2e3f4]. The KG links it to Ezekiel 18 as an antecedent [argument_ezekiel_individual_responsibility_q1r2s3t4].
Scholarship: Brand sees it as a theodicy answering deterministic (possibly Hellenistic) views, while noting a contrary deterministic strand in Sir 33:7-15 [scholarly_argument_brand_ben_sira_free_will_theodicy]; Maston stresses the Deuteronomic two-ways model prioritising human action [scholarly_argument_maston_ben_sira_and_human_agency_1]; Klawans treats it as precedent for Sadducean "relative libertarianism" (Josephus, War 2.164-65), the polemical "Say not" showing a live 2nd-c. BCE debate [scholarly_argument_klawans_2012_sadducee_libertarianism_matches_ben_sira; group_sadducees_a5b6c7d8].
