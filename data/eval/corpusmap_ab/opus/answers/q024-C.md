Chrysippus defines fate as a "natural ordering of all things from eternity, one following on another" whose interweaving is inviolable (ἀπαραβάτου… ἐπιπλοκῆς) [ad59899c-4c0e-4d0b-8825-6ee05e34fb19]; Stoic fate is an unalterable series of causes (εἱρμός… αἰτιῶν ἀπαράβατος) [a809b1d6-b234-5831-b699-6a3ad89fdd81]. Alexander reports the Stoic world-picture in which every event is necessarily bound to a prior cause and nothing happens without a cause [29393616-e446-52a5-aa22-5ad016f6a0e0]. To keep fate yet escape necessity, Chrysippus distinguishes "perfect and principal" from "auxiliary and proximate" causes: fate operates through the latter, so assent remains "in our power" because its principal cause is our own nature (the cylinder analogy) [fc50aff8-32e5-443b-950d-35ad89b15d96; 64cca2fc-2fe6-466e-8443-e836e02bcc83; concept_cylinder_analogy_chrysippus_e5f6g7h8].

Alexander differs on three points. (1) He denies that whatever precedes something is its cause or that whatever follows has its cause in what precedes (night is not the cause of day), so the "chain" (ἁλύσεως δίκην) is not needed to secure "nothing without a cause" [d1cfa77d-b8d6-5e05-8d4f-dd1d91d24906]; the KG reconstructs this as separating necessary conditions from necessitating causes [argument_chain_causation_critique_alex]. (2) He holds that what depends on us requires being masters of both doing and not doing, not following external surrounding causes; the Stoic view abolishes deliberation and thus τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν [28bc2223-dabd-4bb0-933f-faa0fbcc2ef9; debate_alexander_stoics_determinism]. (3) The human being is the cause and "principle" (ἀρχή) of his actions, and this does not make them uncaused [d570b896-73be-45f2-bd92-de1ace50cd0b]. Caveat: KG notes that Sharples says Alexander gives no full positive causal theory, and the corpus does not attest a developed "agent-causation" model [argument_common_cause_alex; argument_agent_causation_two_way_powers_alexander_q8r9s0t1].
