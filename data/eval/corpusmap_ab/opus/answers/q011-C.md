Aristotle's criterion of voluntariness (hekousion, EN III.1) is internal origin plus knowledge of particulars: acts done by force (βίᾳ) or through ignorance are involuntary and merit pardon; voluntary acts merit praise or blame [argument_voluntary_involuntary_distinction_aristotle_g7h8i9j0]. He adds a two-sided "up to us": "where to act is up to us, not acting is too" (ἐν οἷς γὰρ ἐφ' ἡμῖν τὸ πράττειν, καὶ τὸ μὴ πράττειν) [1e336c5e-391c-5613-a59d-b83d2bcc5523]. Frede argues that Aristotle needs no distinct will: hekōn applies even to children and animals, so he has neither a notion of will nor of free will [argument_frede_2011_aristotle_no_will_no_free_will].

The Stoics held that prior necessitation (fate) does not rule out deserved praise and blame [scholarly_argument_salles_stoic_compatibilism_1]. Chrysippus' cylinder makes the impression only the proximate, external cause, while assent follows the agent's own "force and nature" [ac9ffaf1-a72c-5a5c-b8b3-adceec102ca4; 0338fcd0-3bd0-545f-9587-57231fedd860]. Brennan reads this as compatibilism: assent is fully determined, external compulsion excuses but internal causation by character does not [scholarly_argument_brennan_chrysippus_semantic_compatibilism]. So the Stoics keep Aristotle's internal/external contrast but drop his two-sided ability. Whether eph' hēmin is one-sided remains disputed [debate_stoic_compatibilism].

Peripatetics answered by reading Aristotle as an incompatibilist. Alexander argues that Stoic fate abolishes deliberation and hence "what is up to us", defined as being masters of both doing and not doing [28bc2223-dabd-4bb0-933f-faa0fbcc2ef9]. Bobzien argues that the "free-will problem" itself arose from a later-antique conflation of Aristotle's theory of choice with Stoic determinism [scholarly_argument_bobzien_origin_of_free_will_problem_in_0].
