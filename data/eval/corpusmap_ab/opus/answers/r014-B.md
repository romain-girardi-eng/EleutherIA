Barclay. The corpus holds Barclay's Paul and the Gift (2015) only through its sequel, Paul and the Power of Grace (2020), described as a "synthetic sequel" [pub_barclay_2020_power_of_grace]. Barclay notes that Paul's grace/works antithesis (Rom 4.4-6; 9.6-13; 11.6) made Paul the spokesman for an unconditioned divine initiative. In his view this reading fed polemics against any human contribution [scholarly_argument_barclay_divine_grace_and_human_works_3]. The text cited is Rom 9.11-12: election comes before birth and "not from works but from the one who calls" [3841fde5-bcec-5275-bb91-eb13e0838d00; c79e1de9-e7cb-5677-ae74-9e93e74aa48c; scholar_barclay_j].

Barclay does not choose between predestination and synergism. He separates grace into six "perfections" and says long disputes come from perfecting grace differently. Pelagius did not believe in grace "less" than Augustine [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_six_perfections]. "Efficacy" runs on a scale, from grace as an aid to grace that replaces human agency [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_efficacy_spectrum_agency]. Paul perfects incongruity: grace is given without regard to worth and creates worth [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_incongruous_gift]. Grace is "unconditioned" but not "unconditional," because it remakes the agent [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_unconditioned_not_unconditional; scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_undeserved_but_obliging]. The Spirit enables human agency and does not override it [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_spirit_enables_not_overrides]. On this account, divine and human agency are not rival contributions [scholarly_argument_barclay_pauline_paradox_of_divine_and__0].

Patristic context. Nagasawa argues that before Augustine nobody read Romans 9-11 as double predestination [scholarly_argument_nagasawa_predestination_in_romans_1]. He finds that the early fathers held a consistent synergism [scholarly_argument_nagasawa_free_will_and_divine_grace_in__0].

Käsemann. The corpus has no node for Käsemann. He appears only indirectly: Dettwiler tests Romans 6 against Käsemann's "eschatological reserve" [scholarly_argument_dettwiler_free_will_and_baptismal_transf_0]. Käsemann's reading of Romans 9 is not attested, so a direct comparison with Barclay cannot be made from this corpus.
