The EleutherIA corpus cannot document this question. The knowledge graph has a node for Tertullian's De anima (c. 210–213 CE, 58 chapters, "ed. Waszink, CCSL 2"). The node describes it as the earliest systematic Latin work on the soul, one that "engag[es] extensively with Stoic, Platonic, and medical sources". It also says plainly that no passages from De anima are in the database. The 31 passages once assigned to it actually come from Adversus Praxean [work_tertullian_de_anima]. So the corpus has no text of De anima 21. The candidate "chapter 21" passage is Adv. Prax. 21, a Trinitarian reading of John 1:1–3, and it has nothing to do with Stoic determinism [fcf35fba-506e-49ca-9ccc-0a2f66c778da]. A full-text search for chapter-21 wording found only Augustine, Pamphilus and Cassian.

The graph mentions Waszink only as the editor of the CCSL text [work_tertullian_de_anima; person_tertullian_d220]. It does not hold or summarise his commentary. The Tertullian node links De anima to traducianism and its consequences for inherited corruption and responsibility. Tertullian's explicit defence of liberum arbitrium is placed in Adversus Marcionem II.5–7, against Marcion's dualism [person_tertullian_d220; argument_tertullians_antimarcionite_argument_for_free_will_f49cad73]. Préaux goes further and calls Tertullian (with Cyprian) "strangely silent" on fate and providence, adding that no complete Latin treatment exists before Augustine [scholarly_argument_pr_aux_tertullian_and_cyprian_on_fate_3]. Jowers presents ante-Nicene appeals to providence and freedom as a single apologetic, anti-heretical complex [scholarly_argument_jowers_ante_nicene_providence_as_apologetic_complex].

The corpus therefore does not attest what De anima 21 argues against Stoic determinism, or how Waszink interprets it.
