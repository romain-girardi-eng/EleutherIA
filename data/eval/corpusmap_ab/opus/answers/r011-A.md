ANSWER:
Partly. The problem (V.pr.3) is that certain knowledge of X makes X necessary, else God errs; yet foreknowing "uncertain" futures as uncertain would be no better than Tiresias' "whatever I say will or will not be" [argument_boethius_foreknowledge_problem][passage_boethius_cons_112].

The solution has two layers. (1) Eternity: knowledge follows the knower's mode [argument_boethius_modes_cognition]. God's aeternitas, "interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio", is distinct from the world's perpetuity [concept_aeternitas_boethius_g2h3i4j5][37fc51d7-b90b-5c1f-a92a-3a2c40df88d7][passage_boethius_cons_125]. So God has not "praescientia" but "scientia numquam deficientis instantiae": seeing does not necessitate, just as our seeing a man walk does not [passage_boethius_cons_125][passage_boethius_cons_126]. This removes the temporal-priority form of the worry [argument_boethian_solution_divine_eternity_k1l2m3n4].

(2) Eternity alone does not finish the job, and Boethius admits it. Pressed that what God sees "non evenire non posse", he grants the event is "necessarium" relative to divine knowledge. He answers with a separate distinction: simple necessity versus necessitas condicionis [passage_boethius_cons_126][concept_duplex_necessitas_boethius_i4j5k6l7]. Free acts are necessary only "per condicionem divinae notionis", and in their own nature they could have failed to occur [passage_boethius_cons_127]. He even voices the remaining objection, "quid igitur refert non esse necessaria", if they happen with the force of necessity anyway [passage_boethius_cons_127]. He then concludes that freedom, rewards and prayer remain [passage_boethius_cons_129].

Verdict: eternity changes the knowledge relation from foreknowledge to presence. The logical point rests on the conditional/simple distinction, which works just as well for present knowledge. The corpus names later critics (Sorabji, Marenbon) but does not set out their criticisms [argument_boethius_modes_cognition].
