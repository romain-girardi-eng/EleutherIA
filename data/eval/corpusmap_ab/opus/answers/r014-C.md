Barclay treats this as an "incongruous" gift: it is given without regard to worth and creates worth [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_incongruous_gift].
- He separates grace into six independent "perfections". On this view, Augustine and Pelagius did not disagree about how much grace there is; they disagreed about which aspect of grace they pushed furthest [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_six_perfections].
- Grace is "unconditioned but not unconditional": it requires no prior worth, but it is meant to change the person who receives it [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_unconditioned_not_unconditional].
- The Spirit enables the believer's own agency rather than overriding it ("no puppets") [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_spirit_enables_not_overrides]. Barclay also lays out a range of views, from grace as an aid to grace replacing human agency altogether [scholarly_argument_barclay_2020_efficacy_spectrum_agency].
- Overall, Barclay is neither a strict predestinarian nor a simple synergist. He reads Paul's statements about agency as paradoxical but not incoherent [scholarly_argument_barclay_divine_and_human_agency_in_pau_0].
