Chrysippus, as reported by Alexander, held that the cosmos is governed by an everlasting "sequence and order" (εἱρμός, τάξις) in which earlier things cause later ones. Every event is followed by something that "depends on it of necessity as on a cause", and nothing is or comes to be without a cause [29393616-e446-52a5-aa22-5ad016f6a0e0]. Fate is "an unalterable sequence of causes" [a809b1d6-b234-5831-b699-6a3ad89fdd81]. Chrysippus wanted to avoid necessity, so he distinguished "perfect and principal" causes from "auxiliary and proximate" ones. Fate works only through the second kind. An impression starts assent, but the rest comes from the agent's own nature, as with the cylinder [fc50aff8-32e5-443b-950d-35ad89b15d96][0a1bf1ae-9888-4a9e-8da8-9ff3ae4c8f7e][argument_chrysippus_causal_taxonomy].

Alexander rejects this chain model. He calls it plainly false that whatever follows something has its cause in it, or that whatever precedes something is its cause. Night does not cause day, yet neither happens without a cause. So the Stoic "sequence of causes" is not what guarantees that nothing is uncaused [d1cfa77d-b8d6-5e05-8d4f-dd1d91d24906][argument_chain_causation_critique_alex]. The KG reconstructs his point as a distinction between necessary and sufficient conditions, which the Stoics are said to conflate [argument_chain_causation_critique_alex]. He also denies the Stoic claim that acting differently in the same circumstances brings in uncaused motion [603fb3ae-431c-57cd-b4bd-a02765aeacf8]. Using Aristotle's four causes, he makes fate one efficient cause among others, next to nature, choice and chance [argument_fate_efficient_cause_alex]. He holds that the future is not necessitated, even by divine foreknowledge [argument_causal_asymmetry_alex]. Finally, he treats the agent's two-way rational power as a cause of choice that nothing prior fully determines [person_alexander_aphrodisias_fl200ce_n5o6p7q8].
