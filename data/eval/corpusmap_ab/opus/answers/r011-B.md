ANSWER:
Partly, and the text itself shows where it falls short. The problem in V.pr.3 runs: if God knows something will happen, it happens necessarily, so freedom is lost. If God's knowledge were instead uncertain, it would be no better than Tiresias' "it will be or it won't" [argument_boethius_foreknowledge_problem; passage_boethius_cons_112].

Boethius answers in two steps:
1. Knowledge follows the knower's mode, not the thing known. God's eternity is the "interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio" [passage_boethius_cons_123; argument_boethius_modes_cognition]. So God's "foreknowledge" is really knowledge of things as present. It no more forces them than our seeing a man walk forces him to walk [passage_boethius_cons_126; concept_aeternitas_boethius_g2h3i4j5].
2. Necessity comes in two kinds, simplex and condicionis. Divine knowledge imposes only the conditional kind: the act is necessary when referred to God's seeing, but free "in sua natura" [passage_boethius_cons_126; passage_boethius_cons_127; concept_duplex_necessitas_boethius_i4j5k6l7].

Eternity also removes the worry that God's knowledge changes as we change our minds, or that our future acts cause it. God anticipates all our changes "uno ictu" [passage_boethius_cons_128]. This is why rewards, punishments and prayer are kept [passage_boethius_cons_129].

The limit: Boethius concedes the objector's point that what God sees "non evenire non posse" [passage_boethius_cons_126]. The real work is done by the two kinds of necessity, not by timelessness alone. Eternity mainly explains why God's knowledge is certain without causing anything. Later thinkers judged it needed refining: Scotus with synchronic contingency, Ockham with hard and soft facts, Molina with middle knowledge [debate_divine_foreknowledge_future_contingents_a7b8c9d0]. The corpus records no detailed ancient critique of the eternity solution itself.
