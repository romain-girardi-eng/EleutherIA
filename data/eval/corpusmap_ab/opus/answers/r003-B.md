The KG treats Sir 15:11-20 (c. 180 BCE) as the fullest Second Temple statement of bechirah, i.e. free choice [concept_bechirah_c1d2e3f4]. It calls it one of the earliest structurally complete arguments for human moral agency in Jewish wisdom literature, written before the technical terms autexousion and liberum arbitrium existed [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]. The argument has four steps:
(1) vv. 11-12 forbid blaming one's sin on God, because God does not do what he hates;
(2) v. 14 says God "left them in the power of their own deliberation" (ἐν χειρὶ διαβουλίου αὐτοῦ). Here the Greek translator rendered yetzer, a neutral deliberative faculty, as διαβούλιον;
(3) v. 17 echoes Deut 30:19: "life and death" stand before each person;
(4) vv. 18-20 hold that God's omniscience does not cause sin [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0].
Scholars in the KG read the passage in the following ways:
- Brand: Ben Sira is "responding to deterministic approaches", possibly a Judean form of Hellenistic (Stoic) determinism. She also notes that his thought is inconsistent; Sir 33:7-15 is more deterministic [scholarly_argument_brand_ben_sira_free_will_theodicy].
- Brand: Ben Sira and Philo are the only Second Temple texts that face the problem of sin directly, and both place it in human hands [scholarly_argument_brand_philo_free_will_amid_pessimism].
- Klawans: the passage anticipates the "relative libertarianism" of the Sadducees as Josephus reports it (War 2.164-65) [scholarly_argument_klawans_2012_sadducee_libertarianism_matches_ben_sira; group_sadducees_a5b6c7d8].
- Maston: it is a two-ways model that puts human agency first [scholarly_argument_maston_ben_sira_and_human_agency_1], and Paul critiques it in Rom 7 [scholarly_argument_maston_paul_s_critique_of_two_ways_tr_3].
Caveat: the corpus holds no passage of Sirach itself [work_sirach_a3b4c5d6]. The Greek and the claimed reception (Justin, Clement) come only from KG descriptions and are not checked against the texts.
