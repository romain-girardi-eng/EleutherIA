Aristotle. In EN III.1-5, an action is voluntary when its origin (archē) lies in the agent and the agent knows the particular circumstances. Compulsion and ignorance of particulars make it involuntary. Prohairesis concerns only what is up to us [argument_aristotles_voluntary_action_argument_eph_hemin_e5dd9188]. The key formula is two-sided: "ἐν οἷς γὰρ ἐφ' ἡμῖν τὸ πράττειν, καὶ τὸ μὴ πράττειν" (1113b7-8) [1e336c5e-391c-5613-a59d-b83d2bcc5523]. Responsibility reaches our character too: if each person is somehow a cause of his own disposition, he is also somehow a cause of how the good appears to him [28b16b62-34cb-4db0-a445-c778d696cb4e].

Stoics. Chrysippus keeps universal fate but separates proximate external causes from principal internal ones. In the cylinder analogy, an impression starts assent, but how we assent depends on our own nature [17c14800-34a6-50b7-b886-17aa9bc54ac2; 0338fcd0-3bd0-545f-9587-57231fedd860; c1be4fe2-b312-5318-ac8f-11d46cf99c73]. Epictetus narrows what is eph' hēmin to inner acts: judgement, impulse, desire and aversion. Body, property and office are not up to us [dd5cfa96-1255-5ec7-8772-7f5ebe25b298]. Only assent and impulse are unhindered. Bodily execution can be blocked [d79367a3-ab9b-5a90-a869-e7da5176cff7]. What is up to us is the "use of impressions" [00b50707-7fa3-5a84-bcd7-97acb64e12ae].
