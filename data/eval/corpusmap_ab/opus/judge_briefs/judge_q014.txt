ANSWER:
The KG frames "intellectualism vs voluntarism" as a medieval fault line with ancient roots [debate_intellectualism_vs_voluntarism_w3x4y5z6]. For antiquity, the corpus records a scholarly dispute rather than an explicit ancient debate.

Intellectualist reading. Socratic intellectualism holds that virtue is knowledge and no one does wrong willingly [concept_socratic_intellectualism_f6g7h8i9]. Dihle argues the Greeks "never developed a distinct concept of will": action is explained by rational cognition, and prohairesis presupposes knowledge of a determinate object. On this view, no Greek term matches Augustinian voluntas [argument_dihle_1982_greek_intellectualism_thesis]. In Aristotle, deliberation, prohairesis and boulesis stay intellectual, and akrasia is a failure of cognition [synthesis_dihle1982_lec2_greek_intellectualism_action]. Aristotle grounds praise and blame in the voluntary (hekousion): the origin of the act lies in the agent, who knows the particulars [argument_voluntary_involuntary_distinction_aristotle_g7h8i9j0]. He also says that where acting is "up to us" (eph' hēmin), so is not acting [1e336c5e-391c-5613-a59d-b83d2bcc5523], and that each person is in some way responsible for their own appearance of the good [28b16b62-34cb-4db0-a445-c778d696cb4e]. Dihle counts Stoic assent (synkatathesis) as "voluntary, yet necessitated," so it is still cognitive [argument_dihle_1982_synkatathesis_is_cognitive_not_volitional]. He warns that translating these Greek terms as "will" is anachronistic. Frede reverses the charge: Dihle's own idea of will is post-Cartesian [argument_dihle_1982_critique_anachronism_in_attribution_will_to_greeks].

Rejecting the alternative. Voelke argues that in Stoic rationalism "reason is will," which removes the choice between voluntarism and intellectualism [scholarly_argument_voelke_stoic_reason_is_will]. Seneca fits this view. He says right action depends on a right voluntas, which depends on a right disposition of mind and correct judgement [403a1e12-704c-4c70-8f74-843d4eb1fbc9]. Yet he also says "velle non discitur" (willing is not learned) [396e9c14-0988-44c8-b6f9-f0415136cefe].

ANSWER:
In the corpus, "intellectualism vs voluntarism" is a medieval distinction projected back onto antiquity [debate_intellectualism_vs_voluntarism_w3x4y5z6]. Medieval voluntarism (Scotus, Ockham) holds that the will can choose against the intellect's judgment of the best, a self-determining power [concept_voluntarism_medieval_m7n8o9p0; argument_scotuss_voluntarism_501f1bf6; argument_ockhams_rejection_of_necessitation_33c255bf].

Ancient ethics starts from Socratic intellectualism: virtue is knowledge and no one does wrong willingly [concept_socratic_intellectualism_f6g7h8i9], so action reduces to cognition of the good [synthesis_dihle1982_lec2_greek_intellectualism_action]. Epictetus keeps this: no one assents to what seems false, and every soul is deprived of truth unwillingly [d870c668-956a-5fb0-81b4-61f03a62e102; 347cf7bb-095e-53a3-ade7-a51a4191856e].

Aristotle grounds responsibility in voluntary action (origin in the agent, knowledge of particulars) [argument_voluntary_involuntary_distinction_aristotle_g7h8i9j0]. He holds that acting and not acting are both "up to us" [1e336c5e-391c-5613-a59d-b83d2bcc5523], and that we are partly responsible for how the good appears to us [28b16b62-34cb-4db0-a445-c778d696cb4e]. Dihle says prohairesis and boulesis still presuppose cognition, and that akrasia is a failure of cognition [synthesis_dihle1982_lec2_greek_intellectualism_action].

Dihle's thesis is that the Greeks never developed a distinct concept of will [argument_dihle_1982_greek_intellectualism_thesis; pub_dihle_1982_theory_of_will]. He reads Stoic synkatathesis as "voluntary, yet necessitated" and still cognitive [argument_dihle_1982_synkatathesis_is_cognitive_not_volitional]. He calls ascribing a will to the Greeks anachronistic [argument_dihle_1982_critique_anachronism_in_attribution_will_to_greeks]. Voelke rejects the either/or itself: in Stoic rationalism, reason is will [scholarly_argument_voelke_stoic_reason_is_will]. A will that conflicts with itself appears with Augustine [312d556f-781c-574d-801e-3a9c348ae516].

The KG frames intellectualism vs voluntarism as a medieval debate (Aquinas vs Scotus/Ockham) whose roots lie in antiquity [debate_intellectualism_vs_voluntarism_w3x4y5z6; concept_intellectualism_medieval_i3j4k5l6; concept_voluntarism_medieval_m7n8o9p0].

Intellectualist pole: Socrates holds that virtue is knowledge and no one errs willingly (Protagoras 358c–d), so wrongdoing is ignorance [concept_socratic_intellectualism_f6g7h8i9; debate_intellectualism_vs_voluntarism_w3x4y5z6]. Epictetus is the strongest corpus witness: nobody wants to assent to falsehood, and "every soul is unwillingly deprived of truth" [d870c668-956a-5fb0-81b4-61f03a62e102]. People assent to what appears true, so error is involuntary [347cf7bb-095e-53a3-ade7-a51a4191856e].

Complications: Aristotle's analysis of akrasia (NE VII) separates knowing the good from being moved by it [concept_akrasia_weakness_of_will; debate_intellectualism_vs_voluntarism_w3x4y5z6]. He also states the two-sided "up to us" [88ac0d42-994e-5dee-9df0-7069da06cc60]. Augustine places conflict inside the will itself, not between reason and passion [312d556f-781c-574d-801e-3a9c348ae516].

Scholarship: Dihle argues the Greeks never formed a distinct concept of will, so Greek thought is structurally intellectualist [argument_dihle_1982_greek_intellectualism_thesis; concept_greek_intellectualism_dihle]. On his reading, Stoic assent is "voluntary, yet necessitated" and remains cognitive [argument_dihle_1982_synkatathesis_is_cognitive_not_volitional]. Voelke rejects the either/or itself: in Stoic rationalism "reason is will" [scholarly_argument_voelke_stoic_reason_is_will].
