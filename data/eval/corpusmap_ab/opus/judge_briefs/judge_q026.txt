QUESTION: Origen and Justin Martyr on autexousion: continuity and divergence
Reference claims a good answer should support:
- Justin grounds free choice (proairesis eleuthera) in the denial of fate, arguing that without it praise and blame would be meaningless.
- Origen devotes a dedicated treatment 'On free will' (Peri autexousiou) to the question, calling it one of the most necessary problems.
- Origen derives autexousion from the doctrine of God's just judgement: because praise- and blame-worthy things are 'up to us' (to eph' hēmin), free will follows.

ANSWER:
Continuity. Both authors take autexousion as a gift of creation to all rational beings and use it against determinism. Justin says God made "both angels and humans" αὐτεξουσίους for righteous action, while keeping their self-determination intact under his judgments [7b029b3b-d680-4362-a0ea-8ef4b2aef215]. His angel-fall argument places the origin of evil in the misuse of that freedom and treats fate (εἱμαρμένη) as a demonic invention [argument_justin_angel_fall; 0b68baff-e316-4734-a491-acb5acdafee3]. Origen opens De Principiis III.1, "Περὶ αὐτεξουσίου", by appealing to the Church's teaching on God's just judgment and to τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν as the basis of praise and blame [baa51e4b-9ad7-54a1-a971-74de1631661e]. That matches Justin's link between freedom and eschatological retribution [synthesis_furst2022_justin_first_christian_freiheits_philosophy]. Hall holds that Origen built on changes Justin started [scholarly_argument_hall_justin_martyr_s_place_in_auton_8]. Frede traces the term from the Stoics (Musonius, Epictetus) into Christian use beginning with Justin [argument_frede_2011_autexousion_stoic_origin_then_christian].

Divergence. In Justin, freedom serves apologetics. He states it repeatedly and proves it from moral change, but he does not analyse it [synthesis_furst2022_justin_first_christian_freiheits_philosophy]. Bobzien notes that Justin does not use the technical exousia framework [scholarly_argument_bobzien_autexousion_as_philosophical_t_6]. Origen sets out to define its ἔννοια and writes the first systematic treatise on freedom rather than on fate [argument_furst_2022_origen_culmination_autexousion]. He grounds it in a theory of self-moved beings [7e1c8576-c630-5099-8e98-082343bca3c6] and explains it "along Stoic lines" inside a Platonist framework [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_s_doctrine_of_free_will_5]. He also changes the opponent. Justin targets Stoic fate and demons. Origen targets Gnostic doctrines of fixed "natures" and astral determinism [scholarly_argument_sytsma_anti_determinism_idle_argument_deprinc_iii; 96064a2b-fbf5-56de-b760-0fd93c9d66b8]. Origen adds that demons keep their freedom permanently and that souls can fall and return [synthesis_frede2011_ch7_origen]. Fürst treats this as Origen developing Justin's first step toward an ontology of freedom [synthesis_furst2022_justin_first_christian_freiheits_philosophy].

ANSWER:
Continuity. Both ground autexousion in creation and in divine justice. Justin says God made angels and humans "αὐτεξουσίους πρὸς δικαιοπραξίαν" and judges "πεφυλαγμένου τοῦ αὐτεξουσίου" [7b029b3b-d680-4362-a0ea-8ef4b2aef215]. He pairs it with "ἐλευθέρᾳ προαιρέσει" so that reward and punishment are deserved [ad0049c4-8fa3-417c-9f02-42a98b0a862f]. Origen opens De Principiis III.1 with the same link: the Church's teaching on God's just judgment assumes that what deserves praise and blame is ἐφ' ἡμῖν [f7520632-f6f5-5d4d-b18f-c81dca177677; baa51e4b-9ad7-54a1-a971-74de1631661e]. Both use a reductio against fatalism [argument_justin_antifatalism]. Both deny that foreknowledge is a cause: Justin in 1 Apol. 44 and Dial. 140-141 [argument_justin_prophecy_freedom; 9a8ffeae-8eab-445f-9475-5071fc3590f4], and Origen in "ἡ πρόγνωσις… οὐκ ἔστιν αἰτία" [4bf277af-bdd7-55d0-b50b-113e788ec5f7]. The KG treats Justin's argument as an anticipation of Origen's [argument_justin_prophecy_freedom].

Divergence. Justin argues as an apologist and in outline, with Platonic support (Rep. 617e) [argument_justin_antifatalism]. Origen builds a technical account in a Stoic-Epictetan frame. External impressions are not ἐφ' ἡμῖν. Reason judges them, which he calls "τῆς αὐτεξουσίου ἡμῶν κρίσεως", and this sets rational animals apart from other animals [3e767176-490c-58a3-a173-c7e10e5d85a9; 11748b4e-d973-58b0-bc50-fdcad4cddfca]. He rejects blaming externals, as if we were "ξύλοις καὶ λίθοις", and blaming our bare constitution [5c8ff8f7-8ca3-5853-89b6-46474854f639]. He also works through hard scriptural texts, joining τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν with God's knowledge (Rom 9) [6fa086c9-f553-52a0-bc26-80b6e2b901bb]. Scholars in the KG see Justin as the term's Christian point of entry (Frede) and the 2nd-century apologists as the base for Origen's "Freiheitsmetaphysik" (Fürst) [argument_frede_2011_autexousion_stoic_origin_then_christian; argument_furst_2022_christian_philosophers_freedom_innovation]. Bobzien notes autexousion became a standard philosophical term only from the late 2nd century [scholarly_argument_bobzien_autexousion_as_philosophical_t_6]. The corpus returned no Greek text for 1 Apol. 43-44 itself.

ANSWER:
**Continuity.** Autexousion, a term of Stoic origin (Musonius, Epictetus), enters Christian literature with Justin; Origen inherits it [argument_frede_2011_autexousion_stoic_origin_then_christian; scholarly_argument_frede_autexousion_terminology_4]. Hall sees Origen building on changes Justin started [scholarly_argument_hall_justin_martyr_s_place_in_auton_8]. Both tie self-determination to God's just judgment. Justin says God made angels and humans "αὐτεξουσίους πρὸς δικαιοπραξίαν" and judges them "πεφυλαγμένου μέντοι τοῦ αὐτεξουσίου" [7b029b3b-d680-4362-a0ea-8ef4b2aef215]. In 2 Apol. 6(7).5, free will grounds eternal retribution, against Stoic fate [scholarly_argument_minns_as_justin_s_term_for_free_will_2; argument_justin_angel_fall]. Origen starts his Περὶ αὐτεξουσίου from the church teaching on God's just judgment and from τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν [baa51e4b-9ad7-54a1-a971-74de1631661e]. Both apply freedom to angels and demons too, and both oppose fatalism [argument_justin_angel_fall; synthesis_frede2011_ch7_origen]. Fürst calls Justin's emphasis on freedom shown by moral change "the first step" toward the ontology of freedom that Origen developed [synthesis_furst2022_justin_first_christian_freiheits_philosophy].

**Divergence.** For Justin, autexousion is an apologetic axiom, used in a few passages (1 Apol. 43-44; 2 Apol. 5-7; Dial. 88, 102, 141) to answer fate [argument_justin_martyrs_apologetic_argument_for_free_will_45c2dde2]. Justin draws on Carneadean and Middle Platonist material [synthesis_amand1945_justin_first_christian_carneadean_user; argument_justin_equals_alcinous_heimarmene]. Sin and grace play no role in his theology [scholarly_argument_andresen_sin_and_grace_in_justin_s_theo_3]. Origen makes the term a central technical concept: he asks for its ἔννοια and writes the first systematic treatise "On Freedom" [argument_furst_2022_origen_culmination_autexousion; synthesis_frede2011_ch7_origen]. Frede finds Origen's content largely Epictetan Stoic, aimed at Gnostics and astral determinism. Origen's departures from the Stoics come from his Platonism: he equates autexousion with eph' hēmin, and he holds that freedom is never lost, which allows fall and return (apokatastasis) [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_s_doctrine_of_free_will_5; synthesis_frede2011_ch7_origen]. Bobzien's evidence does not settle how technical Justin's usage was [scholarly_argument_bobzien_autexousion_as_philosophical_t_6].
