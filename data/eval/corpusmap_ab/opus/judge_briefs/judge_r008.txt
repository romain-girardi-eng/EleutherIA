QUESTION: Plotinus Ennead VI.8 on the freedom of the One and its Patristic appropriation
Reference claims a good answer should support:
- Plotinus opens VI.8 by asking whether 'what is up to us' (to eph' auto) can be predicated even of the gods and of the First, beyond all things.
- Plotinus refuses to locate human self-determination (to eph' hemin) in mere impulse (horme) or appetite (orexis), which children, beasts, and the mad also have; it must rest on right reason and right desire.
- Plotinus argues that the One's power is not the power for opposites; its making of itself is a single act identical with its will (boulesis), so that the One's will and its substance (ousia) are not distinct.

ANSWER:
Ennead VI.8 [39], "On the Voluntary and the Will of the One", asks whether anything is "up to" the gods and not only to humans [work_plotinus_ennead_vi_8_d8b9c5a4; 2cef74de-5f4c-54e4-a117-9c91addb05fe]. Plotinus says autexousion is not an accident of the One. It is reached by abstracting from lower kinds of self-determination, and in the end even "up to itself" has to be set aside [1019985d-051e-483c-9483-f9b60a748123]. Frede reads the treatise as a four-level ranking of freedom: embodied human, soul, intellect and the One. Each level is a weaker copy of the one above, and the One's unconditioned will is the model for all freedom [synthesis_frede2011_ch8_plotinus; argument_frede_2011_plotinus_hierarchized_freedom]. Plotinus rejects the "bold argument" that the One acts from the necessity of its nature (VI.8.7) [argument_frede_2011_plotinus_divine_will_not_judeo_christian]. Narbonne thinks the opponent was Gnostic. He argues that the One's "will wills nothing" and is self-determination, not the freedom of things that could be otherwise [scholarly_argument_narbonne_god_as_causa_sui_and_self_dete_6]. Dihle holds that this "will" stays intellectualist [argument_dihle_1982_plotinus_remains_intellectualist].

On the Patristic side, the corpus documents no direct Christian borrowing from VI.8. Frede argues against Armstrong that God acting by a free will is not a Judaeo-Christian idea imported into Plotinus. The only possible influence he allows runs from Origen to Plotinus, not the other way [argument_frede_2011_plotinus_divine_will_not_judeo_christian]. Dihle notes that Greek Christian authors (Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Nemesius) used proairesis, autexousion and thelesis but kept Greek categories. In his view the real break came later, from Latin Christianity [synthesis_dihle1982_lec5_late_antiquity_plotinus_no_will]. Christian autexousion has its own lineage in Origen's De Principiis III.1 and Gregory's image-of-God argument. None of these nodes links them to VI.8 [concept_autexousion_christian_freedom_u1v2w3x4; argument_origens_de_principiis_argument_for_free_will_93d043fc; argument_gregory_image_dei_autexousion].

ANSWER:
Ennead VI.8 [39], "On the Voluntary and the Will of the One", opens by asking whether anything is "in the power" (eph' hemin) even of the gods, and not only of weak humans [2cef74de-5f4c-54e4-a117-9c91addb05fe; work_plotinus_ennead_vi_8_d8b9c5a4]. In VI.8.8 Plotinus says self-determination (autexousion) is not an accident of the One. We reach it by abstracting from lower cases of self-determination. Strictly speaking, even "in its power" and "autexousion" are posterior to it [1019985d-051e-483c-9483-f9b60a748123]. Frede reads the treatise as a four-level hierarchy (embodied human, soul, intellect, One). Intellect is free because it cannot act otherwise, and the One is the archetype of all freedom [argument_frede_2011_plotinus_hierarchized_freedom; synthesis_frede2011_ch8_plotinus]. Narbonne holds that the One's "will" wills nothing: it is self-assertion, not the choice of contingent options. He also thinks Plotinus took the causa sui vocabulary from Gnostic opponents [scholarly_argument_narbonne_god_as_causa_sui_and_self_dete_6]. Dihle calls this "will" intellectualist and contrasts it with Augustine's separate faculty of will [argument_dihle_1982_plotinus_remains_intellectualist].

On Christian appropriation, the corpus gives only scholarly framing. Frede, against Armstrong, denies that a freely willing God is a Judeo-Christian import in VI.8. He allows at most an influence from Origen [argument_frede_2011_plotinus_divine_will_not_judeo_christian]. Fürst makes Origen, not Plotinus, the first to make freedom a principle of being. He places Gregory of Nyssa beside Plotinus, Porphyry and Proclus as Platonists who define God as freedom [argument_furst_2022_origen_first_freedom_thinker; synthesis_furst2022_after_origen_new_freedom_debate]. Gill reports Frede's lineage running to Origen and Augustine [scholarly_argument_gill_later_ancient_reception_of_sto_3]. No Patristic passage that uses VI.8 directly is attested in the corpus.

ANSWER:
Ennead VI.8 [39], "On the Voluntary and the Will of the One", is a late treatise (c. 268 CE). It carries the vocabulary of eph' hēmin and autexousion over from creatures to the One. On this reading, freedom means self-willing, not contingency [work_plotinus_ennead_vi_8_d8b9c5a4]. The treatise opens by asking whether "being in one's power" applies to the gods and to the first principle "above all" [70cbd2f3-edb7-5bd0-b827-08a8f14a2c10]. In human beings, what is in our power is traced back to virtue and intellect, which are "masterless" [bacfa1ec-c6ef-41c1-8fd1-a98eceb63dae; passage_plotinus_vi_8_6]. Autexousion is not an accident of the One. It is reached by removing the opposites found in derivative freedoms, and in the end even "being in its own power" must be set aside as posterior to it [1019985d-051e-483c-9483-f9b60a748123; passage_plotinus_vi_8_8].

Scholars disagree about how to read this. Frede sees four levels of freedom, with the One's unconditioned will as the archetype [synthesis_frede2011_ch8_plotinus; argument_frede_2011_plotinus_hierarchized_freedom]. Against Armstrong, Frede also argues that the idea of a free divine will is not a Christian import, though some influence from Origen remains possible [argument_frede_2011_plotinus_divine_will_not_judeo_christian]. Dihle replies that even the One's "will" stays intellectualist [argument_dihle_1982_plotinus_remains_intellectualist].

On the Patristic appropriation, the knowledge graph only states that VI.8 was a source for Gregory of Nyssa, Pseudo-Dionysius and Maximus [work_plotinus_ennead_vi_8_d8b9c5a4]. No edge or passage retrieved shows that borrowing. Gregory bases human autexousion on the imago Dei, which reflects a God free from compulsion [argument_gregory_image_dei_autexousion; concept_autexousion_christian_freedom_u1v2w3x4]. That is a parallel, but no retrieved node or passage documents a direct dependence on Plotinus.
