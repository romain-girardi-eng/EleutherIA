ANSWER:
Origen's argument (De Princ. III.1, Peri autexousiou): (1) a hierarchy of movers. Some things, like stones and wood, are moved from outside; others, like animals and plants, carry the cause of their motion within themselves [7e1c8576-c630-5099-8e98-082343bca3c6; argument_origens_de_principiis_argument_for_free_will_93d043fc]. (2) External impressions are not ἐφ' ἡμῖν, but judging how to use them is the work of "the reason within us, that is, our self-determining judgment (τῆς αὐτεξουσίου ἡμῶν κρίσεως)" [3e767176-490c-58a3-a173-c7e10e5d85a9]. (3) Introspection shows that nothing happens before the mind gives its assent, as before a judge in the heart [81e26db7-ddeb-5169-b72b-e64f7d1877f2]. (4) Blaming externals makes us like wood and stones [ae853539-5bf0-592e-9323-f4dad81d7fc8]. Fürst counts Stoic εἱμαρμένη (III.1.4 ff.) as one of three fronts, alongside astrology and Gnosticism. In his reading, Origen accepts the Stoic causal framework but keeps genuine alternatives [argument_furst_2022_origen_against_three_determinisms].

Frede: Origen explains eph' hemin "along standard Stoic lines". He uses this Stoic doctrine against astral determinism and Gnosticism, not to correct the Stoics [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_s_doctrine_of_free_will_5]. His terminology is "through and through Stoic", mostly found in Epictetus. Where he departs from the Stoics, the source is his Platonism, not his Christianity. So nothing radically new comes from Christianity [argument_frede_2011_origen_stoic_christianity_anti_gnostic].

Argument of De Principiis III.1. Origen ranks moving things: inanimate things moved from outside, animals and plants moved from within, and rational beings who judge their impressions [7e1c8576-c630-5099-8e98-082343bca3c6; argument_origens_de_principiis_argument_for_free_will_93d043fc]. What happens to us from outside is "not ἐφ' ἡμῖν". Deciding how to use it is the work of reason, "our self-determining judgment" (τῆς αὐτεξουσίου ἡμῶν κρίσεως) [3e767176-490c-58a3-a173-c7e10e5d85a9]. Introspection confirms this: nothing happens until the mind assents, and it rules like a judge in "the tribunal of our heart" [81e26db7-ddeb-5169-b72b-e64f7d1877f2]. Blaming external causes makes us like wood and stones [ae853539-5bf0-592e-9323-f4dad81d7fc8]. Fürst counts Stoic εἱμαρμένη as one of three fronts. Origen accepts the Stoic causal framework but keeps genuine alternatives [argument_furst_2022_origen_against_three_determinisms].

Frede disputes the "against the Stoics" premise. Origen explains eph' hemin "along standard Stoic lines", using a "basically Stoic" doctrine against astral determinism and Gnostics, not to correct the Stoics [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_s_doctrine_of_free_will_5]. The treatise's terminology is "through and through Stoic", mostly Epictetan. Its departures (autexousion = eph' hemin, demons keep their freedom, fall and return) come from Platonism, not Christianity [argument_frede_2011_origen_stoic_christianity_anti_gnostic; synthesis_frede2011_ch7_origen].

ANSWER:
Origen opens III.1 by grounding the inquiry in divine judgment: Church preaching presupposes that praiseworthy and blameworthy acts are ἐφ' ἡμῖν, so αὐτεξούσιον must be clarified [f7520632-f6f5-5d4d-b18f-c81dca177677]. He then builds a graded taxonomy of motion. Some things are moved only from outside, others "from themselves", and ensouled beings move "of themselves" when an impression (φαντασία) triggers impulse (ὁρμή) [1d00993f-191d-585e-9a5c-1a09a5e07430]. The rational animal also has λόγος, which judges impressions and accepts or rejects them. Hence what befalls us from outside is "not ἐφ' ἡμῖν", but how we judge and use it is the work of "our self-determining judgment" [11748b4e-d973-58b0-bc50-fdcad4cddfca; 3e767176-490c-58a3-a173-c7e10e5d85a9]. Inner experience confirms this. Against anyone who says the external cause is irresistible, the same temptation leads one man to assent and another, whose reason is trained, to refuse. So the external object is not the self-sufficient cause (αὐτοτελὴς αἰτία) [caa0cde6-5047-5e61-9ac5-b21415c0323e; argument_origens_de_principiis_argument_for_free_will_93d043fc]. The KG notes that Origen draws on Epictetus and Alexander and adapts Carneadean anti-fatalism against εἱμαρμένη and Gnostic determinism [argument_origens_de_principiis_argument_for_free_will_93d043fc].

Frede calls the doctrine "basically Stoic", explained "along standard Stoic lines" and built into a Platonist outlook to answer astral determinism and Gnosticism. It is "not quite the Stoic view", but the differences come from Platonism, not Christianity [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_on_free_will_5]. Like Alexander, Origen equates freedom with things up to us to do or not to do. This freedom is part of rational nature and can never be lost, even by the devil [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_s_differences_from_stoi_6].

The corpus has no Bobzien assessment of Origen specifically. Her framework sets the early Stoic one-sided causative ἐφ' ἡμῖν against a two-sided potestative sense [scholarly_argument_bobzien_eph_hemin_what_depends_on_us_3]. The two-sided problem appears first with Alexander and later spreads into Christian schools [argument_bobzien_2001_b1_rise_fall_freedom_problem]. She also notes the one-sided sense survived as an alternative in 2nd–3rd-century writers [scholarly_argument_bobzien_historical_marginality_of_libe_7].
