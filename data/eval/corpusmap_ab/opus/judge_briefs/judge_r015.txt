QUESTION: Maximus Confessor Disputatio cum Pyrrho: natural will vs gnomic will (Bathrellos 2004)

--- ANSWER X ---
ANSWER:
In the Disputatio cum Pyrrho (Carthage, 645; PG 91, 287–354), Maximus argues against Pyrrhus that will (θέλημα) belongs to nature, not to the hypostasis. Every nature has its own power of willing, so Christ's two natures give him two natural wills, one divine and one human, which agree perfectly [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will; argument_maximus_two_wills; work_maximus_disp_pyrrho]. Pyrrhus's monothelite position is that two wills would either conflict or require two willing subjects, which would be Nestorian. On this view Christ has one divine will [argument_pyrrhus_one_will].

The natural will (θέλημα φυσικόν) is "a faculty desirous of what is in accordance with nature". It does not deliberate, and it is free because it conforms to nature [concept_thelema_physikon_natural_will]. The gnomic will (γνώμη / θέλημα γνωμικόν) is a deliberative mode of willing found in fallen creatures, marked by ignorance and hesitation. Maximus defines it as "deep-seated desire for those things within our power, whence arises choice". In his mature work it is a "mode (τρόπος) of use", not a principle of nature [concept_gnomic_will_gnome]. So Christ assumes a human natural will but has no gnomic will [argument_maximus_two_wills; argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. Maximus's early works had allowed Christ a γνώμη, and Blowers asks whether denying it has a soteriological cost [scholarly_argument_blowers_denial_gnomic_will_soteriological_cost]. The distinction contributed to the dyothelite settlement at Constantinople III (680–681) [debate_monothelite_dyothelite_controversy].

In the Disputatio cum Pyrrho (Carthage, July 645; PG 91, 287–354) Maximus answers Pyrrhus' monothelite dilemma: two wills would either conflict or be subordinated, and since every will belongs to a subject (hypostasis), two wills would mean two subjects, which is Nestorianizing. Hence one divine will [argument_pyrrhus_one_will; work_maximus_disp_pyrrho].

Maximus replies that will belongs to nature, not to hypostasis. Christ has two unconfused natures, so he has two natural wills, divine and human, in harmony [argument_maximus_two_wills; argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. The natural will (θέλημα φυσικόν) is the basic faculty of rational nature, "a faculty desirous of what is in accordance with nature" [concept_thelema_physikon_natural_will]. The gnomic will (γνώμη / θέλημα γνωμικόν) is not that faculty. It is a deliberative mode of willing marked by ignorance, hesitation and the possibility of error, typical of fallen rational agents. Christ therefore assumes a human natural will but no gnomic will [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will; argument_maximus_two_wills]. The KG puts the point this way: humans share τὸ θέλειν (the willing faculty) with Christ by nature, but not τὸ πῶς θέλειν (the manner of willing) [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. Blowers adds Maximus' definition of γνώμη as "deep-seated desire for things in our power, whence arises choice" (προαίρεσις) [scholarly_argument_blowers_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. The position was adopted at Constantinople III (680–681) [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will].

ANSWER:
In the Disputatio cum Pyrrho (Carthage, July 645; PG 91, 287–354) Maximus answers Pyrrhus's monothelite dilemma: two wills would either conflict or be subordinated, and since every will belongs to a hypostasis, two wills would mean two subjects [argument_pyrrhus_one_will; work_maximus_disp_pyrrho]. Maximus's reply rests on a distinction. The natural will (θέλημα φυσικόν) is a faculty of nature, not of the hypostasis. It is defined as "a faculty desirous of what is in accordance with nature" [concept_thelema_physikon_natural_will; argument_maximus_two_wills]. The gnomic will (γνώμη / θέλημα γνωμικόν) is not a natural power. It is a deliberative mode, or "how", of willing, marked by ignorance, hesitation and the possibility of error, and it belongs to fallen rational agents [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will; concept_gnomic_will_gnome]. In his mature Christology γνώμη becomes a "quality of willing" and a "mode (τρόπος) of use, not a principle of nature" [concept_gnomic_will_gnome].

Two conclusions follow. Christ has two natural wills, one divine and one human, united without conflict in the hypostasis of the Logos. He has no gnomic will, which also removes Pyrrhus's worry about conflict [argument_maximus_two_wills; argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. Constantinople III (680–681) adopted this position [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. Blowers gives a parallel modern reconstruction [scholarly_argument_blowers_natural_vs_gnomic_will].
