QUESTION: Clement of Alexandria Stromateis II.6-15: Stoic vs Christian conceptions of moral responsibility
Reference claims a good answer should support:
- Clement holds that the devil, being autexousios (self-determining), was able both to repent and to do wrong, so responsibility rests with the agent.
- Clement argues that vice is voluntary (the badness being akousion is rejected): the soul's own choice (hairesis) is judged justly, so that God remains not the cause of evil (kakias anaitios).
- Clement traces sins to proairesis and horme (choice and impulse) as their origin, with mistaken judgement sometimes prevailing.

ANSWER:
In Strom. II Clement takes over the Stoic psychology of assent (συγκατάθεσις) and puts it to work against Gnostic "natural" determinism [argument_clement_grace_synergy_assent; work_clement_stromateis].

Stoic material: he defines faith as "πρόληψις ἑκούσιος… θεοσεβείας συγκατάθεσις" [408ca550-86bf-5921-8b09-33dbbeaf5aa3]. He says that Platonists and Stoics alike place assents "ἐφ' ἡμῖν" [4031dce4-c46f-5731-b3ed-bed07228525d]. Every opinion, judgement and act of learning is an assent, and unbelief, as apostasy from faith, shows that assent is a real possibility [17d043b5-0b2c-55d1-b92e-6e6e05055c13]. The Logos calls everyone, "ὅτι ἐφ' ἡμῖν τὸ πείθεσθαι", and the people drawn respond "οὐχ ὡς αἴτια, ἀλλ' ὡς συναίτια" [8a7158d8-b842-5b2f-8cb6-d9ac68d184d1]. Löhr links this vocabulary to Chrysippus' distinction between internal and external causes [scholarly_argument_l_hr_clement_s_use_of_stoic_concept_3].

Christian shift: the target is belief and unbelief rather than virtue and vice. If faith were "φύσεως πλεονέκτημα", there could be no just reward, praise or blame. We would be moved like puppets (νευροσπαστουμένων), and repentance and baptism would make no sense [5a221307-b0c5-5f9a-87b3-771fbd2f312b]. Amand reads this as an argument from Carneades with Christian terms swapped in [argument_clement_alex_carneadean_glissement_faith_unbelief]. Responsibility also becomes a cooperation (synergy) with grace: God draws the soul without forcing it [argument_clement_grace_synergy_assent]. Ewing adds that Clement rejects the Stoic identification of providence with fate [scholarly_argument_ewing_stoic_pervasion].

Scholars question how well this works. Löhr calls the argument weak, because Basilides also defined faith as assent [scholarly_argument_l_hr_clement_s_use_of_stoic_concept_3]. Karfíková doubts whether the rejected alternative is really "equally possible" [scholarly_argument_karfikova_autexousion_prerequisite_not_gnostic_freedom]. Müller argues that Clement goes beyond Stoic freedom from the passions (apatheia) by making love the basis of freedom [scholarly_argument_mueller_clement_love_surpasses_apatheia_as_freedom].

ANSWER:
In Strom. II Clement takes over Stoic machinery to defend responsibility, but turns it to Christian ends. The anti-Gnostic premise appears at II.3.11: if faith is an advantage of nature (φύσεως πλεονέκτημα) rather than an achievement of choice (προαιρέσεως κατόρθωμα), neither believer nor unbeliever is answerable, praise and blame lapse, and we are moved like puppets (νευροσπαστουμένων) [5a221307-b0c5-5f9a-87b3-771fbd2f312b]. Amand reads this as a Carneadean argument with faith/unbelief substituted for virtue/vice [argument_clement_alex_carneadean_glissement_faith_unbelief].

The central Stoic tool is assent. Clement says that the Platonists and the Stoics alike hold assents to be up to us (ἐφ' ἡμῖν) [4031dce4-c46f-5731-b3ed-bed07228525d]. Every opinion and act of learning is an assent, and that assent is faith; unbelief, as a falling away from faith, shows that faith is possible [17d043b5-0b2c-55d1-b92e-6e6e05055c13]. Following Chrysippus's συναίτια, the Logos draws hearers as co-causes rather than as causes, while obedience stays up to us [8a7158d8-b842-5b2f-8cb6-d9ac68d184d1]. The KG describes this as a grace–freedom synergy [argument_clement_grace_synergy_assent].

II.15 sorts the voluntary into appetite, choice and thought. It separates error, misfortune and injustice, and puts readiness for education and obedience to the commandments within our power [15512370-1bd6-56f3-a8c2-d88ad954af3e]. Where Stoicism has no such category, II.15 adds forgiveness and repentance [d9c85f6f-4445-5c3a-8560-c75327f999c3], with transgressions up to us and remedied by fitting therapies [563ec79d-4239-5f02-aa84-7505d1493528].

Scholarly assessment: Löhr thinks the appeal to assent is weak, because Basilides also defined faith as assent [scholarly_argument_l_hr_clement_s_use_of_stoic_concept_3]. Karfíková treats αὐτεξούσιον as only the precondition of gnostic freedom [scholarly_argument_karfikova_autexousion_prerequisite_not_gnostic_freedom]. Müller argues that love goes beyond Stoic apatheia [scholarly_argument_mueller_clement_love_surpasses_apatheia_as_freedom].

ANSWER:
In Strom. II.6–15 Clement takes over Stoic action theory to ground a Christian account of responsibility centred on faith, grace and repentance [argument_clement_grace_synergy_assent].

1. Assent. The Stoic doctrine that assent (συγκατάθεσις) is "up to us" is taken up explicitly: Clement says that not only the Platonists but also the Stoics place assents ἐφ' ἡμῖν [4031dce4-c46f-5731-b3ed-bed07228525d]. Every opinion, judgement and act of learning is an assent, and that assent is faith. Unbelief, which is a falling away from faith, shows that assent is within our power [17d043b5-0b2c-55d1-b92e-6e6e05055c13]. Faith itself is a "voluntary preconception, an assent of piety" [408ca550-86bf-5921-8b09-33dbbeaf5aa3].

2. Co-causality. Clement uses the Stoic distinction between cause and co-cause: things drawn by the Spirit are drawn "not as causes but as co-causes" (συναίτια). Teaching is of no use unless the learner receives it, and the Logos calls everyone, even those who will not obey, because obeying is ἐφ' ἡμῖν [8a7158d8-b842-5b2f-8cb6-d9ac68d184d1]. Grace invites but does not compel: universal exhortation plus special help for those made worthy by their own choice [scholarly_argument_havrda_clement_s_model_of_divine_huma_0; scholarly_argument_telfer_grace_and_free_will_in_clement_4].

3. Passions and fear. Clement adopts the Stoic εὐλάβεια, a "reasonable avoidance", and argues that the commanded fear of God is rational, not a passion [b402e9f8-ac1a-5bbd-96f1-b3747c692923; d55384c9-bf4e-5b19-910f-fc362155f143].

4. Grading culpability. The voluntary is divided into what follows desire, choice or thought. Clement separates misfortune, sin (an involuntary wrong) and injustice (voluntary vice). He insists that both readiness for instruction and obedience to the commandments are ἐφ' ἡμῖν [15512370-1bd6-56f3-a8c2-d88ad954af3e; aa3cbcda-6fb2-5236-85d6-097ac2aae2dc]. Medea and Laius illustrate people who have handed themselves over to passion [129755ed-4785-5010-b83e-449735f78016].

5. The Christian addition is a framework of forgiveness the Stoics lack. Baptism forgives once, and repeated repentance after it is suspect [b58f180c-43c6-521e-9639-42249a57672b; 1b692a47-cbfa-5307-b120-e3ee3a412ae8]. Love covers sins [d9c85f6f-4445-5c3a-8560-c75327f999c3].
