QUESTION: Ben Sira 15:11-20 as locus classicus for free choice in Hellenistic Judaism
Reference claims a good answer should support:
- Sirach 15:17 ('Before man is life and death, and whichever he chooses shall be given him') is cited as openly attesting human free choice.

ANSWER:
In the KG, Sir 15:11–20 is called "one of the earliest structurally complete arguments for human moral agency" in Jewish wisdom. It predates the terms autexousion and liberum arbitrium [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]. The argument has four steps [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]:
(1) vv. 11–12 forbid blaming one's sin on God, since God does not do what he hates.
(2) v. 14 says God left humans "in the power of their own deliberation" (ἐν χειρὶ διαβουλίου αὐτοῦ). The grandson's Greek renders the Hebrew yēṣer with διαβούλιον.
(3) v. 17 echoes Deut 30:19 (life and death set before each person).
(4) vv. 18–20 hold that God's omniscience is compatible with human choice.
Brand also takes yēṣer here as a neutral capacity for free choice, not the later rabbinic "evil inclination" [scholarly_argument_brand_ben_sira_free_will_theodicy].

The motive is theodicy. Brand reads the passage as a reply to deterministic views, perhaps Hellenistic ones. She notes a more deterministic strand in Sir 33:7–15 [scholarly_argument_brand_ben_sira_free_will_theodicy]. For Brand, only the wisdom texts (Ben Sira and Philo) take on the problem of sin directly. Philo is far more pessimistic about it [scholarly_argument_brand_philo_free_will_amid_pessimism; scholarly_argument_brand_genre_determines_free_will_view].

Maston traces the passage to the Deuteronomic two-ways model, with the human as the main actor [scholarly_argument_maston_ben_sira_and_human_agency_1]. He argues that Romans 7 breaks down this model [scholarly_argument_maston_paul_s_critique_of_two_ways_tr_3]. Klawans connects Sir 15 to Josephus' Sadducees ("relative libertarianism") [scholarly_argument_klawans_2012_sadducee_libertarianism_matches_ben_sira].

ANSWER:
The KG treats Sir 15:11-20 (c. 180 BCE) as the fullest Second Temple statement of bechirah, i.e. free choice [concept_bechirah_c1d2e3f4]. It calls it one of the earliest structurally complete arguments for human moral agency in Jewish wisdom literature, written before the technical terms autexousion and liberum arbitrium existed [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]. The argument has four steps:
(1) vv. 11-12 forbid blaming one's sin on God, because God does not do what he hates;
(2) v. 14 says God "left them in the power of their own deliberation" (ἐν χειρὶ διαβουλίου αὐτοῦ). Here the Greek translator rendered yetzer, a neutral deliberative faculty, as διαβούλιον;
(3) v. 17 echoes Deut 30:19: "life and death" stand before each person;
(4) vv. 18-20 hold that God's omniscience does not cause sin [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0].
Scholars in the KG read the passage in the following ways:
- Brand: Ben Sira is "responding to deterministic approaches", possibly a Judean form of Hellenistic (Stoic) determinism. She also notes that his thought is inconsistent; Sir 33:7-15 is more deterministic [scholarly_argument_brand_ben_sira_free_will_theodicy].
- Brand: Ben Sira and Philo are the only Second Temple texts that face the problem of sin directly, and both place it in human hands [scholarly_argument_brand_philo_free_will_amid_pessimism].
- Klawans: the passage anticipates the "relative libertarianism" of the Sadducees as Josephus reports it (War 2.164-65) [scholarly_argument_klawans_2012_sadducee_libertarianism_matches_ben_sira; group_sadducees_a5b6c7d8].
- Maston: it is a two-ways model that puts human agency first [scholarly_argument_maston_ben_sira_and_human_agency_1], and Paul critiques it in Rom 7 [scholarly_argument_maston_paul_s_critique_of_two_ways_tr_3].
Caveat: the corpus holds no passage of Sirach itself [work_sirach_a3b4c5d6]. The Greek and the claimed reception (Justin, Clement) come only from KG descriptions and are not checked against the texts.
