ANSWER:
Largely a clarification, with a real change of emphasis. Augustine's own retrospective is the key witness. In Retract. 1.8.2 he says De lib. arb. was written against the Manichaeans, who deny that evil originates in free choice [818a6c6e-7f24-4e5f-94df-07cb8997292f]. Retract. 1.8.6 claims that long before Pelagianism he "argued as if already against them": free choice is a middle good (medium bonum), its good use is a virtue from God, and grace frees from the misery justly inflicted on sinners, since man fell by his own will but could not rise by it [4a65bea1-32ed-4bfb-8acc-022c64ce3b98; cf. synthesis_aug_medium_bonum]. Book III's natural/penal distinction of ignorance and difficulty (3.20-22) [synthesis_aug_natural_penal] is treated in the Retractationes as compatible with original sin: infants carry original sin yet are neither wise nor foolish [5c3303cd-7d80-465d-b3d0-fc1f8e2a5ae6]. He also glosses "sin is only in the will" as true of sin that is not also punishment of sin [3a06e7e9-ad0b-41ec-8ac9-f8530e4ca896; 978f5a2c-1d18-47f4-aa1b-61a992ec3890]. These are clarifications that block a Pelagian reading.
The shift is in the stress, and the corpus supports this. Dihle presents the will as shaped by two fronts: free (anti-Manichaean, De lib. arb. I-III) and wounded and in need of prevenient grace (anti-Pelagian) [argument_dihle_1982_augustine_responds_to_manichean_and_pelagian]. The corpus has no passage showing Augustine admitting a doctrinal reversal on III.18-21. Caveat: the Retractationes are self-interpretation, so the "clarification" verdict is partly Augustine's own. I did not read scholarly assessments of this specific question.
