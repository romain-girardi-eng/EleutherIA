Ennead VI.8 [39], "On the Voluntary and the Will of the One" (c. 268 CE), pushes the One's freedom to its limit: the One is not determined by any prior cause or metaphysical necessity, but is its own willing (self-willing), an autexousion beyond essence. The creaturely vocabulary of eph' hemin and autexousion is transposed analogically, not as contingency but as absolute self-necessity (causa sui), where willing and essence coincide [work_plotinus_ennead_vi_8_d8b9c5a4]. The treatise opens with a critique of Sextus' error on eph' hemin (§1-6), then the "bold" claim of §7 and the One's self-willing (§14-21) [work_plotinus_ennead_vi_8_d8b9c5a4].

Modern readings diverge. Frede 2011 argues against Armstrong that divine free will is not specifically Judeo-Christian: Plotinus rejects the necessity of God's nature as "monstrous" (VI.8.7) and presents reality as originating in a free divine act, of which human will is an image (VI.8.15); at most Origen's influence is conceivable [argument_frede_2011_plotinus_divine_will_not_judeo_christian]. Dihle 1982 holds that Plotinus stays intellectualist, with "will = activity of nous"; the real concept of will emerges on the Latin-Christian side, while Greek Christians (Origen, Gregory of Nyssa, Nemesius) remain dependent on Greek categories [synthesis_dihle1982_lec5_late_antiquity_plotinus_no_will].
