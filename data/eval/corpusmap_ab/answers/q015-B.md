The Augustine-Pelagius debate (411-431 CE) was the foundational controversy shaping Western Christian theology of grace, free will and original sin [debate_augustine_pelagius_grace]. Augustine's anti-Pelagian argument, directed against Pelagius, Caelestius and Julian of Eclanum, marks a shift from early optimism about free choice to a "pessimistic compatibilism" stressing the necessity of grace and human depravity [argument_augustines_antipelagian_argument_grace_necessity_0b29401f]. Dihle (1982) explains Augustine's concept of voluntas as born of two fronts. Against the Manichaeans, the will must be free and capable of defection. Against Pelagius, who exalts the natural freedom of the will, it must be wounded by original sin and in need of prevenient grace. Works cited for this are De spiritu et littera, De gratia et libero arbitrio, De correptione et gratia and De praedestinatione sanctorum [argument_dihle_1982_augustine_responds_to_manichean_and_pelagian]. De gratia et libero arbitrio (426) was addressed to Valentine and the monks of Hadrumetum [work_augustine_de_gratia_la]. Gorday: from Ad Simplicianum 1,2, Augustine rejected foreseen merits as the basis of election, because of the utter gratuity of grace (faith included) [scholarly_argument_gorday_augustine_s_development_on_fre_1]. Matteoli: Pelagius, before the controversy, defended libero arbitrio against deterministic readings of Paul, but "in the substance does not innovate", following Origen and others; it is Augustine who introduces a different perspective [scholarly_argument_matteoli_free_will_libero_arbitrio_in_p_0]. The corpus also links the Pelagian De induratione to Origenian exegesis [scholarly_argument_scarponi_de_induratione_reuses_origen_against_determinism] and Møller finds it plausible, though not certain, that Augustine targets Origen's cooperation model [scholarly_argument_moller_augustine_target_plausible_not_decisive].
