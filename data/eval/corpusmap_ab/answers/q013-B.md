The Lazy Argument (ἀργὸς λόγος / ignava ratio) runs: if it is fated that you recover from illness, you will recover whether or not you call a doctor; if fated that you do not, you will not recover either way; one of the two is fated; so calling a doctor is pointless. Cicero notes it was rightly called "ignavum atque iners" because the same reasoning would remove all action from life, and it can be restated without "fate" via eternal truth/falsity of propositions [01aa3782-db59-442d-8887-b74f4f32df38; debate_lazy_argument]. Cicero reports that Chrysippus rejected it [8ba2536b-c925-490f-b37b-f48bde28a03f].

The Stoic reply is the doctrine of co-fated events (confatalia / συγκαθειμαρμένα): the action and its result are not two separately fated events but co-fated, so the doctor's visit is fated together with the recovery [debate_lazy_argument; synthesis_bobzien2001_ch5_idle_argument; argument_bobzien_2001_b1_lazy_argument_cofated_solution]. Bobzien (2001, ch. 5) reconstructs this, compares Cic. Fat. 30, Origen and Eusebius, and also offers a critique of the refutation [synthesis_bobzien2001_ch5_idle_argument]. Gourinat holds that co-fatedness yields a completely determinist Stoicism, assents included [scholarly_argument_gourinat_2005_cofatedness_complete_determinism].

Modern dispute: Brennan argues the reply fails: it shows only that some type of action is necessary for a fated outcome, not that this token action is, and co-fated events merely add events outside the agent's control; deliberation presupposes openness [scholarly_argument_brennan_lazy_argument_powerful]. Hildebrandt reads Alexander's De fato XXI as a stronger, risk-based Lazy Argument [scholarly_argument_hildebrandt_alexander_new_lazy_argument_risk], distinct from the weaker versions at XI and XVI [scholarly_argument_hildebrandt_traditional_lazy_argument_three_versions].
