Chrysippus holds that everything happens by fate, a natural, eternal connected sequence of causes (Gellius NA 7.2.3) [ad59899c-4c0e-4d0b-8825-6ee05e34fb19]. He rejects necessity yet insists nothing occurs without antecedent causes, and he distinguishes kinds of cause (auxiliary/proximate versus principal/complete). The cylinder analogy illustrates this: an external impulse starts the motion, but the cylinder's own shape determines how it rolls, so the agent's own nature contributes to assent [fc50aff8-32e5-443b-950d-35ad89b15d96] [1812b556-ba17-4407-9998-f0383d158dd2] [64cca2fc-2fe6-466e-8443-e836e02bcc83] [concept_cylinder_analogy_chrysippus_e5f6g7h8]. Scholars dispute whether this escapes necessity (Sorabji) or is determinist (Koch) [concept_cylinder_analogy_chrysippus_e5f6g7h8].

Alexander attacks this chain model [argument_chain_causation_critique_alex]. His wording is a connected series of causes (εἱρμὸς αἰτίων, De Fato 22-25), while "like a chain" is the Stoic definition he reports. His objection is that an antecedent cause need not necessitate: Sophroniscus was necessary for Socrates without determining him, and foundations are necessary for a building without fixing what is built. He therefore separates necessary conditions from sufficient, determining causes, and charges the Stoics with conflating them. He also argues that on the Stoic view deliberation and what depends on us (τὸ ἐφ' ἡμῖν) are abolished [28bc2223-dabd-4bb0-933f-faa0fbcc2ef9] [debate_alexander_stoics_determinism].
