Partly, and the corpus supports a qualified yes. Boethius states the problem in V.pr.3: if God foreknows X with certainty, X is necessary, and then human actions, merit and reward and punishment are void [argument_boethius_foreknowledge_problem; 309e96dc-5fb9-4ea1-a94a-6e1d883056a9]. He answers in three moves. (1) Knowledge is fixed by the knower's mode, not by the known object. Four faculties are ranked: sense, imagination, reason, intelligentia [argument_boethius_modes_cognition]. (2) Divine intelligentia lives in eternity, "interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio", which differs from endless duration [concept_aeternitas_boethius_g2h3i4j5; b5aab3f4-725c-49d3-86e0-a09f76469757]. God therefore does not foresee. He sees all things in a present, and sees necessary and voluntary events as such, like a man who sees someone walking and the sun rising at once [5ed3cdd3-f525-4221-91bf-d03d8418c6cc]. (3) This yields a conditional necessity: what is seen as present is necessary only "per condicionem divinae notionis". Considered in itself, a free act keeps its freedom and could have failed to occur [6dcc4e8f-190e-4bea-9be4-8a073e832ff7; argument_boethian_solution_divine_eternity_k1l2m3n4].
Whether this settles the matter is not argued in the tools' returns. They record only that the solution became the most cited medieval framework [argument_boethian_solution_divine_eternity_k1l2m3n4]. Possible objections, such as an unexamined shift from foreknowledge to timeless knowledge, are not attested in the corpus, so they stay unknown. Boethius's earlier work on De Interpretatione 9 is a separate approach, a denial of bivalence for future contingents, and does not belong to this solution [argument_boethius_future_contingents].
