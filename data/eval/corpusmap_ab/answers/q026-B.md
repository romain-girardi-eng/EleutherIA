Continuity. Both Justin and Origen defend human responsibility through autexousion against fatalism and determinism. Justin's anti-fatalist argument says that if all is fated, "what is up to us is nothing", so praise, blame and reward would be meaningless [argument_justin_antifatalism]. Origen likewise ties freedom to what is up to us (eph' hēmin), and he attacks astral fate and gnostic fixed "natures" [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_s_doctrine_of_free_will_5; scholarly_argument_sytsma_anti_determinism_idle_argument_deprinc_iii]. Both use the term autexousion: Justin applies it to God's creation of angels and humans (2 Apol. 6.5, Dial. 102.4) [scholarly_argument_minns_as_justin_s_term_for_free_will_2; 7b029b3b-d680-4362-a0ea-8ef4b2aef215; argument_justin_angel_fall]. Frede traces the term to Stoic usage and to Christian popularisation from Justin on [scholarly_argument_frede_autexousion_terminology_4; argument_frede_2011_autexousion_stoic_origin_then_christian]. Fürst treats Justin as a precursor.

Divergence. Justin's use is apologetic and scattered across 1 Apol. 43-44, 2 Apol. 5-7 and Dial. [argument_justin_martyrs_apologetic_argument_for_free_will_45c2dde2]. His novelty is extending autexousion to angels, with fate as a demonic invention [argument_justin_angel_fall]. Origen, on Fürst's account, makes autexousion a central technical concept and devotes a dedicated treatise, Περὶ αὐτεξουσίου (De Princ. III.1), to it. Earlier writers wrote "On Fate" [argument_furst_2022_origen_culmination_autexousion; baa51e4b-9ad7-54a1-a971-74de1631661e]. Sytsma warns against glossing it as "free will" [scholarly_argument_sytsma_autexousion_not_a_faculty_of_will]. Frede finds Origen's framework basically Stoic but integrated into his Platonism [scholarly_argument_frede_origen_s_differences_from_stoi_6]. Caveat: Bobzien's note concerns exousia, not autexousion, in Justin [scholarly_argument_bobzien_autexousion_as_philosophical_t_6].
