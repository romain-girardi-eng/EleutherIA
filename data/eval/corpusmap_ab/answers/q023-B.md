Plotinus. His fullest treatment is Ennead VI.8, which reacts to the Stoic notion of free will and distinguishes levels of freedom tied to the intelligible realm and the One [scholarly_argument_frede_plotinus_on_free_will_6]. Augustine. The corpus presents him as treating the will as the deficient cause of sin, not an efficient cause [synthesis_aug_deficient_cause]. It also describes the will as a middle good [synthesis_aug_medium_bonum]. Dihle says he took shape against the Manichean and Pelagian challenges [argument_dihle_1982_augustine_responds_to_manichean_and_pelagian].

Comparison (scholarly dispute). Dihle holds that Plotinian freedom is the agent's coincidence with its intellectual nature, so that even the One's "will" is the activity of nous. On this reading Plotinus does not posit a voluntary faculty distinct from the intellect, whereas Augustine does [argument_dihle_1982_plotinus_remains_intellectualist]. Fürst qualifies the Augustine-centred view, because Origen and Plotinus were decisive before him [argument_furst_2022_critique_dihle_augustine_thesis]. Frede says Augustine is a beneficiary of the Stoic notion, not a radical innovator [scholarly_argument_frede_augustine_on_free_will_7]. Sorabji counts Plotinus among the sources of separate strands of the will [scholarly_argument_sorabji_freedom_and_the_will_in_ancien_0].
