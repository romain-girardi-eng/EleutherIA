Sir 15:11-20 is attested in the corpus as the paradigmatic Hellenistic-Jewish free-choice text, though the passage text itself is not in the corpus; the evidence is the KG and scholarly summaries. The Entity Page quotes 15:14: God "made human beings in the beginning and left them in the power of their own choice" [person_ben_sira_i9j0k1l2]. The KG argument node analyses it in four steps: vv. 11-12 forbid blaming God for sin, since God does not do what he hates (a theodicy move); v. 14 gives the anthropological ground, with the grandson's Greek διαβούλιον rendering the Hebrew yetzer as a neutral deliberative faculty; v. 17 echoes Deut 30:19 ("life and death"); vv. 18-20 hold that divine omniscience does not command sin. It is called one of the earliest structurally complete arguments for moral agency in the Jewish wisdom tradition, parallel to Plato Rep. 617e, and received by Justin, Clement and the rabbinic yetzer tradition [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]. Brand reads it as Ben Sira's most direct treatment of sin, arguing for free will against attributing sin to God [scholarly_argument_brand_ben_sira_free_will_theodicy]. Maston stresses that the two-ways model from Deut 30 makes the human the primary actor and God a judge reacting to deeds [scholarly_argument_maston_ben_sira_and_human_agency_1]. Klawans sees it as precedent for Josephus's Sadducees (War 2.164-65): free choice, no one destined to evil, God opposed to evil; the "Say not..." polemic shows the issue was debated in the 2nd c. BCE [scholarly_argument_klawans_2012_sadducee_libertarianism_matches_ben_sira]. Whether it is the "locus classicus" is a scholarly characterisation, not an ancient attestation.
