The corpus documents Irenaeus's free-will argument in AH IV.37.1-2 but does not directly document a Justin-Irenaeus line of dependence. IV.37.2 (Greek fragment, via John of Damascus) says that if people were good or bad by nature, the good would not be praiseworthy nor the wicked blameworthy. Because all share one nature and can both hold and lose the good, praise and punishment are just, and the prophets exhorted people since the choice is "ἐφ' ἡμῖν" [7c74409a-301c-5e6e-a0a8-ef71a7843cff]. The KG reads this as a Carneadean praise/blame argument transposed against Valentinian natural determinism, with God as supreme judge and eternal punishment [argument_irenaeus_adv_haer_iv_37_praise_blame_transposed]. Löhr calls it a philosophical anti-determinist argument going back at least to Aristotle and widely used in the 2nd century by Alcinous, Alexander and Justin. This supports a shared tradition, not direct borrowing, and Löhr notes Irenaeus never compares Valentinian determinism with Stoic determinism [scholarly_argument_l_hr_irenaeus_s_anti_determinist_ar_1]. Fürst presents Justin (Apol. 43-44, 2 Apol. 6-7) as the first explicit Christian proclamation of free decision (ἐλεύθερα προαίρεσις), linked to judgment and retribution and with a biblical foundation [synthesis_furst2022_justin_first_christian_freiheits_philosophy]. The thematic continuity is therefore plausible: free choice, eschatological recompense and anti-determinism. The tools returned no passage-level evidence on IV.38-39 or on a direct Justin source for IV.37. Grant's translation does not include IV.37-39 [scholarly_argument_grant_irenaeus_on_free_will_and_mora_0], and Rousseau's edition offers no interpretive position [scholarly_argument_rousseau_free_will_in_irenaeus_0]. Direct dependence is unattested here.
