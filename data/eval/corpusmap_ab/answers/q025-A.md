Aristotle: the locus classicus is EN III.5, 1113b7-8, "where acting is up to us, so is not acting" [1e336c5e-391c-5613-a59d-b83d2bcc5523]. This gives a two-sided reading: a genuine power for opposites, which the KG says was already operative in Aristotle and his commentators [concept_eph_hemin_two_sided_potestative]. The agent is also responsible for his own settled character, and in some way for how things appear to him [28b16b62-34cb-4db0-a445-c778d696cb4e; argument_aristotles_voluntary_action_argument_eph_hemin_e5dd9188]. Sorabji's 1980 reading is flagged as disputed [concept_eph_hemin_in_our_power_aristotle_d4e5f6g7].

Early Stoics (Chrysippus): per the KG's Alexander-based reconstruction, eph' hemin is one-sided and causative. The agent's ruling faculty is the proximate cause of the action, but identical prior conditions yield a unique outcome. Alexander accuses the Stoics of evacuating the potestative content [concept_eph_hemin_two_sided_potestative]. Cicero, Fat. 41, reports Chrysippus's distinction between principal and auxiliary proximate causes, which is how he rejects necessity while keeping fate [17c14800-34a6-50b7-b886-17aa9bc54ac2]. The KG states that later reconstructions remain disputed [concept_eph_hemin_in_our_power_aristotle_d4e5f6g7].

Roman Stoics: according to Eliasson, for Seneca, Musonius, Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius what is eph' hemin is mainly the correct use of impressions. The point is no longer responsibility but eudaimonia. Action is excluded because the body can be hindered, and the criterion becomes being unhindered [scholarly_argument_eliasson_roman_stoa_eudaimonist_shift]. Epictetus Diss. 1.25 contrasts "your own" things as unhindered with others' things as hindered [9bc9caf8-1408-53dd-a696-e965a9767b52].
