Sir 15:11-20 is presented in the graph as one of the earliest structurally complete arguments for human moral agency in Jewish wisdom literature [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]. The text is attributed to Ben Sira [person_ben_sira_i9j0k1l2], whose work (Hebrew c. 190-175 BCE, Greek translation by his grandson c. 132 BCE, Alexandria) is [work_sirach_a3b4c5d6]. The argument has four steps [argument_sirach_free_will_theodicy_m7n8o9p0]:
1. vv. 11-12 forbid blaming God for sin, because God does not do what he hates. This is a theodicy.
2. v. 14 says God made human beings "in the beginning" and left them "in the power of their own deliberative capacity" (ἐν χειρὶ διαβουλίου αὐτοῦ). The Greek διαβούλιον renders the Hebrew yetzer, here a neutral faculty and not yet the rabbinic dual yetzer.
3. v. 17 echoes Deut 30:19: life and death lie before each person, and whichever one chooses will be given.
4. vv. 18-20 hold that God sees all deeds but commands no one to sin, so omniscience is compatible with choice.
The node also draws a parallel with Plato Rep. 617e, and lists Justin and Clement among those who received the passage. Scholarly nodes support this reading. Brand reads the passage as Ben Sira's most direct treatment of sin, a defence of free will against deterministic thought [scholarly_argument_brand_ben_sira_free_will_theodicy]. Maston stresses the two-ways model from Deuteronomy, in which the human is the primary actor and God reacts as judge [scholarly_argument_maston_ben_sira_and_human_agency_1]. Klawans sees in it a precedent for the Sadducean libertarianism reported by Josephus (War 2.164-65), motivated by theodicy [scholarly_argument_klawans_2012_sadducee_libertarianism_matches_ben_sira].
