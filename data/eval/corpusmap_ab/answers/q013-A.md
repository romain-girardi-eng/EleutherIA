The Lazy Argument (argos logos / ignava ratio) is a dilemma against universal fate. Cicero gives it in De Fato 29. If it is fated that you recover from this illness, you will recover whether or not you call the doctor. If it is fated that you will not recover, you will not recover whether or not you call the doctor. One of the two is fated, so calling the doctor is pointless. Cicero says the form was rightly called "ignavum atque iners" because it would remove all action from life [01aa3782-db59-442d-8887-b74f4f32df38]. It can be restated without the word "fate", using propositions true or false from eternity [01aa3782-db59-442d-8887-b74f4f32df38]. Cicero introduces it at Fat. 28 and rejects it [d3a3c37e-a837-484f-961d-c8de568ecbc3]. [argument_the_lazy_argument_argos_logos_702a77ed]

The Chrysippean response, reported at Fat. 30, distinguishes simple from copulate (conjoined) events. "Socrates will die on that day" is simple. "Oedipus will be born to Laius" is conjoined, or "confatalis", because it is fated together with Laius' intercourse. Likewise "Milo will wrestle" implies an opponent. So "you will recover whether or not you call the doctor" is a captious inference, since calling the doctor is itself fated together with recovery [8ba2536b-c925-490f-b37b-f48bde28a03f; concept_confatalia_chrysippus; argument_the_cofated_events_argument_confatalia_b7715646]. Modern assessments differ. Hildebrandt reports the co-fated reply as the transmitted rebuttal [scholarly_argument_hildebrandt_chrysippus_cofated_response]. Gourinat holds that it saves the Stoics but leaves a completely determinist Stoicism, assents included [scholarly_argument_gourinat_2005_cofatedness_complete_determinism]. Brennan argues that it fails to meet the original complaint about deliberation [scholarly_argument_brennan_lazy_argument_powerful].
