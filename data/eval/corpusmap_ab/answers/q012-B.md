ANSWER:
The principal Academic assault is Carneades' (the Carneades-Chrysippus confrontation is the core of the Stoic-Academic debate [debate_stoic_academic_hellenistic]). It is transmitted chiefly through Cicero's De Fato [work_de_fato_cicero_44bce_b9c4e5d2].

1. A reductio of universal fate (CAFMA): if everything is fated, then (1) nothing is in our power (to eph' hemin); (2) not even assent is in our power, so the Stoic "locus of responsibility" is itself determined; (3) praise and blame become unjust; (4) exhortation and admonition are pointless; (5) there is no desert or justice [argument_cafma_carneades_m3n4o5p6; argument_cafma_futility_of_sanctions_0e5f7h43].

2. The chain of assent-impulse-action: if impulse is an effect of prior causes, assent and action are effects of an external principle, hence not in our power. Since praise, blame, honours and punishments would then be unjust, the conclusion is that not everything happens by fate. Amand reads this as a compressed Carneadean proof in De fato 17 and 40 [argument_carneadean_assent_chain_via_cicero_amand1945].

3. Against Chrysippus' replies: the Academic retort to the internal/external cause (cylinder) distinction is that internal causes, if themselves fated, do not secure origination. The same is said of the "co-fated" reply, which leaves no genuine alternatives [argument_cafma_carneades_m3n4o5p6; argument_cylinder_analogy_chrysippus_k1l2m3n4]. Brennan holds that the co-fated reply to the Lazy Argument fails [scholarly_argument_brennan_lazy_argument_powerful].

4. Positive move: Fürst, following De Fato 23-25, says Carneades makes voluntas a cause with no external antecedent, rooted in the soul's nature [synthesis_furst2022_carneades_will_innovation].

Caveat: Koch calls the De Fato 39 debate a Ciceronian staging, not a historical dispute [scholarly_argument_koch_2011_veteres_debate_anachronistic]. I did not read the primary passages themselves, only these summaries.
