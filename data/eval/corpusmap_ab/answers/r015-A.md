In the Disputatio cum Pyrrho (Carthage, 645; PG 91, 287–354) [work_maximus_disp_pyrrho], Maximus distinguishes the natural will (φυσικὸν θέλημα) from the gnomic will (γνωμικὸν θέλημα) [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. The argument runs as follows. Will belongs to nature, not to hypostasis. Christ has two natures, so he has two natural wills, divine and human, in harmony; this is the dyothelite conclusion [argument_maximus_two_wills]. The gnomic will is not the natural willing-power. It is a deliberative mode of willing marked by ignorance, hesitation and possible error, and it belongs to fallen agents. Christ therefore assumes a human natural will but no gnomic will [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will; argument_maximus_two_wills]. In the KG's gloss, the two kinds of will share τὸ θέλειν (the willing-power) by nature but differ in τὸ πῶς θέλειν (the manner of willing) [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will]. The Disputatio itself is the answer to Pyrrhus's monothelitism and monenergism [argument_pyrrhus_one_will; argument_pyrrhus_one_energy]. The doctrine was adopted at Constantinople III, 680–681 [argument_maximus_natural_vs_gnomic_will].
Bathrellos 2004 (The Byzantine Christ, OUP) appears in the KG only as a named item in the "scholarly reception" line of [argument_maximus_two_wills], together with Louth 1996 and Larchet 1996. The KG has no node for Bathrellos and no summary of his position. His specific claims are therefore not attested here and are not given.
For scholarly discussion the KG offers Blowers: natural will as the basic faculty, set against gnomic will [scholarly_argument_blowers_natural_vs_gnomic_will]; a possible soteriological cost of denying a gnomic will in Christ [scholarly_argument_blowers_denial_gnomic_will_soteriological_cost]; and the Adamic fall as a gnomic failure [scholarly_argument_blowers_adamic_fall_gnomic_failure].
