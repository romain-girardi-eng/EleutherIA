The corpus frames the question mainly through modern scholarship, not through ancient passages (none retrieved). The classic thesis is Socratic intellectualism: virtue is knowledge and "no one does wrong willingly" (Protagoras 358c-d, as summarised) [concept_socratic_intellectualism_f6g7h8i9]. Dihle (1982) argues that the Greeks never developed a distinct concept of will: action is explained by rational cognition, intention is intellectual, and striving is attributed to instinct or emotion [argument_dihle_1982_greek_intellectualism_thesis]. In his reading, Aristotle's deliberation, prohairesis and boulesis remain intellectual, and akrasia is a cognitive failure. He concedes that akrasia could open space for a faculty of will, but Aristotle does not take that step [synthesis_dihle1982_lec2_greek_intellectualism_action].
Stoic assent (synkatathesis) is the strongest objection. Dihle answers that assent is voluntary yet necessitated, and remains rational; weak assent is analysed as weakness of judgement [argument_dihle_1982_synkatathesis_is_cognitive_not_volitional].
Voelke rejects the alternative itself: in Stoic rationalism reason is will [scholarly_argument_voelke_stoic_reason_is_will].
Frede (2011) sees Aristotle as raw material only, with no separate faculty of will. He places the first genuine free will in Epictetus's prohairesis, and treats Augustine as a variation on it, against Dihle [pub_frede_2011_free_will].
The medieval intellectualism/voluntarism debate has ancient roots but is a medieval structuring dispute [debate_intellectualism_vs_voluntarism_w3x4y5z6]. The corpus consulted does not attest a native ancient "voluntarism".
