Mostly a Stoic carryover in vocabulary, with a Christian transformation in doctrine. Frede (2011) argues that autexousion first appears in a Stoic context (Musonius Rufus, then frequently Epictetus), is then taken up by Platonists and Peripatetics, and enters Christian literature with Justin. Its Christian frequency therefore reflects adoption of existing Stoic-philosophical vocabulary, not invention [argument_frede_2011_autexousion_stoic_origin_then_christian]. The corpus supports the Epictetan side. Epictetus Diss. 4.1.55-69 defines freedom as αὐτεξούσιον and αὐτοδέσποτον, which the passage's gloss calls the essence of ἐφ' ἡμῖν [264e75f4-d79b-5483-b5f5-591f912a42de]. Plotinus Enn. 6.8.3 also uses the term in a pagan setting [20d7f221-df29-4157-aa9a-969426a3cf37]. The KG's Christian concept node itself notes pre-Christian attestation (Philo) [concept_autexousion_christian_freedom_u1v2w3x4].

Two qualifications. First, Bobzien notes that autexousion is linked to exousia already in Epictetus "in a deterministic setting". From the late 2nd century it became common as a philosophical term and gradually superseded ἐφ' ἡμῖν. Alexander uses it very rarely, and it may have been read as implying indeterminist freedom [scholarly_argument_bobzien_autexousion_as_philosophical_t_6]. Second, the Christian concept node asserts a distinctively libertarian, creation-gift, theodicy-bearing sense that rejects Stoic heimarmene [concept_autexousion_christian_freedom_u1v2w3x4]. Those doctrinal functions are the Christian (and Bardaisan-style) novelty, not the word [argument_bardesanes_triple_delimitation_physis_heimarmene_autexousion].

Marguerite Harl (1960) treats exactly this question [pub_harl_1960_problemes_histoire_autexousion], but the KG gives only the title, so her conclusions are unattested here. Likewise, the corpus returned no Musonius or Justin passage.
