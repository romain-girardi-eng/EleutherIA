Chrysippus holds that everything happens by fate, an eternal chain of causes extending infinitely backward and forward. Even so, he thinks responsibility survives, because what matters is whether the determining cause is internal (character, assent) or external (force). His cylinder analogy illustrates this: an external push starts the motion, but the cylinder's own nature determines how it moves [person_chrysippus_280_206bce_i9j0k1l2]. The Cicero passage reports this analogy, applied to assent [Fat. 43: ac9ffaf1-a72c-5a5c-b8b3-adceec102ca4]. Bobzien reads Stoic causation as body-to-body, transmitted by pneuma and tension. The agent is a rational body and an internal cause [argument_bobzien_2001_b1_pneumatic_causation_model]. On this model there are no uncaused motions [Fat. 20: 3fc3b850-3770-5d9c-ab3a-f051677d266f].

Alexander (De Fato) rejects this. He is the foremost ancient critic of determinism and defends libertarian incompatibilism [person_alexander_aphrodisias_fl200ce_n5o6p7q8]. On the KG's account he argues that Stoic determinism destroys "what is in our power", deliberation and responsibility. For him, rational two-way powers are not themselves causally determined. Their actualization in one direction has no sufficient prior cause and is fixed only by the agent's choice, which is agent causation. Bobzien adds that Alexander rejects all predetermining causes, including character and reason, and requires that the same agent in the same circumstances could choose differently [same node]. Amand sees much of his anti-Chrysippean argument as inherited from Carneades [same node].

The corpus returned for this question contains no De Fato passages by Alexander himself, so the description of his view rests on KG node summaries.
