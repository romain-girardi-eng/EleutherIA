## Arm A

Justin Martyr (c. 100–165 CE) represents a foundational moment in the history of Christian philosophy, being the first extant Christian writer to systematically employ the technical term **αὐτεξούσιον** (*autexousion*, self-determination) to describe the capacity of rational beings for free choice [Source 1, Source 15]. His thought is deeply rooted in the intersection of Middle Platonism and Christian revelation, specifically directed against Stoic fatalism and the emerging deterministic tendencies of Gnostic thought [Source 1, Source 12].

### 1. The Antifatalist Argument and "What is Up to Us"
Justin’s primary concern is to protect the coherence of moral responsibility. In his *First Apology*, he argues that if human actions were dictated by **εἱμαρμένη** (*heimarmene*, fate), the concepts of virtue and vice would be rendered meaningless [Source 2, Source 9].

He writes:
> "ἐπεὶ εἰ μὴ τοῦτό ἐστιν, ἀλλὰ καθ᾿ εἱμαρμένην πάντα γίνεται, οὔ τε τὸ ἐφ᾿ ἡμῖν ἐστιν ὅλως· εἰ γὰρ εἵμαρται τόνδε τινὰ ἀγαθὸν εἶναι καὶ τόνδε φαῦλον, οὔθ᾿ οὗτος ἀπόδεκτος οὐ δὲ ἐκεῖνος μεμπτέος." [Source 5, Source 14]
> (For if this is not so, but all things happen by fate, there is nothing at all up to us; for if it is fated that this person be good and that one bad, neither is the former praiseworthy nor the latter blameworthy.)

Justin utilizes the Stoic technical term **τὸ ἐφ᾿ ἡμῖν** (*to eph’ hēmin*, what is up to us) but turns it against the Stoic concept of fated necessity (**καθ᾿ εἱμαρμένης ἀνάγκην**) [Source 2, Source 5]. He argues that the reality of moral change—the fact that the same individual can transition from vice to virtue—proves that character is not fated [Source 9, Source 14].

### 2. The Concept of *Autexousion* (Self-Determination)
Justin extends the capacity for free choice to all rational beings, including both humans and angels [Source 13, Source 18]. He defines this capacity as **ἐλευθέρα προαίρεσις** (*eleuthera proairesis*, free choice) and **αὐτεξούσιον** [Source 4, Source 8].

In the *Dialogue with Trypho*, he explains:
> "Βουλόμενος γὰρ τούτους ἐν ἐλευθέρᾳ προαιρέσει καὶ αὐτεξουσίους γενομένους, τούς τε ἀγγέλους καὶ τοὺς ἀνθρώπους, ὁ θεὸς πράττειν ὅσα ἕκαστον ἐνεδυνάμωσε δύνασθαι ποιεῖν, ἐποίησεν..." [Source 4, Source 8]
> (For God, wishing them—both angels and humans—to act with free choice and self-determination, made them capable of doing what each was empowered to do...)

For Justin, this freedom is the prerequisite for divine judgment. If humans were like "trees or quadrupeds" (**δένδρα καὶ τετράποδα**), incapable of acting by choice, they would not be worthy of reward or punishment [Source 7, Source 9].

### 3. Foreknowledge vs. Causal Necessity
A significant challenge for Justin was reconciling divine foreknowledge (**πρόγνωσις**) and prophecy with human freedom. He argues that God’s ability to predict the future does not impose necessity on the events themselves [Source 3, Source 10].

He clarifies:
> "διότι προεγίνωσκεν αὐτοὺς ἀμεταβλήτως γενησομένους πονηρούς, προεῖπε ταῦτα, ἀλλ᾿ οὐχ, ὅτι αὐτοὺς ὁ θεὸς τοιούτους ἐποίησεν." [Source 16]
> (Because He foreknew they would become unchangeably wicked, He foretold these things; but not because God made them such.)

To support this, Justin famously cites Plato’s *Republic* (617e): **"αἰτία ἑλομένου· θεὸς ἀναίτιος"** (The blame belongs to the one who chooses; God is blameless) [Source 3, Source 17]. Justin asserts that Plato "borrowed" this principle from Moses, thereby grounding the philosophical defense of free will in the oldest scriptural traditions [Source 11, Source 17].

### 4. Moral Responsibility and the Origin of Evil
Justin maintains a rigorous view of individual responsibility. While acknowledging the fall of humanity, he emphasizes that sin is the result of individual choice rather than an inherited, irresistible nature [Source 1, Source 4].

He states:
> "...παρὰ τὴν ἰδίαν αἰτίαν ἑκάστου αὐτῶν πονηρευσαμένου." [Source 4, Source 6]
> (...each one having done evil through his own fault.)

Furthermore, Justin introduces a demonological element to his theodicy: he believes fallen angels introduced the deceptions of fate and astrology to enslave humanity, meaning that "fate" itself is not a cosmic law but a demonic invention intended to obscure human freedom [Source 13].

### 5. Scholarly Synthesis: Justin and Middle Platonism
Modern scholarship, notably Carl Andresen, has demonstrated that Justin’s doctrine of fate is structurally parallel to Middle Platonist handbooks, such as Alcinous' *Didaskalikos* [Source 1, Source 20]. 

* **Andresen's Thesis:** Justin adopts the Middle Platonist "conditional fate" model: while the soul is free in its initial decisions (**προαίρεσις**), the consequences of those decisions are subject to a fated order of justice [Source 20].
* **Distinction from later Theology:** Unlike later Pauline or Augustinian developments, Justin’s theology does not emphasize the role of grace or the total corruption of the will; he maintains a "libertarian" view where the human capacity for virtue remains intact through the exercise of reason and choice [Source 1, Source 12].

## Arm B

Justin Martyr (c. 100–165 CE), a second-century apologist and philosopher, is recognized as the first author in the Christian tradition to provide a systematic defense of free will [2, 5]. Integrating his training in Middle Platonism with Christian scripture, Justin argues that moral responsibility is contingent upon the human capacity for self-determination, a position he develops primarily to counter the deterministic frameworks of his era [2, 24].

### Antifatalism and Moral Responsibility
Justin’s doctrine of freedom is fundamentally an argument against fatalism (*heimarmene*). He contends that if human actions were determined by necessity, the concepts of merit, praise, and blame would be rendered incoherent [P9, 10]. In his *First Apology*, he employs a *reductio ad absurdum*: if it were fated for one man to be good and another to be evil, the former could not be justly rewarded nor the latter justly punished [P9].

> εἰ γὰρ εἵμαρται τόνδε τινὰ ἀγαθὸν εἶναι καὶ τόνδε φαῦλον, οὔθ᾽ οὗτος ἀπόδεκτος οὐ δὲ ἐκεῖνος μεμπτέος. [P9]
>
> For if it were fated that this man should be good and that one bad, neither would the former be acceptable nor the latter blameworthy [P9].

Justin further argues that the observable reality of moral change—where the same individual shifts between vice and virtue—proves that humans possess a free choice (*proairesei eleuthera*) to pursue the good or avoid the shameful [P9]. Without this power, the human race would be without cause (*anaitios*) for its actions, making divine judgment impossible [P9].

### The Doctrine of Autexousion
Central to Justin’s anthropology is the concept of *autexousion* (αὐτεξούσιον), or self-determination [1]. He maintains that God created both the human race and the angelic host with this inherent power, making them responsible for their own transgressions [P10, 20]. This term, which became a staple of Patristic theology, emphasizes the self-originating nature of human freedom as distinct from mere voluntariness [1, 3].

> τὴν ἀρχὴν γὰρ αὐτεξούσιον καὶ τὸν τῶν ἀνθρώπων καὶ τὸν τῶν ἀγγέλων γένος ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός. [P10]
>
> For in the beginning God made the race of angels and men with free-will (autexousion) [P10].

Justin extends this framework to explain the fall of the angels, suggesting that some used their *autexousion* to transgress divine order, subsequently introducing the deceptive doctrines of fate and astrology to enslave humanity [20]. By placing the origin of evil in the misuse of created freedom rather than in the divine will or material necessity, Justin preserves the justice of God [3, 20].

### Foreknowledge and Synergistic Salvation
Justin reconciles divine foreknowledge with human freedom by distinguishing between prediction and causation [19]. He argues that when God predicts future events through the prophets, this foreknowledge (*proginōskei*) does not impose a fatal necessity (*heimarmenēs anankēn*) upon the actors [P9, 19]. Rather, God simply foresees how individuals will use their freedom [19].

His view of salvation is characterized by a synergistic cooperation between human choice and divine assistance [94]. While humans must actively "choose to be persuaded" (*hairoumenous pepeisthai*) by God’s will, the capacity to understand the Scriptures and achieve salvation is ultimately facilitated by divine grace [P3, 97].

> ἀπεκάλυψεν οὖν ἡμῖν πάντα ὅσα καὶ ἀπὸ τῶν γραφῶν διὰ τῆς χάριτος αὐτοῦ νενοήκαμεν... [P3]
>
> He has revealed to us all things which we have understood from the Scriptures by His grace... [P3].

### Philosophical Context and Structural Parallels
Modern scholarship, notably the work of Carl Andresen, has highlighted that Justin’s structural approach to fate mirrors Middle Platonist frameworks [22, 24]. Specifically, Justin’s arguments regarding what is up to us (*to eph’ hēmin*) and the futility of praise and blame under determinism find a direct parallel in the conditional fate doctrine of Alcinous [22, 24]. This suggests that Justin did not merely rely on biblical proof-texts but actively utilized the philosophical tools of his time to articulate a coherent Christian defense of moral agency [5, 24].
