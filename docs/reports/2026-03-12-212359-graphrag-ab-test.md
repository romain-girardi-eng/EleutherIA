## Arm A

The Stoic position on fate and moral responsibility, primarily developed by Chrysippus of Soli, represents the most influential ancient defense of **compatibilism**: the view that universal causal determinism is consistent with human agency and moral accountability [Source 1], [Source 3], [Source 17].

### I. The Definition of Fate (εἱμαρμένη)
For the Stoics, fate (**εἱμαρμένη / heimarmenê**) is not a random or external force, but a rational, eternal, and unbreakable chain of causes governed by divine reason (**λόγος / logos**) [Source 1]. 

Aulus Gellius preserves Chrysippus’s definition in Latin:
> *"Fatum est sempiterna quaedam et indeclinabilis series rerum et catena volvens semetipsa sese et inplicans per aeternos consequentiae ordines, ex quibus apta nexaque est."* 
> ("Fate is a certain eternal and unchangeable series of things and a chain rolling and entangling itself through eternal orders of consequence, from which it is fit and woven together.") [Source 12].

In Greek, as reported by Eusebius, Chrysippus describes this as a "natural arrangement of the whole" (**φυσικὴν τινὰ σύνταξιν τῶν ὅλων**):
> "εἱμαρμένην εἶναι φυσικὴν τινὰ σύνταξιν τῶν ὅλων ἐξ ἀϊδίου τῶν ἑτέρων τοῖς ἑτέροις ἐπακολουθούντων" 
> ("Fate is a certain natural arrangement of the whole from eternity, with some things following upon others.") [Source 12].

### II. The Mechanism of Responsibility: Assent (συγκατάθεσις)
The Stoics argue that while fate determines the external circumstances (impressions) we encounter, our response to those circumstances—our **assent (συγκατάθεσις / synkatathesis)**—is "up to us" (**ἐφ' ἡμῖν / eph' hēmin**) [Source 2], [Source 17].

Chrysippus used the **Cylinder Analogy** to illustrate this distinction between external and internal causes [Source 11]. Just as a person gives a cylinder a push (the external, auxiliary cause), but the cylinder rolls according to its own nature and shape (the internal, principal cause), so too does an external impression strike the mind, but the mind assents according to its own character [Source 11], [Source 12].

Gellius explains that for Chrysippus:
> *"ingenia tamen ipsa mentium nostrarum proinde sunt fato obnoxia, ut proprietas eorum est ipsa et qualitas."* 
> ("nevertheless the dispositions of our minds are subject to fate in such a way that their own property and quality [determine the outcome].") [Source 12].

### III. Co-fated Events (συνειμαρμένα)
To counter the "Lazy Argument" (**ἀργὸς λόγος / argos logos**)—which claims that if everything is fated, one need not act—Chrysippus developed the doctrine of **co-fated events** [Source 1], [Source 5]. He argued that certain outcomes are fated *along with* the actions necessary to produce them [Source 17].

Eusebius records this distinction:
> "καθείμαρται γὰρ, φησὶ, σωθῆναι θοιμάτιον, εἰ φυλάττοις αὐτὸ, καὶ παῖδας ἔσεσθαι, εἰ καὶ σὺ βουληθείης" 
> ("For it is fated, he says, that the cloak be saved if you guard it, and that children will be born if you also should so will it.") [Source 24].

### IV. Ancient Critiques and Modern Interpretations
Ancient critics, most notably **Alexander of Aphrodisias**, rejected this compatibilism as a "misleading" substitution of terms [Source 10]. Alexander argued that if our character and our assent are themselves fated by prior causes, then we lack the "power of choosing and acting with respect to opposites" (**τῶν οὕτως γινομένων ἡμεῖς ἐσμεν κύριοι**) [Source 4], [Source 10].

Modern scholars like **R.W. Sharples** characterize the Stoic position as a form of **soft determinism** [Source 18]. This interpretation emphasizes that the Stoics did not see freedom as the absence of causes, but as the ability to act from one's own rational nature [Source 1]. 

In contrast, Middle Platonists attempted to preserve a stronger sense of freedom by distinguishing between **Providence (πρόνοια)** and **Fate (εἱμαρμένη)**, suggesting that while the laws of the universe are fated, individual choices remain contingent [Source 16]. Plutarch summarizes this "contrary account" as one where all things are not only "in fate" but "according to fate" (**οὐ μόνον ἐν εἱμαρμένῃ ἀλλὰ καὶ καθ' εἱμαρμένην**), a view the Stoics held but their opponents found destructive to moral life [Source 5], [Source 6].
