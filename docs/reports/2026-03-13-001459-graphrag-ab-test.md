## Arm A

Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–253 CE) is regarded as the most philosophically sophisticated Patristic defender of human freedom, developing the first systematic Christian "metaphysics of freedom" [Source 1], [Source 7]. His work, particularly *De Principiis* (Περὶ Ἀρχῶν) Book III, serves as a foundational defense of human agency against Stoic fate (εἱμαρμένη), Gnostic predestinarianism, and astrological determinism [Source 2], [Source 10].

### 1. The Definition of *Autexousion* (Αὐτεξούσιον)
Origen’s technical term for free will is **αὐτεξούσιον** (*autexousion*), which denotes "self-determining power" or being "master of oneself" [Source 1], [Source 17]. He distinguishes between external circumstances and the internal rational response.

In *De Principiis* III.1.3 (preserved in the *Philocalia*), Origen writes:
> "Τὰ μὲν ἔξωθεν ἡμῖν συμβαίνοντα οὐκ ἔστιν ἐφ' ἡμῖν· τὸ δὲ ὁρίσαι καὶ δοκιμάσαι πῶς τούτοις χρηστέον... οὐδενὸς ἄλλου ἔργον ἐστὶν ἢ τοῦ ἐν ἡμῖν λόγου, τουτέστι τῆς αὐτεξουσίου ἡμῶν κρίσεως." 
> (What happens to us from outside is not in our power [οὐκ ἔστιν ἐφ' ἡμῖν]; but to determine and judge how we should use these things... is the work of nothing else than the reason within us, that is, of our self-determining judgment [τῆς αὐτεξουσίου ἡμῶν κρίσεως].) [Source 8]

This definition draws on the Epictetan distinction between things "up to us" and things "not up to us," grounding freedom in the rational soul's capacity to judge and assent to impressions [Source 5], [Source 8].

### 2. Moral Responsibility and Divine Justice
For Origen, the existence of *autexousion* is a necessary condition for moral responsibility and divine justice. He argues that if human actions were necessitated by fate or nature, praise and blame would be meaningless [Source 1].

In *Against Celsus*, Origen contends that virtue must be voluntary:
> "...ἐὰν γὰρ ἀνέλῃς τὸ ἑκούσιον τῆς ἀρετῆς, ἀνεῖλες αὐτῆς καὶ τὴν οὐσίαν."
> (...for if you remove the voluntary character [τὸ ἑκούσιον] of virtue, you destroy its very essence [οὐσίαν].) [Source 4]

He further argues that God’s justice in rewarding or punishing individuals presupposes that they had the power to choose otherwise. Without this power, God would be the author of evil, a conclusion Origen rejects by asserting that evil results from the creaturely misuse of freedom [Source 6], [Source 10].

### 3. Reconciling Providence and Foreknowledge
A major challenge Origen addressed was how divine providence (πρόνοια) and foreknowledge (πρόγνωσις) could coexist with human freedom. He famously argued that **foreknowledge is not causality** [Source 9], [Source 19].

In *De Oratione* 6, he explains:
> "Ἡ πρόγνωσις τοῦ θεοῦ οὐκ ἔστιν αἰτία πάντων τῶν ἐσομένων καὶ τῶν ἀπ' αὐτεξουσίου κινήσεως ἡμῶν ἀποβησομένων."
> (God's prescience is not the cause of all future events or of those resulting from our self-determining motion.) [Source 9], [Source 11]

He clarifies the logical priority of the event over the knowledge of it:
> "...οὐχ ὅτι γινώσκει ὁ θεὸς τὸ ἐσόμενον, διὰ τοῦτο καὶ ἔσται· ἀλλ' ὅτι ἐσόμενόν ἐστι, διὰ τοῦτο γινώσκεται ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ πρὸ τοῦ γενέσθαι."
> (...it is not because God knows that it will be, that it will be; but because it will be, that God knows it beforehand.) [Source 9], [Source 11]

To illustrate this, Origen uses the analogy of an astronomer: just as an astronomer predicts an eclipse without causing it, God foreknows free human choices without necessitating them [Source 9], [Source 14].

### 4. Polemics Against Determinism
Origen’s defense of freedom was forged in opposition to three main deterministic systems:

* **Stoic Fate (εἱμαρμένη):** Origen utilized Carneadean anti-fatalist arguments to show that if fate governs all, then admonition, laws, and piety are useless [Source 1], [Source 10]. He specifically refuted the *argos logos* (lazy argument), which suggests that if an outcome is fated, effort is pointless [Source 14].
* **Gnostic Determinism:** He rejected the Valentinian doctrine that humans are "saved by nature" (the *pneumatikoi*) or "damned by nature" (the *hylikoi*). Origen insisted that all rational beings were created equal and that their current status (angelic, human, or demonic) is the result of prior free choices [Source 2], [Source 10].
* **Astrological Determinism:** Origen argued that stars are "signs" (σημεῖα) rather than "causes" (αἰτίαι). He famously used the "twin argument" (Jacob and Esau) to demonstrate that individuals born under the same celestial configuration can have vastly different moral characters and fates [Source 16].

### 5. Scholarly Synthesis
Modern scholarship, such as the work of C. Hengstermann, emphasizes that Origen created a "metaphysics of freedom" where reality is structured as a hierarchy of graded freedom [Source 7]. While Origen’s position is often described as "libertarian" because he insists on genuine alternatives and the soul as the true originator of choice, scholars like Frede and Bobzien note the complexity of applying modern labels to ancient voluntarism [Source 1], [Source 17]. Ultimately, Origen’s synthesis of Platonic philosophy and Christian scripture established the framework for the Greek Patristic consensus on *autexousion* and influenced the Latin development of *liberum arbitrium* [Source 1], [Source 17].

## Arm B

### Identity and Context
Origen of Alexandria was a Christian Platonist educated in Alexandria's philosophical milieu. His extensive corpus includes biblical commentaries, homilies, apologetics, and systematic theology. His work *De Principiis* is particularly significant for its sustained philosophical argument for free will, addressing scriptural passages that appear to negate it[2]. Origen defines free will using the Greek term *autexousion* (αὐτεξούσιον), meaning "self-determining power" or being "in one's own power"[1].

### Works and Doctrine
In *De Principiis* Book III, Origen provides a detailed argument for free will, countering deterministic views. He argues that all rational creatures were created equal with free will, and present inequalities result from prior free choices[10]. Origen's defense of free will was specifically directed against Gnostic predestinarianism, which categorized humans into fixed spiritual types[21]. He emphasized that evil exists because free beings can misuse their freedom, not because of a flawed demiurge[21].

### Textual Basis
> "Since the doctrine of God's just judgment is contained in the Church's proclamation — which, when believed to be true, calls those who hear it to live rightly and to flee sin by every means, with the evident assumption that what is worthy of praise and blame is within our power — let us, for our part, treat briefly and individually the question of free will, this being an absolutely indispensable problem"[P2].

### Providence and Moral Responsibility
Origen argued that divine prescience (foreknowledge) is not the cause of future events, thereby preserving human moral responsibility[29]. He used the analogy of an astronomer predicting an eclipse but not causing it to reconcile divine foreknowledge with human freedom[29]. Additionally, Origen utilized a "permissive theodicy" formula, suggesting that God's actions, such as hardening Pharaoh's heart, are the "occasion" rather than the "cause" of human sin[49].

### Eschatology and Apocatastasis
Origen's eschatology included the concept of *apocatastasis*, the eventual restoration of all rational beings to their original state through the exercise of will[107]. This view, which suggests a cosmic restoration at Christ's return, was later condemned in 553 CE but remains a significant part of Origen's theological framework[107].
