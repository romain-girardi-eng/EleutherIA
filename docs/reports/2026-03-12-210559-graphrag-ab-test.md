## Arm A

The Stoics conceptualized fate as Heimarmenê (Εἱμαρμένη) [4, 6] and Providentia (Providence) [1], maintaining that while the universe is governed by these forces, individuals retain moral responsibility through specific faculties. Central to this is the doctrine of 'tò eph' hēmin' (what is in our power) [2, 4]. Epictetus defined this through 'Prohairesis' (Προαίρεσις), or deliberate choice, which allows for moral agency within a fated system [2]. Marcus Aurelius similarly emphasized the 'Hegemonikon' (ἡγεμονικόν), or ruling faculty, as the seat of individual reason [3]. To reconcile universal fate with responsibility, Chrysippus of Soli introduced a distinction between 'Perfect' and 'Antecedent' causes [9, 10]. This framework supports the 'Willing Assent to Fate' (Praebere se Fato) argument, particularly in the Imperial tradition of Seneca, who viewed adversity as 'Exercitatio' (training) rather than mere misfortune [1]. The Stoic position is a key component of the ancient debate between compatibilism and incompatibilism [7, 8], with Cleanthes of Assos providing foundational arguments such as the 'Hymn to Zeus' to illustrate the alignment of the human will with divine fate [4].
